Question. What do we hear in a single note?

Answer. Four things: its pitch, loudness, timbre and duration. Each can change while the other three stay the same.

The four aspects

  • Pitch: how high or low the note sounds.1
  • Loudness: how strong it sounds.
  • Duration: how long it lasts.
  • Timbre (say “TAM-ber”): what still tells two notes apart when their pitch, loudness and duration are the same. It is why a flute and a violin playing the same note, equally loud and equally long, still sound different.2

1 The Acoustical Society of America defines pitch as the attribute by which sounds are ordered from low to high; it is measured as the frequency of the pure tone that listeners judge equally high. Standard terminology: pitch, 11.01; B. C. J. Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., Emerald, 2012, chapter 6.

2 Adapted from the ANSI/ASA definition of timbre (terminology 12.09), as quoted by Wikipedia, Timbre.

3 Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., 2012, chapters 4, 6 and 8 (loudness, pitch, timbre); R. Plomp, Aspects of Tone Sensation: A Psychophysical Study, Academic Press, 1976.

All four are perceptions, measured by asking listeners.3

Pitch is shown in hertz (Hz), vibrations per second; loudness in decibels (dB), where −10 dB is a tenth of the power. The four timbres have the same pitch and the same power.

Each control changes one thing in the sound and leaves the rest fixed. To the ear the four aspects are nearly, not perfectly, independent: at the same power a low note sounds a little quieter than a middle one,4 and the bright note a little louder than the pure one.5

Two questions

Try it: six instruments, each playing middle C (lowest frequency 262 Hz) at about the same strength.

Each sound is a model built from pure tones, as the next page explains. The flute also has the breath noise and the slow wobble in pitch (vibrato) that a real flute has; none has the sound of a bow or a stick.
