A plucked string moves as a sum of simple shapes: one arch, two arches, three arches, and so on, each swinging at its own frequency, exactly n times the lowest. This post shows why, for the simplest instrument there is, from Newton’s law to the sound you hear.

A plucked string, slowed down so that one swing of the lowest shape takes 4 seconds. Dashed: the exact motion. Solid: the sum of the first N shapes; slide N up and the sum closes in on the exact motion. Stems: how much of each shape the pluck contains. No friction in the picture; the sound fades only so that it stops.

The reason, in one sentence: measured along these shapes, the string’s equation falls apart into separate springs, one per shape, and these are the only shapes that do this. The rest of the post earns that sentence.

The problem

Pull a string aside at one point and let go. We want its height u(x, t) at every point x, from 0 to the bridge at L, at every time t \ge 0. Four facts pin it down: \begin{aligned} &u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx} &&\text{the equation of motion},\\ &u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0 &&\text{the ends are tied},\\ &u(x, 0) = u_0(x) &&\text{the starting shape, a triangle},\\ &u_t(x, 0) = 0 &&\text{let go at rest}. \end{aligned} Subscripts are partial derivatives: u_{tt} is how fast a point’s speed changes, its acceleration, and u_{xx} is how sharply the string curves there. The first line says that each bit of string accelerates in proportion to its curvature. The tied ends are a condition at the two edges of the string for all time, a boundary condition; the start is a condition at every point at one moment.

Where the equation comes from

Newton’s law on a short piece of string gives the first line.1

1 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2 (the vibrating string).

The string has tension T and mass \mu per unit length. The piece from x to x + dx is pulled along the string at both ends. For a small slope, the upward part of a pull T is T times the slope u_x, so the net upward force is T u_x(x + dx) - T u_x(x) \approx T u_{xx}\,dx. Mass times acceleration is \mu\,dx\,u_{tt}. Setting them equal, u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}, \qquad c = \sqrt{T/\mu} . On a hump u_{xx} < 0, so the hump is pulled down; in a dip, up. The model assumes a perfectly flexible string, small slopes and no friction (friction comes back below).

The whole idea with two beads

Before the string, take a version with only two moving points, where everything fits in a 2 \times 2 matrix.

Put two beads on a weightless string, at a third and two thirds of the way along, each carrying a third of the string’s mass, m = \mu L/3. Call their heights u_1 and u_2. Each bead is pulled toward its neighbours, the tied ends counting as neighbours at height zero, so with h = L/3 the spacing, \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix}'' = -K \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K = \frac{T}{mh}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . Each bead’s acceleration depends on the other bead’s height: in these coordinates the motion is coupled, and it looks like a tangle.

Now measure along two other directions: both beads together, q_1 = (u_1 + u_2)/\sqrt2, and the beads opposite, q_2 = (u_1 - u_2)/\sqrt2. Adding and subtracting the two equations gives q_1'' = -\frac{T}{mh}\,q_1, \qquad q_2'' = -\frac{3T}{mh}\,q_2 . Two separate springs. Each swings on its own, q_1 at one frequency and q_2 at \sqrt3 \approx 1.73 times it, and every motion of the beads is just the two swings added.

Slowed down. Right: the plane of the two heights, u₁ across and u₂ up. A mix traces a tangle; started along q₁ or q₂ alone, the motion runs straight along that tilted line. The sound puts the q₁ swing at 196 Hz.

Why did those two directions work? Because K only stretches them: K(1, 1) = \frac{T}{mh}(1, 1) and K(1, -1) = \frac{3T}{mh}(1, -1). They are the eigenvectors of K, and the stretch factors, its eigenvalues, are the springs’ stiffnesses. And they are exactly the string’s first two shapes, one arch and two arches, sampled at the beads.

The string is the same calculation with infinitely many beads.

PreciselyMore beads, and the limit matrices and limits

With N beads, spacing h = L/(N+1) and m = \mu h, the matrix is K = \frac{T}{mh}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1). Its eigenvectors are the sines \sin(n\pi x/L) sampled at the beads, with eigenvalues \frac{c^2}{h^2}\,4\sin^2\frac{n\pi h}{2L}, which tend to the string’s (n\pi c/L)^2 as N \to \infty. (The frequency ratios tend to 1 : 2 : 3 whatever the beads weigh; the frequencies themselves need m = \mu h.) Each row of the bead equation tends to u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}, by Taylor’s theorem.

The string: the same move

For the string, the column of two heights becomes the whole curve u(\cdot, t), and the matrix K becomes the rule A u = -c^2\,u_{xx}, \qquad \text{for shapes with } u(0) = u(L) = 0 , so the equation reads u_{tt} = -Au. The tied ends are part of A: it only acts on shapes that vanish at both ends.

The shapes that A only stretches. Solve AX = \lambda X, that is X'' = -(\lambda/c^2)X with X(0) = X(L) = 0. Solutions of X'' = -k^2X are a\cos kx + b\sin kx; the end x = 0 kills the cosine; the end x = L needs \sin kL = 0, so kL = n\pi. (No \lambda \le 0 works: the fold shows why.) So the eigenfunctions of A and their eigenvalues are X_n(x) = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad \lambda_n = \Big(\frac{n\pi c}{L}\Big)^2, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots Shape n has n arches.

Coordinates along them. Measure the string by how much of each shape it contains, q_n(t) = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L u(x, t)\,X_n(x)\,dx, the continuous version of q_1 = (u_1 + u_2)/\sqrt2. Then each coordinate is a spring: q_n'' = \tfrac{2}{L}\!\int_0^L u_{tt}X_n = \tfrac{2}{L}\!\int_0^L c^2u_{xx}X_n = \tfrac{2}{L}\!\int_0^L c^2u\,X_n'' = -\lambda_n\,q_n . The middle step moves both derivatives from u onto X_n by integrating by parts twice (for a pluck, whose corner has no u_{xx}, read this through the second fold below); the boundary terms vanish because both u and X_n are zero at the ends. The last step is X_n'' = -(\lambda_n/c^2)X_n. So, measured along the sines, the string is a row of independent springs, spring n swinging at f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{n c}{2L} = n f_1 .

Why only these shapes. Measure along any other set of perpendicular shapes that vanish at the ends and build every shape (an orthonormal basis \psi_1, \psi_2, \dots, such as the sines scaled by \sqrt{2/L}), and the same calculation gives q_i'' = -\sum_j M_{ij}\,q_j with M_{ij} = \int (A\psi_j)\psi_i: each coordinate feels the others, as the bead heights u_1 and u_2 did. The coordinates come apart exactly when M is diagonal, and that happens exactly when every shape in the family is an eigenfunction. Nothing about the form of the solution was assumed: choosing coordinates along eigenfunctions is the one choice that separates the springs.

Why these shapes are enough. Every starting shape is a sum of sines, u_0 = \sum a_n X_n; this is the Fourier sine series, and it is the one theorem this post quotes rather than proves.2

2 E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis: An Introduction, Princeton University Press, 2003, chapters 2–3 (Fejér’s theorem; mean-square convergence). A shape on [0, L] extended to an odd function of period 2L has a Fourier series of sines only.

PreciselyDecoupling happens exactly in an eigenbasis inner products, orthonormal bases

Write \langle u, w\rangle = \int_0^L u\,w\,dx. (The main text’s sines are orthogonal but have \int X_n^2 = L/2; normalised, \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L), the factor 2/L in q_n disappears.) For shapes u, w vanishing at the ends, two integrations by parts give \langle Au, w\rangle = c^2\!\int u'w' = \langle u, Aw\rangle: A is symmetric, like K. With w = u, \langle Au, u\rangle = c^2\!\int (u')^2 > 0 unless u' is identically zero, which with u(0) = 0 forces u = 0; so A is positive, and AX = \lambda X forces \lambda > 0. That is why no \lambda \le 0 appears above.

Proposition. Let \psi_1, \psi_2, \dots be an orthonormal basis of square-integrable shapes, each twice differentiable and zero at the ends, and let q_j = \langle u, \psi_j\rangle. Then q_i'' = -\sum_j M_{ij}q_j with M_{ij} = \langle A\psi_j, \psi_i\rangle, and M is diagonal if and only if every \psi_j is an eigenfunction of A.

Proof. For a smooth solution, q_i'' = \langle u_{tt}, \psi_i\rangle = -\langle Au, \psi_i\rangle = -\langle u, A\psi_i\rangle, by symmetry of A; for a pluck, q_i'' = -\langle u, A\psi_i\rangle is the definition of solution in the next fold, with \varphi = \psi_i. Expanding u = \sum_j q_j\psi_j gives q_i'' = -\sum_j q_j\langle\psi_j, A\psi_i\rangle = -\sum_j M_{ij}q_j. If A\psi_j = \mu_j\psi_j for every j, then M_{ij} = \mu_j\langle\psi_j, \psi_i\rangle is diagonal. Conversely, A\psi_j = \sum_i M_{ij}\psi_i (expansion in the basis), which is M_{jj}\psi_j when M is diagonal. \square

In a basis of polynomials vanishing at the ends (Gram–Schmidt on x(L - x)x^k), M has nonzero entries far from the diagonal; with L = 1 its top-left entry is 10c^2, just above \lambda_1 = \pi^2c^2 \approx 9.87c^2.

PreciselyWhat “solution” means for a pluck, and why there is exactly one integration by parts, Cauchy–Schwarz

The triangle has a corner, where u_{xx} does not exist, so the equation cannot hold there literally. The calculation of q_n'' above never needed u_{xx} once the derivatives were moved onto X_n, which suggests the definition. A solution is a shape u(t) with \int u^2 < \infty, changing continuously in mean square, such that for every smooth \varphi vanishing at the ends, \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\langle u, \varphi\rangle = -\langle u, A\varphi\rangle, with \langle u(0), \varphi\rangle = \langle u_0, \varphi\rangle and \frac{d}{dt}\langle u, \varphi\rangle(0) = \langle v_0, \varphi\rangle. Smooth solutions satisfy it (integrate by parts); testing against all such \varphi loses nothing, because they are dense in mean square. (L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., AMS, 2010, section 7.2)

Uniqueness. With \varphi = X_n, each q_n solves q_n'' = -\lambda_nq_n with given q_n(0), q_n'(0). That has exactly one solution: the difference r of two has r(0) = r'(0) = 0, and E = (r')^2 + \lambda_nr^2 has E' = 0, so r = 0. Two solutions with the same start therefore have the same coordinates, so they are equal (the sines are a basis).

Existence. The series \sum_n (a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt)X_n, with \omega_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}, a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int u_0X_n and b_n = \frac{2}{L}\int v_0X_n/\omega_n, is a solution when the start has finite energy, \int(u_0')^2 < \infty and \int v_0^2 < \infty. Integration by parts and Bessel’s inequality give \sum n^2a_n^2 < \infty, so Cauchy–Schwarz gives \sum |a_n| + |b_n| < \infty; the series and its term-by-term time derivatives against \varphi then converge uniformly, and the defining identity holds.

Counting. A mass on a spring has one eigenvalue and a two-dimensional space of motions (\cos and \sin); two beads have two eigenvalues and four; the string has two dimensions of motion for each eigenvalue.

The pluck

Put the pieces together. Each spring starts where the pluck puts it and at rest, so q_n(t) = a_n\cos(2\pi f_n t), and u(x, t) = \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} a_n \cos(2\pi f_n t)\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad a_n = \frac{2hL^2\,\sin(n\pi x_0/L)}{\pi^2 n^2\,x_0(L - x_0)} , for a triangle of height h with its apex at x_0. This is the motion in the demo at the top.

One thing jumps out: a_n = 0 when nx_0/L is a whole number. A shape with a still point exactly where you pluck is never started. Pluck a fifth of the way along and the 5th, 10th, 15th, … shapes are missing; slide the pluck point in the demo to 0.20 and watch those stems vanish.

DetailsThe coefficients, and the exact motion integration by parts

a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L u_0 X_n. The triangle is straight on both sides of x_0, where its slope jumps by -hL/(x_0(L - x_0)); integrating by parts twice turns the integral into that jump times X_n(x_0)\,(L/n\pi)^2, which is the formula above.

The dashed curve in the demo is the exact motion in d’Alembert’s form: extend u_0 to an odd function of period 2L, call it F; then u(x, t) = \frac12\big(F(x - ct) + F(x + ct)\big), two copies of the triangle running in opposite directions and flipping over at the ends. (J. le Rond d’Alembert, “Recherches sur la courbe que forme une corde tenduë mise en vibration”, Histoire de l’Académie royale des sciences et belles-lettres de Berlin 3, 1747, published 1749, 214–219) Its sine series is the series above, since \frac12[\sin(\theta - \phi) + \sin(\theta + \phi)] = \sin\theta\cos\phi. The corners travel, which is why a solution has to be defined without u_{xx}.

What you hear

You hear the string through the bridge, which it pushes at the frequencies f_n = n f_1: whole-number multiples of one frequency. So, in this frictionless model, the push repeats every 1/f_1 seconds (fading comes below), and you hear the pitch f_1 (post 1 explains why repeating means pitch).

The push on the bridge is the upward part of the tension at x = L, -T u_x(L, t). Its partials have sizes proportional to n a_n, that is, to \sin(n\pi x_0/L)/n. For a sharp pluck it is a rectangular wave, a short strong pull once per period and a weak pull the other way the rest of the time:

The push of the string on the bridge, plucked a fifth of the way along, over two periods (computed). Thick: the exact push. Thin: the sum of its first 12 partials, which overshoots next to each jump; adding more partials narrows the overshoot but never shrinks its height.

Three everyday facts follow from that one formula.

  • Pluck near the end and it sounds bright. Near the end, \sin(n\pi x_0/L)/n \approx \pi x_0/L for many n: lots of high partials, all about as strong. Pluck at the middle and the even partials vanish: a hollow sound.
  • A fingertip sounds mellower than a pick. A fingertip makes a rounded start with no corner; without the corner the coefficients fall off faster than 1/n^2, so the high partials are weaker.
  • Shorter, tighter or lighter strings sound higher. f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}: Mersenne’s laws of 1636.3

Fading, and the light touch

Real strings fade, and they lose their high partials first, which is why a guitar note turns mellower as it rings.4 Friction that acts the same way all along the string, like air drag, keeps the springs separate: each coordinate becomes a damped spring, q_n'' + \gamma q_n' + \lambda_n q_n = 0, and fades on its own at its own rate. Losses that grow with frequency, as in Chaigne and Askenfelt’s model of a piano string, are still split by the sines.5

4 J. Wolfe, Strings, standing waves and harmonics, UNSW: high-frequency components “disappear quickly”, so a guitar note becomes “more mellow”.

5 A. Chaigne and A. Askenfelt, “Numerical simulations of piano strings. I. A physical model for a struck string using finite difference methods”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 95 (1994), 1112–1118.

6 J. Wolfe, Strings, standing waves and harmonics, UNSW: touching the string lightly at a point 1/n of its length gives the harmonics.

Friction at one point is different, and it explains a trick every string player knows. Rest a finger lightly at the middle of the string and the note jumps up an octave.6 The finger is friction at one point: it drains every shape that moves under it, and it cannot touch the shapes that have a still point there. At the middle those are the even shapes, at 2f_1, 4f_1, 6f_1, \dots, all multiples of 2f_1: the octave. No frequency moved; the finger only chose which shapes keep ringing. Touch at a third and 3f_1, 6f_1, \dots survive, an octave and a fifth up. String players call these harmonics. Try it in the demo above.

PreciselyWhich frictions keep the springs apart matrices

A loss term \alpha u_t + \beta A u_t gives q_n'' + (\alpha + \beta\lambda_n)q_n' + \lambda_nq_n = 0: still one equation per shape, with higher shapes fading faster. A friction force -r\,u_t(s, t) at one point s gives instead q_n'' + \sum_m C_{nm}q_m' + \lambda_nq_n = 0 with C_{nm} \propto X_n(s)X_m(s), a rank-one matrix that couples the shapes. Shapes with X_n(s) = 0 have a zero row and column in C, so they ring on untouched; for a light touch, each other shape drains at a rate proportional to X_n(s)^2.

Reader questionWhy eigenfunctions in x, and not in t?

Space has two edges, where a condition holds for all time; time has one start. The operator A is built from the x-derivatives and the two edges, so its eigenfunctions are functions of x, and time is handled by one spring equation per shape with its two starting values. Nothing in the problem gives time a second edge. In space and time together, a product X(x)\cos(\omega t) solves the equation exactly when AX = \omega^2X; for X = \sin kx that is \omega^2 = c^2k^2, and the ends pick k = n\pi/L.

Reader questionIsn’t every function of (x, t) a sum of products f(x)g(t)?

Yes: products of a basis in x and a basis in t form a basis for functions of (x, t), solution or not. (M. Reed and B. Simon, Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics I: Functional Analysis, revised ed., Academic Press, 1980, section II.4) So “a sum of products” is not what makes the method work. What makes it work is that, in the eigenbasis, each coefficient q_n(t) obeys its own spring equation, so it is a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt, fixed by the start.
