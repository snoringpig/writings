A plucked string moves as a sum of simple shapes, one arch, two arches, three arches and so on, and the shape with n arches swings at exactly n times the lowest frequency.

A plucked string, slowed so that one swing of the lowest shape takes 4 seconds. Dashed: the exact motion. Solid: the sum of the first N shapes, which closes in on it as N grows. Stems: how much of each shape the pluck contains. The picture has no friction; the sound fades only so that it stops.

This post follows the sound back from your ear, through the soundboard (a large thin wooden panel) and the bridge on it, to the string.

What you hear

You hear the air pressure p(t) at your ear, a sum of fading partials c_n e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n) with frequencies f_n, amounts c_n, fading times \tau_n and phases \varphi_n. Pitch, loudness, timbre and duration are read off these numbers (post 1).

From force to sound

A string about 1 mm thick cannot move the air itself; the air simply flows around it.1 On a piano or guitar the bridge is a hardwood strip glued to the soundboard; on a violin it is a carved maple piece standing on the top plate, held there by the strings’ pressure.2 The bridge does move, just much less than the string: too little to change the string’s motion, which is why the string model below ties it down, but enough to drive the soundboard.

1 A. Askenfelt (ed.), Five Lectures on the Acoustics of the Piano, Royal Swedish Academy of Music, 1990, “Introduction”: “as the thin string cannot radiate a sound wave itself”.

2 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapters 9 (guitars), 10 (bowed string instruments) and 12 (the piano).

The soundboard and the room turn the force F(t) with which the string pushes the bridge into the pressure at your ear, by a fixed linear filter: p(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} h(t - s)\,F(s)\,ds , written p = h * F, a convolution. Here h is what a tap test records: on a silent instrument, tap the bridge once, sharply, and record the pressure at your ear, per unit of the tap’s strength (force times duration). During a note, the string’s push is a continuous run of such taps, each heard as a copy of h, delayed and scaled, and p is the sum of the copies. The filter is fixed and linear because small motions of the board and the air obey linear equations, nothing in them changes in time, and nothing can happen before its cause, which is why the integral stops at s = t.

Because the filter does not change in time, the pure tones e^{2\pi i f t} = \cos 2\pi f t + i\sin 2\pi f t (Euler’s formula) are its eigenfunctions. Substituting \tau = t - s, \begin{gathered} \int_{-\infty}^{t} h(t - s)\,e^{2\pi i f s}\,ds = H(f)\,e^{2\pi i f t},\\ H(f) = \int_0^{\infty} h(\tau)\,e^{-2\pi i f \tau}\,d\tau , \end{gathered} so the eigenvalue is H(f), the Fourier transform of h. Taking real parts, each tone in F (next section: tones at the string’s f_n) keeps its frequency in p, multiplied by the size |H(f_n)| of this complex number and shifted in time, once the board’s brief ring at the start has died away (fold).3

3 A. V. Oppenheim and A. S. Willsky, with S. H. Nawab, Signals and Systems, 2nd ed., Prentice Hall, 1997, sections 2.2 (the convolution integral) and 3.2 (the response of linear time-invariant systems to complex exponentials).

PreciselyThe exact shift, and the start of the note complex numbers, integrals

Since h is real, the real parts say that \cos 2\pi f t comes out as |H(f)|\cos\big(2\pi f t + \arg H(f)\big), where \arg H(f) is the angle of H(f).

F is a sum of tones only from t = 0; before, it is the steady pull F(0) of the held string. A steady push makes no sound, H(0) = \int_0^\infty h = 0, so p(t) = \int_0^t h(t - s)\big(F(s) - F(0)\big)\,ds. This differs from the sum of rescaled tones only by integrals of h over lags longer than t, which vanish once the tap’s own ring has died away. Until then the board also rings at its own frequencies; after that, p is exactly the sum of rescaled tones.

From string to force

The force on the bridge depends only on the string’s slope there: F(t) = -T\,u_x(L, t), where u(x, t) is the string’s height at point x and time t, x = L is the bridge end, T is the tension and u_x the slope (set up below).

The string’s motion is u = \sum_n a_n\cos(2\pi f_n t)\,X_n(x), with shapes X_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L) and amounts a_n set by the pluck (derived below), so F(t) = \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n+1}\,\frac{n\pi T}{L}\,a_n \cos 2\pi f_n t . The map from u to F acts at each instant, so it keeps every frequency and only weights shape n, by -T X_n'(L) = \pm Tn\pi/L. So you pluck once, but the string pushes the bridge up and down hundreds of times a second, for seconds (262 times a second for middle C); for a sharp pluck, F is a rectangular wave (the figure’s middle row).

PreciselyWhy F = -T u_x(L, t), and why the bridge carries every shape integrals

The string touches the bridge only at x = L, and there the two pull on each other along the string’s direction with tension T. By the rule derived below, the upward part of the bridge’s pull on the string is T u_x(L, t), so by Newton’s third law the string pulls the bridge with F(t) = -T u_x(L, t). A check on the sign: a string bulging up near the bridge has u_x(L, t) < 0, so F > 0, and it pulls the bridge up.

A check that nothing else acts: multiply the string’s equation (below) by its mass per unit length \mu, giving \mu u_{tt} = T u_{xx}, and integrate over the string. The string’s total upward momentum, P(t) = \int_0^L \mu\,u_t\,dx, then changes at the rate P'(t) = T u_x(L, t) - T u_x(0, t) , which is exactly the bridge’s pull plus the nut’s, -T u_x(0, t) by the same rule.

One point of the string, u(x_1, t), misses every shape with a still point at x_1: at the middle, every even shape. The bridge misses none, because the slope X_n'(L) = (-1)^n n\pi/L is never zero. So F contains every shape the pluck started.

The string

For a plucked string, this is the only step that creates the frequencies. The reason: measured along the shapes X_n, the string’s equation falls apart into separate springs, one per shape, and these are the only shapes that do this.

The problem

Pull a string aside at one point and let go: we want its height u(x, t) for all x and all t \ge 0. The string runs from the nut at x = 0, fixed to the heavy neck (on a piano, a bar on the iron frame), to the bridge at x = L, and the model ties both ends down. Four facts pin u down: \begin{aligned} &u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx} &&\text{Newton's law},\\ &u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0 &&\text{tied ends},\\ &u(x, 0) = u_0(x) &&\text{plucked shape},\\ &u_t(x, 0) = 0 &&\text{let go at rest}. \end{aligned} Subscripts are partial derivatives: u_{tt} is the acceleration of the point at x, and u_{xx} is how sharply the string curves there. So the first line says that each bit of string accelerates in proportion to its curvature, with a constant c^2 found in the next section. The tied ends are a boundary condition: they hold at the edges for all time, while the start holds everywhere at one moment.

Where the equation comes from

Newton’s law on a short piece of string gives the first line.4

4 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2 (the vibrating string).

The string has tension T and mass \mu per unit length. The piece from x to x + dx is pulled along the string at each end with force T, and for a small slope the upward part of that pull is T times the slope u_x. So the net upward force is T u_x(x + dx) - T u_x(x) \approx T u_{xx}\,dx, and it equals mass times acceleration, \mu\,dx\,u_{tt}: u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}, \qquad c = \sqrt{T/\mu} . The model assumes a perfectly flexible string, small slopes and no friction (friction comes back below).

The whole idea with two beads

With two beads in place of the string, the whole idea fits in a 2 \times 2 matrix.

Put two beads on a weightless string, at a third and two thirds of the way along, each carrying a third of the string’s mass, m = \mu L/3. Call their heights u_1 and u_2. Each bead is pulled toward its neighbours, the tied ends counting as neighbours at height zero, so with spacing \ell = L/3, \begin{gathered} \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix}'' = -K \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix},\\ K = \frac{T}{m\ell}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . \end{gathered} Each bead’s acceleration depends on the other’s height: the motion is coupled.

Now measure along two other directions: both beads together, q_1 = (u_1 + u_2)/\sqrt2, and the beads opposite, q_2 = (u_1 - u_2)/\sqrt2. Adding and subtracting the two equations gives q_1'' = -\frac{T}{m\ell}\,q_1, \qquad q_2'' = -\frac{3T}{m\ell}\,q_2 . Two separate springs, with frequencies in the ratio 1 : \sqrt3; every motion of the beads is the two swings added.

Slowed down. Right: the plane of the two heights, u₁ across and u₂ up. A mix traces a tangle; started along q₁ or q₂ alone, the motion runs straight along that tilted line. The sound puts the q₁ swing at 196 Hz.

These directions work because K only stretches them: K(1, 1) = \frac{T}{m\ell}(1, 1) and K(1, -1) = \frac{3T}{m\ell}(1, -1). They are the eigenvectors of K, and the stretch factors, its eigenvalues, are the springs’ stiffnesses. They are also the string’s first two shapes, one arch and two arches, sampled at the beads; the string is the same calculation with infinitely many beads.

PreciselyMore beads, and the limit matrices and limits

With N beads, spacing \ell = L/(N+1) and m = \mu\ell, the matrix is K = \frac{T}{m\ell}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1). Its eigenvectors are the sines \sin(n\pi x/L) sampled at the beads, with eigenvalues \frac{c^2}{\ell^2}\,4\sin^2\frac{n\pi\ell}{2L}, which tend to the string’s (n\pi c/L)^2 as N \to \infty. (The frequency ratios tend to 1 : 2 : 3 whatever the beads weigh; the frequencies themselves need m = \mu\ell.) Each row of the bead equation tends to u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}, by Taylor’s theorem.

The string: the same move

For the string, the column of two heights becomes the whole curve u(\cdot, t), and the matrix K becomes the operator \begin{gathered} A u = -c^2\,u_{xx},\\ \text{for shapes with } u(0) = u(L) = 0 , \end{gathered} so the equation reads u_{tt} = -Au, and the tied ends are part of A.

The shapes that A only stretches. Solve AX = \lambda X, that is X'' = -k^2X with k^2 = \lambda/c^2 and X(0) = X(L) = 0. The solutions are C_1\cos kx + C_2\sin kx; the end x = 0 kills the cosine, and the end x = L needs \sin kL = 0, so kL = n\pi. (No \lambda \le 0 works; the first fold below shows why.) So the eigenfunctions of A and their eigenvalues are \begin{gathered} X_n(x) = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad \lambda_n = \Big(\frac{n\pi c}{L}\Big)^2,\\ n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \end{gathered} Shape n has n arches.

Coordinates along them. Measure the string by how much of each shape it contains, q_n(t) = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L u(x, t)\,X_n(x)\,dx, the continuous version of q_1 = (u_1 + u_2)/\sqrt2. Then each coordinate is a spring: \begin{aligned} q_n'' &= \tfrac{2}{L}\!\int_0^L u_{tt}X_n = \tfrac{2}{L}\!\int_0^L c^2u_{xx}X_n\\ &= \tfrac{2}{L}\!\int_0^L c^2u\,X_n'' = -\lambda_n\,q_n . \end{aligned} The middle step integrates by parts twice, moving both derivatives onto X_n; the boundary terms vanish because u and X_n are zero at the ends. (A pluck’s corner has no u_{xx}; the second fold below handles it.) The last step is X_n'' = -(\lambda_n/c^2)X_n. So spring n swings at f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{n c}{2L} = n f_1 .

Why only these shapes. Take any other family of perpendicular shapes \psi_1, \psi_2, \dots that vanish at the ends and build every shape (an orthonormal basis), with coordinates q_i = \int u\,\psi_i. The same calculation gives q_i'' = -\sum_j M_{ij}\,q_j with M_{ij} = \int (A\psi_j)\psi_i: each coordinate feels the others, as u_1 and u_2 did. The coordinates come apart exactly when M is diagonal, which happens exactly when every \psi_j is an eigenfunction. Nothing about the form of the solution was assumed.

Why these shapes are enough. Every starting shape is a sum of sines, u_0 = \sum a_n X_n: the Fourier sine series, the one theorem this post quotes rather than proves.5

5 E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis: An Introduction, Princeton University Press, 2003, chapters 2–3 (Fejér’s theorem; mean-square convergence). A shape on [0, L] extended to an odd function of period 2L has a Fourier series of sines only.

PreciselyDecoupling happens exactly in an eigenbasis inner products, orthonormal bases

Write \langle u, w\rangle = \int_0^L u\,w\,dx. (The main text’s sines are orthogonal but have \int X_n^2 = L/2; normalised, \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L), the factor 2/L in q_n disappears.) For shapes u, w vanishing at the ends, two integrations by parts give \langle Au, w\rangle = c^2\!\int u'w' = \langle u, Aw\rangle: A is symmetric, like K. With w = u, \langle Au, u\rangle = c^2\!\int (u')^2 > 0 unless u' is identically zero, which with u(0) = 0 forces u = 0; so A is positive, and AX = \lambda X forces \lambda > 0. That is why no \lambda \le 0 appears above.

Proposition. Let \psi_1, \psi_2, \dots be an orthonormal basis of square-integrable shapes, each twice differentiable and zero at the ends, and let q_j = \langle u, \psi_j\rangle. Then q_i'' = -\sum_j M_{ij}q_j with M_{ij} = \langle A\psi_j, \psi_i\rangle, and M is diagonal if and only if every \psi_j is an eigenfunction of A.

Proof. For a smooth solution, q_i'' = \langle u_{tt}, \psi_i\rangle = -\langle Au, \psi_i\rangle = -\langle u, A\psi_i\rangle, by symmetry of A; for a pluck, q_i'' = -\langle u, A\psi_i\rangle is the definition of solution in the next fold, with \varphi = \psi_i. Expanding u = \sum_j q_j\psi_j gives q_i'' = -\sum_j q_j\langle\psi_j, A\psi_i\rangle = -\sum_j M_{ij}q_j. If A\psi_j = \kappa_j\psi_j for every j, then M_{ij} = \kappa_j\langle\psi_j, \psi_i\rangle is diagonal. Conversely, A\psi_j = \sum_i M_{ij}\psi_i (expansion in the basis), which is M_{jj}\psi_j when M is diagonal. \square

In a basis of polynomials vanishing at the ends (Gram–Schmidt on x(L - x)x^k), M has nonzero entries far from the diagonal; with L = 1 its top-left entry is 10c^2, just above \lambda_1 = \pi^2c^2 \approx 9.87c^2.

PreciselyWhat “solution” means for a pluck, and why there is exactly one integration by parts, Cauchy–Schwarz

The triangle has a corner, where u_{xx} does not exist, so the equation cannot hold there literally. The calculation of q_n'' above never needed u_{xx} once the derivatives were moved onto X_n, which suggests the definition. A solution is a shape u(t) with \int u^2 < \infty, changing continuously in mean square, such that for every smooth \varphi vanishing at the ends, \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\langle u, \varphi\rangle = -\langle u, A\varphi\rangle, with \langle u(0), \varphi\rangle = \langle u_0, \varphi\rangle and \frac{d}{dt}\langle u, \varphi\rangle(0) = \langle v_0, \varphi\rangle, where v_0 is the starting velocity (zero for a pluck). Smooth solutions satisfy it (integrate by parts); testing against all such \varphi loses nothing, because they are dense in mean square. (L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., AMS, 2010, section 7.2)

Uniqueness. With \varphi = X_n, each q_n solves q_n'' = -\lambda_nq_n with given q_n(0), q_n'(0). That has exactly one solution: the difference r of two has r(0) = r'(0) = 0, and E = (r')^2 + \lambda_nr^2 has E' = 0, so r = 0. Two solutions with the same start therefore have the same coordinates, so they are equal (the sines are a basis).

Existence. The series \sum_n (a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt)X_n, with \omega_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}, a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int u_0X_n and b_n = \frac{2}{L}\int v_0X_n/\omega_n, is a solution when the start has finite energy, \int(u_0')^2 < \infty and \int v_0^2 < \infty. Integration by parts and Bessel’s inequality give \sum n^2a_n^2 < \infty, so Cauchy–Schwarz gives \sum |a_n| + |b_n| < \infty; the series and its term-by-term time derivatives against \varphi then converge uniformly, and the defining identity holds.

Counting. A mass on a spring has one eigenvalue and a two-dimensional space of motions (\cos and \sin); two beads have two eigenvalues and four; the string has two dimensions of motion for each eigenvalue.

The pluck

Each spring starts where the pluck puts it, at rest, so q_n(t) = a_n\cos(2\pi f_n t) and \begin{gathered} u(x, t) = \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} a_n \cos(2\pi f_n t)\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\\ a_n = \frac{2u_0(x_0)\,L^2\,\sin(n\pi x_0/L)}{\pi^2 n^2\,x_0(L - x_0)} , \end{gathered} for a triangle with its apex above x_0, at height u_0(x_0). This is the motion in the demo at the top.

a_n = 0 when nx_0/L is a whole number: a shape with a still point exactly where you pluck is never started. Pluck a fifth of the way along and the 5th, 10th, 15th, … shapes are missing.

DetailsThe coefficients, and the exact motion integration by parts

a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L u_0 X_n. The triangle is straight on both sides of x_0, where its slope jumps by -u_0(x_0)L/(x_0(L - x_0)); integrating by parts twice turns the integral into that jump times X_n(x_0)\,(L/n\pi)^2, which is the formula above.

The dashed curve in the demo is the exact motion in d’Alembert’s form: extend u_0 to an odd function \tilde u_0 of period 2L; then u(x, t) = \frac12\big(\tilde u_0(x - ct) + \tilde u_0(x + ct)\big), two copies of the triangle running in opposite directions and flipping over at the ends. (J. le Rond d’Alembert, “Recherches sur la courbe que forme une corde tenduë mise en vibration”, Histoire de l’Académie royale des sciences et belles-lettres de Berlin 3, 1747, published 1749, 214–219) Its sine series is the series above, since \frac12[\sin(\theta - \phi) + \sin(\theta + \phi)] = \sin\theta\cos\phi. The corners travel, which is why a solution has to be defined without u_{xx}.

The chain in one line

In one line, the amount of partial n at your ear is c_n = |H(f_n)| \cdot \frac{Tn\pi}{L} \cdot |a_n| , the soundboard’s factor times the bridge’s weight times what the pluck gave shape n. The four aspects trace back:

  • pitch to the spectrum of A: f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi = nf_1;
  • timbre to the three factors and the spacing of the spectrum;
  • loudness to how far you pull the string, u_0(x_0);
  • duration to the losses (next section).

Three everyday facts follow: two from n|a_n| \propto |\sin(n\pi x_0/L)|/n, since |H(f_n)| is fixed for a given instrument, and one from f_1.

  • Pluck near an end and it sounds bright. For small x_0, \sin(n\pi x_0/L)/n \approx \pi x_0/L for many n: many high partials, all about equally strong. Pluck at the middle and the even partials vanish: a hollow sound.
  • A fingertip sounds mellower than a pick. A fingertip leaves a rounded start with no corner, so the a_n fall off faster than 1/n^2 and the high partials are weaker.
  • Shorter, tighter or lighter strings sound higher. f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}: Mersenne’s laws of 1636.6

Fading, and the light touch

Real strings fade, high partials first, which is why a guitar note turns mellower as it rings.7 Friction that acts the same way all along the string, like air drag, keeps the springs separate: each coordinate becomes a damped spring, q_n'' + \gamma q_n' + \lambda_n q_n = 0 with friction rate \gamma. So do losses that grow with frequency, as in Chaigne and Askenfelt’s model of a piano string.8

7 J. Wolfe, Strings, standing waves and harmonics, UNSW: high-frequency components “disappear quickly”, so a guitar note becomes “more mellow”.

8 A. Chaigne and A. Askenfelt, “Numerical simulations of piano strings. I. A physical model for a struck string using finite difference methods”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 95 (1994), 1112–1118.

9 J. Wolfe, Strings, standing waves and harmonics, UNSW: touching the string lightly at a point 1/n of its length gives the harmonics.

Friction at one point is different, and it explains a trick every string player knows: rest a finger lightly at the middle of the string and the note jumps up an octave.9 The finger drains every shape that moves under it and cannot touch the shapes with a still point there. At the middle those are the even shapes, at 2f_1, 4f_1, 6f_1, \dots, all multiples of 2f_1: the octave. No frequency moved; the finger only chose which shapes keep ringing. String players call these harmonics; try them in the demo above.

PreciselyWhich frictions keep the springs apart matrices

A loss term \alpha u_t + \beta A u_t, with constants \alpha, \beta \ge 0, gives q_n'' + (\alpha + \beta\lambda_n)q_n' + \lambda_nq_n = 0: still one equation per shape, with higher shapes fading faster. A friction force -r\,u_t(s, t) at one point s gives instead q_n'' + \sum_m C_{nm}q_m' + \lambda_nq_n = 0 with C_{nm} \propto X_n(s)X_m(s), a rank-one matrix that couples the shapes. Shapes with X_n(s) = 0 have a zero row and column in C, so they ring on untouched; for a light touch, each other shape drains at a rate proportional to X_n(s)^2.

Reader questionWhy eigenfunctions in x, and not in t?

Space has two edges, where a condition holds for all time; time has one start. The operator A is built from the x-derivatives and the two edges, so its eigenfunctions are functions of x, and time is handled by one spring equation per shape with its two starting values. Nothing in the problem gives time a second edge. In space and time together, a product X(x)\cos(\omega t) solves the equation exactly when AX = \omega^2X; for X = \sin kx that is \omega^2 = c^2k^2, and the ends pick k = n\pi/L.

Reader questionIsn’t every function of (x, t) a sum of products f(x)g(t)?

Yes: products of a basis in x and a basis in t form a basis for functions of (x, t), solution or not. (M. Reed and B. Simon, Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics I: Functional Analysis, revised ed., Academic Press, 1980, section II.4) So “a sum of products” is not what makes the method work. What makes it work is that, in the eigenbasis, each coefficient q_n(t) obeys its own spring equation, so it is a_n\cos 2\pi f_nt + b_n\sin 2\pi f_nt, fixed by the start.
