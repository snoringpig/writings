Piano tuners don’t tune octaves to an exact frequency ratio of 2 : 1. They stretch them slightly, so the top of a piano ends up sharp and the bottom flat, compared with equal temperament, the tuning in which all twelve semitones are the same ratio.1 An octave tuned to exactly 2 : 1 on a piano wobbles. Why would exact sound wrong?

1 Wikipedia, Piano acoustics: “octaves are normally stretched on a well-tuned piano. That is, the high notes are tuned higher, and the low notes tuned lower, than they are in a mathematically idealized equal-tempered scale.”

Exact sounds wrong because a piano wire’s second partial sits a little above twice its fundamental, so an exact 2 : 1 octave puts two nearly equal frequencies side by side, and they beat: a slow wobble in loudness. Listen: an exact octave, then a stretched one.

6.0

Until you tick "Play every partial", each note plays only its lowest partials (C4: 1 to 3, C5: 1 and 2), so the one beat a tuner listens for stands out and the stretched octave is steady. Stiffness starts at 6 times a real middle C wire's, so the wobble is quick enough to hear; at 1 it is a slow swell every few seconds.

The reason, in one sentence: a piano wire is a string and a bar at once, and the bar’s stiffness pushes every partial sharp of its whole-number line, the more so the higher the partial.

Where the equation comes from

Two forces act on each short piece of a piano wire, and they add. Tension pulls it toward the inside of its curve, the net force T\,u_{xx} per unit length of the string in post 2. Stiff steel resists bending, the force -EI\,u_{xxxx} per unit length of the kalimba’s tine. Newton’s law for a wire of mass \mu per unit length takes their sum:2 \mu\,u_{tt} = T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx} . Here T is the tension, E steel’s Young’s modulus, and I = \pi d^4/64 the second moment of area of a round wire of diameter d. At the bridge and at the far bearing point the wire is held in place, u = 0, and if it can pivot freely there, no bending moment acts, u_{xx} = 0: the ends are pinned. So u_{tt} = -Au with \begin{gathered} A = -c^2\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + \kappa^2\frac{d^4}{dx^4}, \\ c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\mu}, \end{gathered} the string’s operator plus the bar’s.

2 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 12 (the piano): the stiff string and its inharmonicity.

DetailsThe piece of wire, step by step partial derivatives

Take the piece of wire between x and x + dx, of mass \mu\,dx. Tension pulls along the wire at both ends; for small slopes the upward part of each pull is T times the slope, so the net upward force is T u_x(x + dx) - T u_x(x) \approx T\,u_{xx}\,dx, as for post 2’s string. Bending adds the bar’s force: a bent wire carries the bending moment M = EI\,u_{xx}; a thin slice cannot spin, so its turning effects balance and the sideways shear force is V = -M_x = -EI\,u_{xxx}; and the shear forces on the two faces differ by V_x\,dx = -EI\,u_{xxxx}\,dx (the kalimba’s fold has the steps). Newton’s law takes the total force, so the two add: \mu\,u_{tt} = T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx}. Small slopes also keep the tension at T.

DetailsWhat the model leaves out

The model assumes a plain, uniform steel wire, pinned ends, small slopes and no friction; the fading comes back below. Real piano strings are held a little more firmly than pinned, which shifts the numbers slightly but not the picture. Bass strings are wound with copper, which adds mass but little stiffness (N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 12).

The wire’s shapes

The string’s sines still work. Each X_n = \sin(n\pi x/L) is zero at both ends, and so is X_n'', so the pinned ends hold; two derivatives multiply it by -k^2 and four by k^4, with k = n\pi/L. So both terms of A only stretch it, and their stretch factors add: \begin{gathered} X_n(x) = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \\ \lambda_n = c^2k^2 + \kappa^2k^4 . \end{gathered} Taking f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi and factoring out the string’s part gives \begin{gathered} f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, \\ f_0 = \frac{c}{2L}, \qquad B = \frac{\pi^2EI}{TL^2} . \end{gathered} Here f_0 is the lowest frequency the wire would have without stiffness. The stiffness constant B is small, but it multiplies n^2: the fundamental barely moves, while high partials run sharp. The wire is slightly inharmonic.3

3 Wikipedia, Inharmonicity, on the partials of stiff strings.

For a middle C wire B is roughly 4.4\times10^{-4}, which puts the 10th partial about 37 cents, a third of a semitone, sharp of ten times the fundamental. Since I grows like the fourth power of the diameter and B falls like 1/L^2, short strings that are thick for their length are the stiffest.

ProofThere are no other modes inner products

The sines already form a basis of shapes on [0, L] (post 2’s Fourier sine series). With pinned ends, integrating by parts shows that A is symmetric, \int (Au)\,w = \int u\,(Aw), so eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues are perpendicular. An eigenfunction for any eigenvalue other than the \lambda_n would be perpendicular to every sine, hence zero.

DetailsHow big is B? A middle C wire logarithms

Take roughly d = 1 mm and L = 0.6 m, with steel’s E = 2\times10^{11} Pa and density 7850 kg/m³; a tension of about 610 N then gives middle C. So I \approx 4.9\times10^{-14} m^4 and B \approx 4.4\times10^{-4}. The 10th partial, at 10f_0\sqrt{1 + 100B}, sits 1200\log_2\sqrt{(1 + 100B)/(1 + B)} \approx 37 cents sharp of ten times the fundamental f_1 = f_0\sqrt{1 + B}. These are rough illustrative values; real strings vary.

Why exact octaves beat

A tuner sets an octave by ear, listening for beats between the lower note’s second partial and the upper note’s fundamental; two nearly equal frequencies played together beat at a rate equal to their difference. The spectrum says where that second partial sits: \frac{f_2}{f_1} = 2\sqrt{\frac{1 + 4B}{1 + B}} \approx 2\Big(1 + \frac{3B}{2}\Big) . It is more than twice the fundamental. An upper note tuned to exactly twice the fundamental sits just below it, and the two beat; raising the upper note slows the beat, so the beat-free octave is slightly wider than 2 : 1. Tick “Play every partial” in the demo at the top and the stretched octave wobbles too: no octave silences every beat.

Reader questionWhy can’t a tuner silence every beat?

Tuners also listen to other pairs, such as the lower note’s fourth partial against the upper note’s second, and the upper note’s own partials run sharp too, by different amounts. Widening the octave until one pair stops beating leaves the others beating slowly, so real tuning is a compromise among them.

The stretch is largest at the ends of the keyboard. Repeated octave by octave, the small widenings add up away from the middle, and the most inharmonic strings sit at both ends: the short treble strings, and the bass strings, which are shorter than their pitch would call for.4 O. L. Railsback measured this stretch on pianos tuned by ear in the 1930s. The plot of how far each note sits from equal temperament is the Railsback curve: close to equal temperament in the middle, flat in the bass, sharp in the treble.5

4 Wikipedia, Piano acoustics: “As the tuning progresses up and down the scale, the inharmonicity, hence the stretch, accumulates”; “the bass strings on the piano are shorter than the ‘double with each octave’ rule would predict”.

5 O. L. Railsback, “Scale temperament as applied to piano tuning”, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 9 (1938), 274, doi:10.1121/1.1902056.

Struck: how the modes start and stop

Treat the hammer’s blow as a quick kick: the wire starts straight but moving. It then moves as a sum of its shapes, each fading at its own rate: u = \sum_n a_n e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_n t)\,X_n(x) . The demos are built this way, with illustrative fading times.

The hammer sets the amounts. A blow starts each mode in proportion to the mode’s own height where the hammer hits, so a mode with a still point there stays silent. In the demos the hammer strikes an eighth of the way along, where the 8th and 16th modes stand still. How hard the key is struck matters too, and not only for loudness: the hammer’s felt acts like a soft spring under a gentle blow and a hard one under a heavy blow, so a heavy blow starts many more high partials, and loud notes sound brighter (post 1).6

6 D. A. Russell, Hammer nonlinearity, dynamics and the piano sound, 1997: a gentle blow “acts as a soft spring”, a heavy one “acts as a hard spring”; “Loud notes sound brighter than soft notes.” See also A. Askenfelt and E. Jansson, “The nonlinear hammer”, in Five Lectures on the Acoustics of the Piano, Royal Swedish Academy of Music, 1990.

7 G. Weinreich, “Two or more strings”, from The coupled motion of piano strings, in Five Lectures on the Acoustics of the Piano, Royal Swedish Academy of Music, 1990: in the out-of-phase motion “the decay rate of the vibration will be very slow”, so the vibration can “decompose into a prompt sound and an aftersound”.

Two strings, two stages. Most notes have two or three strings, coupled through the bridge. Moving in step, they shake the bridge hard and lose energy fast; moving opposite, they leave it nearly still and ring on. So a note starts loud and fades fast, then leaves a quiet, long aftersound.7 The demos leave this coupling out.

The player’s damping. Letting go of a key drops a felt damper on its strings, which drains each mode in proportion to how much it moves under the felt. The pedals act on the dampers or on the hammer’s blow.8 So every control a pianist touches changes how strongly the modes start or how fast they die; only the tuner’s wrench, which changes the tension, moves the frequencies.

8 Wikipedia, Piano pedals.

On a grand piano the middle pedal is usually the sostenuto, which holds up only the dampers of keys already down (Wikipedia, Piano pedals and Soft pedal).

PreciselyHow fast a damper drains each mode

For weak damping each mode fades at a rate set by how much it moves under the felt, \gamma_n \propto \int_{\text{felt}} X_n^2\,dx \big/ \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx, so a mode with a still point under the felt barely notices the damper.

Reader questionWhat would a piano without dampers sound like?

Releasing a key would do nothing: every string would ring until it died on its own, as if the sustain pedal were always down. The keyboard above leaves its top 20 keys without dampers, as real pianos leave their highest notes undamped: those short strings die out quickly by themselves (Kawai, Why do the upper notes of my digital piano sustain without the damper pedal?: the uppermost strings of an acoustic piano have no dampers).
