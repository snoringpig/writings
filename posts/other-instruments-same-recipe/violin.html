Touch a vibrating string lightly at its exact middle, without pressing it down, and the note jumps up an octave. Touch it a third of the way along and it jumps to three times the frequency. How can a feather-light finger, which does not shorten the string, change the note so much?

The finger moves none of the string’s frequencies. It silences every shape of the string that moves under it, and the shapes it spares, those standing still at the middle, are exactly the shapes of a note an octave up. Pluck the string, then pick a point to touch and pluck again; string players call these touched notes harmonics.1

1 J. Wolfe, Strings, standing waves and harmonics, UNSW: touching the string lightly at a point 1/n of its length gives the harmonics.

The reason, in one sentence: a light touch is a damper at one point, and a shape that stands still there gives the damper nothing to grab. Below, that sentence is built from Newton’s law; then the bow keeps the same string going.

The string’s shapes

The violin string is post 2’s string. Let it run from the nut, near the pegs, at x = 0, to the bridge at x = L, with tension T and mass \mu per unit length. Newton’s law on a short piece of it gives the wave equation, derived in post 2:2 \begin{gathered} u_{tt} = -Au, \qquad Au = -c^2u_{xx}, \\ c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}, \end{gathered} for shapes with u(0) = u(L) = 0. In words: each bit of string accelerates in proportion to how sharply it bends, and the tied ends decide which shapes are allowed.

2 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2 (the vibrating string).

DetailsWhere the equation comes from partial derivatives

Take the piece of string from x to x + dx, of mass \mu\,dx. The tension T pulls along the string at both ends; for a small slope, the sideways part of each pull is T times the slope u_x. The net sideways force is therefore T\,u_x(x + dx) - T\,u_x(x) \approx T\,u_{xx}\,dx, and Newton’s law sets it equal to mass times acceleration, \mu\,dx\,u_{tt}. Dividing by \mu\,dx gives u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx} with c^2 = T/\mu. The nut and the bridge hold the ends still, u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0. The model assumes a perfectly flexible string, small slopes, no air and no friction; post 2 draws the forces.

The shapes that the operator A only stretches, its eigenfunctions, and their eigenvalues, are \begin{gathered} X_n(x) = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad \lambda_n = \Big(\frac{n\pi c}{L}\Big)^2, \\ f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = n f_1, \qquad f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} . \end{gathered} Shape n has n arches and swings at exactly n times the lowest frequency, so every partial of a free string sits on a whole-number line of the pitch ruler.

PreciselyThe shapes, step by step, and why there are no others derivatives, integration by parts

Solve AX = \lambda X, that is -c^2X'' = \lambda X with X(0) = X(L) = 0. Writing \lambda = c^2k^2 with k > 0, the solutions of X'' = -k^2X are a\cos kx + b\sin kx. The nut, X(0) = 0, forces a = 0; the bridge, X(L) = 0, then needs \sin kL = 0, so k = n\pi/L for n = 1, 2, 3, \dots This gives \lambda_n = (n\pi c/L)^2 and f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi = nc/2L.

No zero or negative \lambda occurs. Multiply -c^2X'' = \lambda X by X and integrate by parts; the boundary term vanishes because X = 0 at both ends, which leaves \lambda\int_0^L X^2\,dx = c^2\int_0^L (X')^2\,dx. A \lambda \le 0 would force X' = 0, so X would be a constant vanishing at the ends, that is X = 0. Each \lambda_n has multiplicity 1, since the nut removed the cosine. Post 2 shows that measured along these shapes the string falls apart into independent springs, and that every starting shape is a sum of them.

Released in any shape, the string moves as a sum of these, each shape fading at its own rate 1/\tau_n: u = \sum_n a_n e^{-t/\tau_n} X_n(x)\cos(2\pi f_n t) . The pluck sets the amounts a_n. The fading rates are measured, not derived, and the high partials fade first;3 the demo’s rates are illustrative.

3 J. Wolfe, Strings, standing waves and harmonics, UNSW: high-frequency components “disappear quickly”, so a plucked note becomes “more mellow”.

Where you pluck sets the brightness

A string pulled aside a height h at the point x_0 and let go at rest starts shape n with the amount a_n = \frac{2hL^2}{\pi^2 x_0(L - x_0)}\cdot\frac{\sin(n\pi x_0/L)}{n^2}, post 2’s pluck formula. You hear the string through its push on the bridge, which is proportional to the string’s slope there, so partial n reaches the bridge in proportion to n a_n, that is, to \sin(n\pi x_0/L)/n.

Pluck near the bridge and \sin(n\pi x_0/L) \approx n\pi x_0/L for the first several shapes, so they push the bridge about equally hard: a bright sound. Pluck at the middle and the even shapes are missing, since they stand still where you pulled, and the rest fall off like 1/n: a mellow, hollow sound. Move the pluck slider in the demo and watch.

The open string, plucked 0.16 of its length from the bridge: mode n at n times the lowest frequency, exactly on line n, drawn as far as it swings just after the pluck.

DetailsWhere the pluck formula comes from integration by parts

The coefficient is a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L u_0\,X_n\,dx, since the sines are perpendicular: \int_0^L X_mX_n\,dx = 0 for m \ne n. The plucked triangle u_0 is straight on each side of x_0, so integrating by parts twice on each piece leaves only the jump in slope at the corner, from h/x_0 to -h/(L - x_0), times X_n(x_0)(L/n\pi)^2. That is the formula above, and the 1/n^2 comes from the single corner. Measuring x_0 from the bridge instead, as the demo’s slider does, only flips the sign of every second a_n, because \sin(n\pi(L - x_0)/L) = \pm\sin(n\pi x_0/L); signs do not change how loud a shape is.

Pressing down makes a new end

Pressing a finger firmly onto the fingerboard ties the string down there, which changes the operator itself: only the piece between finger and bridge sounds, and by the formula for f_1 a shorter string sounds higher, in inverse proportion to its length. Two thirds of the length sounds a fifth up, half the length an octave up, and the semitone positions bunch together toward the bridge. Play a whole violin: press and hold a string to bow it, slide sideways to finger it, or switch to plucking.

DetailsWhere the finger goes for each semitone powers

An open string is one not pressed down by a finger. With the finger at distance d from the nut, the vibrating length shrinks to L - d, so the pitch rises by the factor L/(L - d). In equal temperament the twelve semitones of an octave are equal frequency ratios, each 2^{1/12}, so k semitones are 2^{k/12}. Solving L/(L - d) = 2^{k/12} gives d_k = (1 - 2^{-k/12})L: the positions bunch together toward the bridge. These are the ticks on the violin’s fingerboard above.

The bow: one repeating cycle

A bowed string does not fade: the bow keeps feeding it, and its motion settles into one repeating cycle. The bow grips the string, drags it along until the string’s pull tears it free, lets it slip back, and grips it again, once per cycle. By 1860 Hermann von Helmholtz had watched the result through a vibration microscope:4 two straight pieces joined by one sharp corner, which races between bridge and finger and back, Helmholtz motion. The corner runs at the wave speed c, so the round trip of 2L takes 2L/c = 1/f_1 seconds: the cycle repeats at the string’s lowest frequency. As a sum of the string’s shapes, u(x, t) \propto \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{1}{n^2}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\sin(2\pi n f_1 t) . Every partial sits exactly on a line, because the motion repeats exactly (post 1), and partial n pushes the bridge in proportion to 1/n.

A vibration microscope’s lens rides on a vibrating tuning fork, so that a bright speck on the string traces a pattern revealing its motion. Helmholtz reported what he saw in 1860 (H. Helmholtz, “On the motion of the strings of a violin”, Proceedings of the Philosophical Society of Glasgow 5 (1860–64), 17–21, read 19 December 1860, archive.org) and in his 1863 book (H. von Helmholtz, On the Sensations of Tone, translated by A. J. Ellis, 2nd English ed., Longmans, Green, 1895).

At each moment the string is two straight pieces, like a plucked string, with the corner at some point x_c. Helmholtz showed that the corner’s height is proportional to x_c(L - x_c), so it traces two arcs of a parabola, the lens in the demo, and that the corner moves at the wave speed c = 2Lf_1. Put that height in place of h in the pluck formula with x_0 = x_c: the factor x_c(L - x_c) cancels, leaving a_n \propto \sin(n\pi x_c/L)/n^2. With x_c = 2Lf_1t this is \sin(2\pi nf_1t)/n^2. On the way back the corner runs along the lower arc, and the same formula covers it: for x_c between L and 2L, \sin(n\pi x_c/L) describes a corner at 2L - x_c with negative height. The force on the bridge is proportional to the slope there, and the slope of \sin(n\pi x/L) carries an extra factor n\pi/L, so partial n pushes the bridge in proportion to 1/n.

A real string is slightly stiff, so its own frequencies run a little sharp of the lines (the piano page); bowed, its motion is still “nearly exactly periodic”, so the bow pulls the partials onto the lines.5 The locking comes from the bow’s friction, which is not linear: a self-sustained oscillation (appendix), not derived here. Faster bowing, or bowing nearer the bridge, makes a larger swing and a louder note; more force makes a brighter tone; and only a limited range of force keeps Helmholtz motion going at all.6

5 J. Wolfe, Bows and strings, UNSW: “the motion of the string is nearly exactly periodic”, so bowing reduces the inharmonicity that plucking shows.

DetailsHow bow and string lock together

The bow adds a friction force at one point, which depends on how fast the string slips past the hair there. How the two lock into Helmholtz motion is observed and reproduced in computer models (M. E. McIntyre, R. T. Schumacher and J. Woodhouse, “On the oscillations of musical instruments”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 74 (1983), 1325–1345; N. H. Fletcher and T. D. What the light touch does

A light touch keeps only the shapes that stand still under the finger. The finger is soft and lossy: it takes energy from whatever moves under it, but does not hold the string still, as a new end would. A shape with a still point, a node, under the finger rings on; every other shape loses its energy within a moment. In the model, a finger at x_f drains shape n at a rate proportional to X_n(x_f)^2, how much that shape moves under it.

At the middle, X_n(L/2) = \sin(n\pi/2) is zero exactly when n is even. The survivors swing at 2f_1, 4f_1, 6f_1, \dots, multiples of 2f_1, so they repeat twice as often: a note an octave up. At a third, the survivors are n = 3, 6, 9, \dots, with lowest frequency 3f_1: an octave and a fifth up, since 2 \times \tfrac32 = 3. No frequency moved; the finger chose which shapes keep ringing.

PreciselyWhy the drain rate is X_n(x_f)^2 matrices

A friction force -r\,u_t(x_f, t) at the one point x_f turns post 2’s separate springs into q_n'' + \sum_m C_{nm}q_m' + \lambda_nq_n = 0 with C_{nm} \propto r\,X_n(x_f)X_m(x_f) (post 2). A shape with X_n(x_f) = 0 has a zero row and column in C, so it rings on untouched. For a light touch C is small, its off-diagonal entries matter only to second order, and each other shape drains at the rate C_{nn} \propto X_n(x_f)^2, the rule the piano page uses for felt dampers. The demo adds 45 per second times X_n(x_f)^2 to each shape’s own fading rate.

The body changes amounts, never frequencies

The string rocks the bridge, the bridge shakes the wooden body, and the body moves the air. Each step is linear: shaken at one frequency, it responds at that frequency, louder or quieter. So the body’s resonances, the same for every note, change the partials’ heights, not their positions, and are part of the violin’s timbre. Its air resonance sits near 300 Hz, and the bridge passes vibration to the body best from about 1 to 4 kHz.7 Toggle the wooden body on the violin above: the stems change height and stay put. (The demo’s four body resonances are rough illustrative values.)
