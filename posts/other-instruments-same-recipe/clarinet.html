Why is a clarinet almost an octave deeper than a flute?

A deep dive from the clarinet’s entry in post 3, which also covers the flute. Builds on post 2; the air obeys the string’s equation. Uses sines, cosines and derivatives; derivations are in folds. About 13 minutes. Written in collaboration with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model.

A clarinet and a flute are about the same length, yet the clarinet’s lowest note is almost an octave below the flute’s. And when players overblow, the flute jumps up an octave while the clarinet jumps an octave and a fifth. The same equation governs the air in both tubes. What is different?

The ends. A flute is open at both ends and a clarinet is closed at the reed, and a tube closed at one end fits a quarter of a wave where an open tube fits a half, so the same length sounds an octave lower and has modes only at the odd multiples of its lowest frequency. Hold the blow button on the clarinet, then switch the same tube to a flute and blow again; the register key (on the flute it stands in for a faster, narrower airstream) makes either tube jump to its next mode.

Shading: how much the pressure swings, dark where it swings most, pale where it stays at room pressure. The first open hole acts, roughly, as the end of the tube.

The reason, in one sentence: the two tubes have the same operator with different ends, and the ends decide which shapes, and so which frequencies, the air can have.

The tube’s equation

Let p(x, t) be the air pressure in the tube minus room pressure, at distance x from the reed end. Newton’s law on a thin slab of air, pushed by the pressure difference across it, together with the way air pushes back when squeezed, gives the wave equation again, the string’s equation, now for the pressure (the flute’s entry derives it): \begin{gathered} p_{tt} = -Ap, \qquad Ap = -c^2p_{xx}, \\ c^2 = \frac{K}{\rho} . \end{gathered} In words: the pressure at each point changes in proportion to how sharply the pressure shape bends. Here K is the air’s bulk modulus, its resistance to being squeezed, \rho its density, and c the speed of sound, about 343 m/s at 20 °C.1

1 Wikipedia, Speed of sound: about 343 m/s in dry air at 20 °C.

Only the ends differ between a flute and a clarinet, and they are part of the operator A:

  • An open end meets the room, so the pressure there is room pressure, p = 0, a Dirichlet condition.2 The flute has two: p(0) = p(L) = 0.
  • The clarinet’s reed is nearly shut, so the air there cannot move back and forth, and air that never moves feels no net push: the pressure has no slope, p_x(0) = 0, a Neumann condition. Its bell is open, p(L) = 0.

2 J. Wolfe, Pipes and harmonics, UNSW: at an open end the “sound pressure (difference from atmospheric) is close to zero”.

DetailsThe reed end, the end correction, and what the model leaves out

Let \xi(x, t) be how far the air that normally sits at x has moved along the tube. Newton’s law on a slab is \rho\,\xi_{tt} = -p_x. At the reed the air cannot move, so \xi = 0 there at all times, so \xi_{tt} = 0, and Newton’s law gives p_x = 0. The air just outside an open end moves with the air inside, so the tube acts slightly longer, by about 0.6 of its radius, an amount called the end correction (J. Wolfe, Pipes and harmonics, UNSW: “usually about 0.6 times the radius”). The model assumes a narrow cylindrical tube, small pressure changes and no friction at the walls. A real clarinet’s bore is not exactly cylindrical, and its holes and bell matter; they refine the numbers below, not the picture (N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapters 8 and 15).

Two tubes, two spectra

The shapes the operator only stretches, AX_n = \lambda_nX_n, swing at f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi. With the flute’s ends they are the string’s sines; with the clarinet’s they are cosines that fit an odd number of quarter waves: \begin{aligned} &\text{flute:} && X_n = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \\ & && f_n = n\,\frac{c}{2L}, \\ &\text{clarinet:} && X_n = \cos\frac{(2n - 1)\pi x}{2L}, \\ & && f_n = (2n - 1)\,\frac{c}{4L} . \end{aligned} The flute’s modes sit at 1, 2, 3, 4, … times its lowest frequency, the clarinet’s at 1, 3, 5, 7, … times its lowest, and the clarinet’s lowest, c/4L, is half the flute’s c/2L.3

3 J. Wolfe, Pipes and harmonics, UNSW: closed cylindrical bores “have only odd harmonics starting one octave lower”.

PreciselyThe two spectra, step by step derivatives

Writing \lambda = c^2k^2, the mode equation -c^2X'' = \lambda X becomes X'' = -k^2X, solved by X = a\cos kx + b\sin kx, with f = \sqrt\lambda/2\pi = ck/2\pi.

Flute. X(0) = 0 forces a = 0, and X(L) = 0 needs \sin kL = 0, so kL = n\pi: one half wave, two half waves, and so on. So \lambda_n = (n\pi c/L)^2 and f_n = nc/2L.

Clarinet. X'(0) = bk = 0 forces b = 0, leaving X = \cos kx, and X(L) = 0 needs \cos kL = 0, so kL = (2n - 1)\pi/2: one quarter wave, three quarter waves, and so on. So \lambda_n = \big((2n - 1)\pi c/2L\big)^2 and f_n = (2n - 1)c/4L.

As for the string, integration by parts rules out \lambda \le 0, and every pressure shape is a sum of these modes; the appendix says when such a sum is guaranteed.

The tubes' modes, as multiples of each tube's lowest frequency: open at both ends (top), every line; closed at one end (bottom), the odd lines only. The stems mark where modes can sit; their heights carry no meaning here.

Almost an octave deeper

A closed tube fits a wave twice as long. The flute’s lowest mode, \sin(\pi x/L), has room pressure at both ends and swings most in the middle: half a wavelength fits in the tube. The clarinet’s, \cos(\pi x/2L), swings most at the reed and has room pressure at the bell: only a quarter of a wavelength fits. A wave twice as long has half the frequency, so the same ideal tube sounds exactly an octave lower closed at one end.4 Real flutes and clarinets differ a little in length, the clarinet’s mouthpiece and bell change its effective length, and open ends act slightly longer than they are, so the real interval comes out a bit less than an octave.5

4 J. Wolfe, Open vs closed pipes (flutes vs clarinets), UNSW: for the same length, the closed pipe’s lowest frequency “c/4L = 140 Hz – one octave lower than the flute”.

5 J. Wolfe, Open vs closed pipes (flutes vs clarinets), UNSW: the flute’s lowest note is close to middle C, while “the lowest note on a clarinet is either the D or the C# below middle C”; “There are complications due to end corrections”, and the clarinet has a mouthpiece at one end and “a flare and a bell” at the other.

Blown: one repeating cycle

A blown tube does not ring and fade: the reed or the air jet keeps feeding it, and the air settles into one motion that repeats. The clarinet’s reed acts as a valve: when the pressure in the mouthpiece drops it closes, and when the pressure rises it opens, so the air column drives the reed that feeds it.6 On a flute, a jet of air blown across the embouchure hole does the reed’s job.7 Either way the result is a self-sustained oscillation (appendix), at a playing frequency close to the tube’s sounding mode. For the lowest notes that is c/4L on the clarinet and c/2L on the flute, with L the tube up to the first open hole: the fingers choose the note by choosing L.

6 J. Wolfe, Clarinet acoustics: an introduction, UNSW: “the reed acts like an oscillating valve”.

7 J. Wolfe, Flute acoustics: an introduction, UNSW: “The flutist blows a rapid jet of air across the embouchure hole”; “The flute is open at both ends.”

8 J. Wolfe, Clarinet acoustics: an introduction, UNSW: “the sound spectrum in the low register has strong first and third harmonics, but weak second and fourth”; even harmonics become more important from about E4.

9 J. Wolfe, Clarinet acoustics: an introduction, UNSW: added harmonics make the sound brighter and louder; soft playing in the linear range gives “few high harmonics”.

The tube’s modes pick the strong partials. A sound that repeats has partials only at whole-number multiples of its repetition rate (post 1), but it may have all of them. So the clarinet’s modes are odd multiples only, while its sound has even multiples too: weak in the low register, where no mode of the tube supports them, but not absent. In the low register the clarinet has strong first and third harmonics and weak second and fourth; from about E4 up, the even harmonics matter more.8 Blowing harder drives the reed further from gentle, nearly linear motion, which adds high harmonics, so the tone gets brighter as well as louder.9

The blown sounds, as multiples of the note being played: the flute (top) has every whole-number multiple; the clarinet (bottom) has strong odd multiples and faint even ones. The demos' sounds are made this way; the exact strengths are an illustrative choice.

Overblowing: a twelfth or an octave

The flute’s spectrum has every whole-number multiple of its lowest frequency; the clarinet’s has only the odd ones, with nothing in between. So when a clarinetist presses the register key, the tube jumps to its next mode, 3 times its lowest frequency: an octave and a fifth, called a twelfth.10 A flutist overblows with a faster jet, and the flute’s next mode is 2 times its lowest, an octave.11 That is why clarinet fingerings repeat a twelfth apart and flute fingerings an octave apart. (The demo keeps the low-register tone colour when the register key is on.)

10 J. Wolfe, Open vs closed pipes (flutes vs clarinets), UNSW: “the second register of the clarinet is a musical twelfth above the first”.

11 J. Wolfe, Flute acoustics: an introduction, UNSW: blowing harder or narrowing the lips gives “a faster jet”, and the flute “jumps up” to the octave, 2f_1.

DetailsHow the register key picks out the 3× mode

The register key opens a small hole about a third of the way down the closed part of the tube. An open hole pulls the pressure there toward room pressure, which spoils the fundamental, a mode that needs pressure variation at that point. For one note in the low register the hole sits right where that note’s 3f_1 mode has no pressure variation anyway, so the open hole does not disturb that mode. For the other notes the hole is off that point, and a second effect helps: the air in a small hole has mass, so the faster it must move back and forth, the harder it is to move. At the fundamental’s lower frequency the hole leaks enough to spoil that mode; at the 3f_1 mode’s higher frequency it acts almost as a plug: “high frequency waves pass by as though it were closed”. Either way the tube jumps to the 3f_1 mode. (J. Wolfe, Clarinet acoustics: an introduction, UNSW)

For a sustained instrument the four aspects come from different places than for a ringing one: the repeating cycle gives the whole-number multiples, the tube’s modes set which are strong, and the player sets the rest.
