Struck or plucked and left to ring, most instruments sound at the frequencies set by their own eigenvalues: a little sharp of whole-number multiples of the fundamental on a piano, far off them on a kalimba, between them on a drum. Bowed, blown or, like the theremin, kept going by a circuit, they sound exactly on whole-number multiples. Below, one instrument from each of the five Hornbostel–Sachs classes1 plays Grieg,2 every voice built from the spectra on this page.

The cards, as text. Where the stems come from. Musicologists sort instruments into five classes by what makes the sound (Hornbostel and Sachs, 1914; electrophones added later). One recipe turns each into its stems. (1) For small motions, Newton's law on a small piece of the instrument takes the form utt = −Au: u is the instrument's shape, and the operator A turns a shape into minus the acceleration it causes. (2) A shape that the operator only stretches, AX = λX, swings at one frequency, f = √λ / 2π. (3) Left to ring, every motion is a sum of such shapes (for a bounded object without friction; page 5 proves it for the string, and the fold under “The same recipe” gives the theorem for the others), so the list of the λ's, the spectrum of the operator, decides where the stems stand.

Electrophone, sine theremin. Kirchhoff's law on a coil and a capacitor; operator A = 1/LC, a single number, like a mass on a spring; spectrum f = 1/(2π√(LC)). One stem: a pure tone. A real theremin runs two such circuits at radio frequencies, the hand changing C of one, and you hear the difference of their frequencies, with overtones.

Chordophone, piano. Newton's law on a short piece of stiff steel wire; operator A = −c² d²/dx² + κ² d⁴/dx⁴, ends pinned; spectrum fn = n f0 √(1 + Bn²). Just above the lines, more so as n grows: a note, slightly stretched.

Aerophone, clarinet. Newton's law on a thin slice of the air in the tube; operator A = −c² d²/dx² on pressure, closed at the reed and open at the far end; the tube's spectrum fn = (2n − 1) c / 4L. Blown, the air repeats one cycle, so the partials sit exactly on the lines; the tube's odd modes make the odd ones strong and the even ones weak: a hollow note.

Idiophone, glockenspiel. Newton's law on a thin slice of a steel bar; operator A = κ² d⁴/dx⁴, both ends free; spectrum from cos βL cosh βL = 1, ratios 1 : 2.76 : 5.40. Off the lines, but the overtones die fast: a bright, clean note.

Membranophones, bass drum and timpani. Newton's law on a small patch of the drumhead; operator A = −c²Δ, rim held still; spectrum from Jm(ka) = 0, ratios 1 : 1.59 : 2.14 : 2.30 …; the timpani's spectrum is measured, not derived. Bass drum: struck off centre, mostly round modes, off the lines and fading fast: a boom with no clear pitch. Timpani: the same skin; the air it drags along pulls its spoke modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5, the stroke favours them and the round modes fade fast: a note.

What you hear: two kinds of instrument

What you hear is a sum of partials, {p(t) = \sum_n c_n e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_nt + \varphi_n)}, read as in page 2, and the instruments come in two kinds by where the frequencies f_n sit.

A ringing instrument is struck or plucked, then left alone: its partials sit at its operator’s spectrum (below), each fading at its own rate.

A self-sustained instrument is kept going by a bow, an air jet, a reed or an amplifier, whose push depends on the motion itself, not in proportion to it.3 The drive locks the motion into one repeating cycle, a self-sustained oscillation, so the frequencies are exact multiples nf_1 of the playing frequency f_1 (page 2). The spectrum of the driven part, the resonator, then decides which notes can sound; the drive’s waveform (on a harmonica, the puff’s shape) and the resonator (and, for the violin, the body) decide how strong each partial is.

3 Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapters 10 (bowed strings), 15 (woodwind reed instruments) and 16 (flutes).

In every demo the stems are the sound’s partials on page 2’s pitch ruler, whose gray lines are whole-number multiples of one frequency; taller stems are stronger partials.

How the sound gets out

The vibrating part pushes on something that moves the air. A string about 1 mm thick is too thin to move the air itself,4 so it pushes the bridge, which moves the soundboard, a large thin wooden panel that pushes the air. A piano’s or guitar’s bridge is glued on; a violin’s is a carved maple piece held on the top plate by the strings’ pressure.5 A kalimba tine pushes its box through the clamp. A drum skin, and the air at a tube’s open end, push the air directly.

For a string, as in page 3, the sound at your ear is p = h * F: the input F that reaches the air, convolved with the tap response h; the table shows how the other routes generalize it. Once the body’s own ring at the start has died away, this keeps each frequency and multiplies it by |H(f)|, where H is the Fourier transform of h.

The input F comes from the vibrating part’s motion u by a route specific to each instrument, the table’s second row. Each route acts at each instant, so it keeps every frequency and only weights each mode: for a string of tension T and length L, F = -T\,u_x(L, t) weights mode n by T times its end slope, \pm n\pi/L (subscripts are partial derivatives).

In the table, which leaves out fading, a_n and \psi_n are the size and phase of mode n in u, X_n is mode n’s shape (next section), \omega_n = 2\pi f_n, x runs along a string or tube, and u_{tt} is the acceleration.

DetailsWhy the frequencies survive, and the drum, kalimba and clarinet columns

A sine stays a sine. With H(f) = \int h(s)\,e^{-2\pi i f s}\,ds, the Fourier transform of h, a short computation gives h * \sin(\omega t) = |H(f)|\sin(\omega t + \arg H(f)) for \omega = 2\pi f. So with one input, c_n is the input’s amount of mode n times |H(f_n)|.

Drum. A drum small compared with the wavelength sends out sound in proportion to the net volume each mode sweeps, so for a given swing and frequency c_n \propto |\iint X_n\,dA|. A mode with m \ge 1 still lines through the center varies around the drum as \cos m\theta, so \iint X_n\,dA = 0: their sound is zero at this (monopole) order, and they radiate only weakly, as dipoles (m = 1) or weaker, while the round modes radiate strongly (N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapters 7 and 18).

Kalimba. The clamp, at x = 0, passes two things to the box: the bent tine’s shear force, F_1 = V(0, t) = -EI\,u_{xxx}(0, t) in the kalimba page’s convention, and its bending moment, a turning push, F_2 = EI\,u_{xx}(0, t); here E is steel’s Young’s modulus and I the second moment of area (derived on the kalimba page).

Clarinet and flute. The open end is a small source whose strength is the rate at which the outflow changes, and the pressure slope -u_x(L, t) sets that rate. At a distance R in open air, p(t) = -(S/4\pi R)\,u_x(L, t - R/c_0), where S is the area of the opening and c_0 the speed of sound. For most notes the “open end” is the first open tone holes, and the bell radiates the lowest notes and the high partials above the tone holes’ cutoff; a flute also radiates from its embouchure hole (Fletcher and Rossing, above, chapters 7, 8, 15 and 16).

The same recipe

For a plucked or struck note, the last link of the chain is the only one that creates frequencies: the motion obeys u_{tt} = -Au, with the object’s boundary conditions and its start or drive. Here the operator A turns a shape into minus the acceleration it causes. For the instruments left to ring, every motion is a sum of the object’s own shapes, the eigenfunctions X_n with AX_n = \lambda_nX_n, each swinging at f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi. The kettledrum is the exception: it radiates into the unbounded air, which takes it outside this argument (fold), so its ratios here are measured.6

6 A kettledrum measured by A. H. Benade, Fundamentals of Musical Acoustics, Oxford University Press, 1976, chapter 9, pp. 143–144, as tabulated by HyperPhysics, The Timpani, Georgia State University: spoke modes at about 1.50, 2.00, 2.49 and 2.98 times the lowest.

PreciselyThe theorem, and its three names uses Hilbert spaces

What carries over from the string: on a bounded object with a reasonable edge, each of these operators is self-adjoint and \geq 0 and has a compact resolvent, so it has an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions, and drifts split off. The three routes are Sturm–Liouville theory for strings and tubes, a compact Green’s operator for bars, and Rellich’s theorem for drums (R. Courant and D. Hilbert, Methods of Mathematical Physics, vol. 1, Interscience, 1953, chapters III (symmetric kernels), V and VI; L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., American Mathematical Society, 2010, sections 5.7 and 6.5.1). The kettledrum’s radiation, sound carried away into the unbounded air, makes its problem non-self-adjoint, and its full air loading is beyond this page; the air’s added mass alone would keep it inside the theorem, as the drum page’s toy model of the air does.

A Sturm–Liouville problem is -(p\,X')' + q\,X = \lambda\,w\,X on a bounded interval, with p, w > 0 and one condition at each end of the form \alpha X + \beta X' = 0; the string (p = c^2, q = 0, w = 1) and the tubes, open or closed at each end, are of this form, and its eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis (R. Courant and D. Hilbert, Methods of Mathematical Physics, vol. 1, Interscience, 1953, chapters V and VI). For a bar, (A + 1)^{-1} is an integral operator with a kernel continuous on [0, L]^2, the Green’s operator, which is compact and self-adjoint, so the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators applies to it, and its eigenfunctions are A’s. For a drumhead, Rellich’s theorem says that on a bounded region, every sequence of shapes that vanish on the rim and have \iint u^2 and \iint |\nabla u|^2 bounded has a subsequence converging in mean square; this makes (A + 1)^{-1} compact (L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., American Mathematical Society, 2010, section 5.7). A drift occurs only when A has the eigenvalue 0: on this page, for the glockenspiel bar.

In short, c_n = |H(f_n)|\,w_n\,|a_n| , with w_n the route weight (for one input; on the kalimba, mode n’s shear V_n and moment M_n at the clamp combine as {|H_1V_n + H_2M_n|}, and a small drum’s points act as one): the pitch traces back to the spectrum of A, the timbre to the start, the route, H and the spacing of that spectrum, and the duration to the losses.

A piano

A piano note is clear, but its upper partials run slightly sharp of the whole-number lines because the steel wire resists bending as well as being pulled straight.7 So an exact 2 : 1 octave beats, and tuners widen octaves.8

A kalimba

A kalimba tine gives a click, then a clean note: its first overtone, far off the lines and strong at the attack, dies within a fraction of a second.9

A glockenspiel

A glockenspiel gives a bright, clear note although its overtones sit off the lines: they are high and die quickly, leaving the fundamental.12 Its bar is the kalimba tine’s kind of steel bar with both ends free, resting on soft supports and struck with a mallet: \begin{gathered} u_{tt} = -\kappa^2u_{xxxx} \quad (0 < x < L), \\ u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 \ \text{ at } x = 0 \text{ and } x = L, \\ u(x, 0) = 0, \qquad u_t(x, 0) = v_0(x). \end{gathered} Here u(x, t) is the bar’s displacement, L its length, \kappa^2 its stiffness over its mass, and v_0 the mallet’s blow (fold).

12 G. P. Scavone, Percussion instruments, course notes, CCRMA, Stanford University, 1999: “All but the lowest transverse mode die out quickly when the bar is struck”; Fletcher and Rossing, chapter 19 (mallet percussion).

13 Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapters 2 and 19.

Writing \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, the eigenvalues solve \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = 1, which puts the frequencies in the ratios 1 : 2.756 : 5.404, off the lines.13 Two eigenvalues are 0: the bar sliding or tilting without bending, drifts that move as a + bt, for constants a and b, and make no sound.

DetailsWhere the equation comes from

Here \kappa^2 = EI/\rho S: E is steel’s Young’s modulus, I the second moment of area of the cross-section, \rho the density and S the cross-section’s area; v_0 is the mallet’s blow, concentrated where it lands. The equation is the kalimba tine’s beam equation, from the same slice of bar (derived on the kalimba page). Only the ends differ: on soft supports both ends are free, so at each end no bending moment and no shear force act, u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0. The supports are taken as not holding the bar at all; resting at the fundamental’s still points, they are where that mode does not move.

PreciselyThe spectrum, step by step uses a 2×2 determinant

With \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, X = b_1\cos\beta x + b_2\sin\beta x + b_3\cosh\beta x + b_4\sinh\beta x. The free left end, X''(0) = X'''(0) = 0, gives b_3 = b_1 and b_4 = b_2. With c_1 = \cos\beta L, s_1 = \sin\beta L, c_2 = \cosh\beta L and s_2 = \sinh\beta L (hyperbolic functions), the free right end gives (c_2 - c_1)b_1 + (s_2 - s_1)b_2 = 0 and (s_2 + s_1)b_1 + (c_2 - c_1)b_2 = 0, whose determinant is (c_2 - c_1)^2 - (s_2^2 - s_1^2) = 2 - 2c_1c_2. So \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = 1, with roots \beta L = 4.7300, 7.8532, 10.9956, \dots For \lambda = 0, X'''' = 0 with free ends leaves exactly X = a + bx: two eigenfunctions with eigenvalue 0, whose coordinates move as q = a + bt. The fundamental’s still points, where X_1 = 0, are at 0.224L and 0.776L (computed).

A flute

A flute plays a steady note on the lines. It is a tube of air open at both ends,17 kept going by a jet blown across the embouchure hole.18 An open end holds room pressure, so the tube’s own spectrum is the string’s, f_n = nc/2L, with c the speed of sound and L the tube’s length: on the lines too.19 It overblows to the octave with a faster, narrower airstream.20

A clarinet

A clarinet sounds a hollow note nearly an octave below a flute of the same length. Its tube is closed at the reed, so its spectrum is f_n = (2n - 1)c/4L: the odd multiples of c/4L, half the flute’s lowest frequency.21 So in the low register the odd partials are strong and the even ones weak.22

A harmonica

A harmonica reed’s own overtones sit far off the lines, yet its note has partials only on the lines, odd and even both strong. You hear the air, not the reed: the reed lets one identical puff through its slot per cycle, and repeating puffs have only whole-number partials.23

A theremin

A theremin’s tone glides as the hand moves, without the instrument being touched.24 It comes from coil-and-capacitor circuits, LC circuits, whose charge swings like a mass on a spring: \ddot q = -\frac{1}{LC}\,q . Here q is the charge, \ddot q its second time derivative, L the coil’s inductance and C the capacitance (fold). An amplifier keeps the swing at a steady size.25

24 Wikipedia, Theremin.

25 Wikipedia, Electronic oscillator: an amplifier feeding a tuned circuit in phase, its amplitude limited by a nonlinearity.

26 Wikipedia, Theremin: two radio-frequency oscillators below 500 kHz.

The spectrum is one frequency, f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}), a radio frequency below 500 kHz.26 The instrument runs two such circuits, one changed by the hand, and outputs the difference of their frequencies, which is audible (heterodyning).

DetailsWhere the equation comes from

Here q is the charge on the capacitor, L the coil’s inductance and C the capacitance, so the operator is A = 1/LC, one number. Kirchhoff’s voltage law, which balances voltages as Newton’s law balances forces, says that the voltages around a closed loop add to zero. A capacitor holding charge q has the voltage q/C across it; a coil carrying the current I = \dot q has the voltage L\,\dot I. Around the loop, L\,\ddot q + q/C = 0, that is, \ddot q = -q/LC: the coil plays the mass and 1/C the spring’s stiffness. For ideal parts both laws are linear, so no small-motion step is needed.

DetailsHow two radio frequencies make one audible tone

A mixer multiplies the two oscillators’ signals, and \sin(2\pi f_1t)\sin(2\pi f_2t) = \tfrac12\cos(2\pi(f_1 - f_2)t) - \tfrac12\cos(2\pi(f_1 + f_2)t); a filter keeps the difference. With both near a few hundred kilohertz, a hand that changes one circuit’s capacitance by a small fraction moves the difference across the whole audible range. Real theremins’ mixers and circuits add overtones, on the whole-number lines of the difference frequency, since the output repeats at that rate.
