Struck or plucked and left to ring, most instruments sound at the frequencies of their own eigenvalues: a little sharp of the whole-number lines on a piano, far off them on a kalimba, between them on a drum. Bowed, blown or, like the theremin, kept going by a circuit, they sound exactly on the lines. Below, one instrument from each of the five Hornbostel–Sachs classes, sorted by what makes the sound,1 plays Grieg,2 every voice built from the spectra on this page.

The cards, as text. Where the stems come from. Musicologists sort instruments into five classes by what makes the sound (Hornbostel and Sachs, 1914; electrophones added later). One recipe turns each into its stems. (1) For small motions, Newton's law on a small piece of the instrument takes the form utt = −Au: u is the instrument's shape, and the operator A turns a shape into minus the acceleration it causes. (2) A shape that the operator only stretches, AX = λX, swings at one frequency, f = √λ / 2π. (3) Left to ring, every motion is a sum of such shapes (for a bounded object without friction; post 2 proves it for the string, and the fold under “The same recipe” gives the theorem for the others), so the list of the λ's, the spectrum of the operator, decides where the stems stand.

Electrophone, sine theremin. Kirchhoff's law on a coil and a capacitor; operator A = 1/LC, a single number, like a mass on a spring; spectrum f = 1/(2π√(LC)). One stem: a pure tone. A real theremin runs two such circuits at radio frequencies, the hand changing C of one, and you hear the difference of their frequencies, with overtones.

Chordophone, piano. Newton's law on a short piece of stiff steel wire; operator A = −c² d²/dx² + κ² d⁴/dx⁴, ends pinned; spectrum fn = n f0 √(1 + Bn²). Just above the lines, more so as n grows: a note, slightly stretched.

Aerophone, clarinet. Newton's law on a thin slice of the air in the tube; operator A = −c² d²/dx² on pressure, closed at the reed and open at the far end; the tube's spectrum fn = (2n − 1) c / 4L. Blown, the air repeats one cycle, so the partials sit exactly on the lines; the tube's odd modes make the odd ones strong and the even ones weak: a hollow note.

Idiophone, glockenspiel. Newton's law on a thin slice of a steel bar; operator A = κ² d⁴/dx⁴, both ends free; spectrum from cos βL cosh βL = 1, ratios 1 : 2.76 : 5.40. Off the lines, but the overtones die fast: a bright, clean note.

Membranophones, bass drum and timpani. Newton's law on a small patch of the drumhead; operator A = −c²Δ, rim held still; spectrum from Jm(ka) = 0, ratios 1 : 1.59 : 2.14 : 2.30 …; the timpani's spectrum is measured, not derived. Bass drum: struck off centre, mostly round modes, off the lines and fading fast: a boom with no clear pitch. Timpani: the same skin; the air it drags along pulls its spoke modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5, the stroke favours them and the round modes fade fast: a note.

The reason, in one sentence: a ringing instrument moves as a sum of its own modes, as the string does, so you hear its spectrum; a bowed or blown one is pushed into one repeating cycle, and a repeating cycle has only whole-number partials.

The same recipe

Every instrument below is a problem of the same shape as the string’s: an equation, how the object is held, and how it starts or is driven. For the ones left to ring, the string’s argument carries over to every bounded object here: each has its own shapes, the eigenfunctions of its operator, and every motion is a sum of them, each swinging at f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi, apart from the glockenspiel’s two drifts, its slide and tilt, which move as a + bt. The exception is the kettledrum: its sound radiates into the unbounded air around it, which takes it outside this argument (see the fold), so its ratios here are measured.3

3 A kettledrum measured by A. H. Benade, Fundamentals of Musical Acoustics, Oxford University Press, 1976, chapter 9, pp. 143–144, as tabulated by HyperPhysics, The Timpani, Georgia State University: spoke modes at about 1.50, 2.00, 2.49 and 2.98 times the lowest.

PreciselyThe theorem, and its three names uses Hilbert spaces

What carries over from the string: on a bounded object with a reasonable edge, each of these operators is self-adjoint and \geq 0 and has a compact resolvent, so it has an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions, and drifts split off. The three routes are Sturm–Liouville theory for strings and tubes, a compact Green’s operator for bars, and Rellich’s theorem for drums (R. Courant and D. Hilbert, Methods of Mathematical Physics, vol. 1, Interscience, 1953, chapters III (symmetric kernels), V and VI; L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., American Mathematical Society, 2010, sections 5.7 and 6.5.1). The kettledrum’s radiation, sound carried away into the unbounded air, makes its problem non-self-adjoint, and its full air loading is beyond this page; the air’s added mass alone would keep it inside the theorem, as the drum page’s toy model of the air does.

A Sturm–Liouville problem is -(p\,X')' + q\,X = \lambda\,w\,X on a bounded interval, with p, w > 0 and one condition at each end of the form \alpha X + \beta X' = 0; the string (p = c^2, q = 0, w = 1) and the tubes, open or closed at each end, are of this form, and its eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis (R. Courant and D. Hilbert, Methods of Mathematical Physics, vol. 1, Interscience, 1953, chapters V and VI). For a bar, (A + 1)^{-1} is an integral operator with a kernel continuous on [0, L]^2, the Green’s operator, which is compact and self-adjoint, so the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators applies to it, and its eigenfunctions are A’s. For a drumhead, Rellich’s theorem says that on a bounded region, every sequence of shapes that vanish on the rim and have \iint u^2 and \iint |\nabla u|^2 bounded has a subsequence converging in mean square; this makes (A + 1)^{-1} compact (L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., American Mathematical Society, 2010, section 5.7). A drift occurs only when A has the eigenvalue 0: on this page, for the glockenspiel bar.

Two kinds of entry

A ringing entry is struck or plucked, then left alone: its partials sit wherever its spectrum falls, each fading at its own rate.

A self-sustained entry is kept going by the player, with a bow, an air jet, a reed or an amplifier, whose push depends on the motion itself, not in proportion to it.4 Its motion settles into one repeating cycle, a self-sustained oscillation, so its partials are exact whole-number multiples (post 1), whatever the spectrum of the driven part, the resonator; the drive and the resonator decide only which partials are strong.

4 Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapters 10 (bowed strings), 15 (woodwind reed instruments) and 16 (flutes).

In every demo the stems are the sound’s partials on the pitch ruler, taller when stronger, and the picture is slowed down and enlarged.

A piano

A piano note is clear, but its upper partials run slightly sharp of the whole-number lines, more so the higher they are, because the steel wire resists bending as well as being pulled straight.5 So an exact 2 : 1 octave beats, and tuners widen octaves, most at the keyboard’s ends.6

5 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 12 (the piano); Wikipedia, Inharmonicity.

6 O. L. Railsback, “Scale temperament as applied to piano tuning”, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 9 (1938), 274 (DOI); Wikipedia, Piano acoustics.

Illustrative: the fading times.

The spectrum is f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, where B measures the wire’s stiffness, about 4.4\times10^{-4} for a middle C wire. The player controls the hammer’s speed (harder is louder and brighter),7 the dampers and the pedals; none of them moves a frequency (A Piano).

A kalimba

A kalimba tine gives a click, then a clean note: its first overtone, far off the lines and strong at the attack, dies within a fraction of a second.8 The thumb bends the tine into a smooth curve and lets go.

8 D. M. F. Chapman, The sound of the African thumb piano (kalimba), Acoustics’08 Paris, paper 5aMUf1, Acoustical Society of America, 2008: one dominant overtone, at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, dying away quickly after the attack.

Illustrative: the fading times, chosen to match Chapman's observation that the overtones die within a fraction of a second; the stems shrink as each partial fades. The tine is drawn as it moves; in displacement its first overtone is only 2.5% of the fundamental, so the picture shows mostly the first mode, while in the push on the bridge it starts at 55%.

The spectrum is in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55, and f_1 \propto 1/L^2: half the length sounds two octaves up.9 The player sets the loudness by how far the tip is pushed and can stop a tine with a finger; makers tune a tine by sliding it over the bridge.10

A glockenspiel

A glockenspiel gives a bright, clear note although its overtones sit off the lines: they are high and die quickly, leaving the fundamental.11 Its bar is the same kind of steel bar as the kalimba’s tine, with both ends free; it rests on soft supports and is struck with a mallet. \begin{gathered} u_{tt} = -\kappa^2u_{xxxx} \quad (0 < x < L), \\ u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 \ \text{ at } x = 0 \text{ and } x = L, \\ u(x, 0) = 0, \qquad u_t(x, 0) = v_0(x). \end{gathered} Here \kappa^2 is the bar’s stiffness over its mass, and v_0 the mallet’s blow (fold).

11 G. P. Scavone, Percussion instruments, course notes, CCRMA, Stanford University, 1999: “All but the lowest transverse mode die out quickly when the bar is struck”; Fletcher and Rossing, chapter 19 (mallet percussion).

12 Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapters 2 and 19.

The spectrum is in the ratios 1 : 2.756 : 5.404, off the lines, from \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = 1.12 It also has two eigenvalues 0, the bar sliding or tilting without bending: drifts, which move as a + bt instead of swinging and make no sound.

The supports, at the fundamental’s still points 0.224L from each end, barely damp it.13 Xylophone and marimba bars, unlike glockenspiel bars, are thinned underneath to put the first overtone on a line, 3× and 4×.14 The player sets the loudness by the blow, the brightness by the mallet, metal brighter and rubber warmer,15 and stops a note with a hand; some glockenspiels on a stand have a damper pedal.16

DetailsWhere the equation comes from

Here \kappa^2 = EI/\rho S: E is steel’s Young’s modulus, I the second moment of area of the cross-section, \rho the density and S the cross-section’s area; v_0 is the mallet’s blow, concentrated where it lands. The equation is the kalimba tine’s beam equation, from the same slice of bar (derived on the kalimba page). Only the ends differ: on soft supports both ends are free, so at each end no bending moment and no shear force act, u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0. The supports are taken as not holding the bar at all; resting at the fundamental’s still points, they are where that mode does not move.

PreciselyThe spectrum, step by step uses a 2×2 determinant

With \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, X = b_1\cos\beta x + b_2\sin\beta x + b_3\cosh\beta x + b_4\sinh\beta x. The free left end, X''(0) = X'''(0) = 0, gives b_3 = b_1 and b_4 = b_2. With c_1 = \cos\beta L, s_1 = \sin\beta L, c_2 = \cosh\beta L and s_2 = \sinh\beta L (hyperbolic functions), the free right end gives (c_2 - c_1)b_1 + (s_2 - s_1)b_2 = 0 and (s_2 + s_1)b_1 + (c_2 - c_1)b_2 = 0, whose determinant is (c_2 - c_1)^2 - (s_2^2 - s_1^2) = 2 - 2c_1c_2. So \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = 1, with roots \beta L = 4.7300, 7.8532, 10.9956, \dots For \lambda = 0, X'''' = 0 with free ends leaves exactly X = a + bx: two eigenfunctions with eigenvalue 0, whose coordinates move as q = a + bt. The fundamental’s still points, where X_1 = 0, are at 0.224L and 0.776L (computed).

Drums

A bass drum booms with no clear pitch; a kettledrum, one of the timpani, plays a note. A violin, bowed

A bowed string holds a steady note with its partials exactly on the lines: the bow’s friction grabs the string and lets it slip once per period, so the string repeats one cycle, which Helmholtz watched through a vibration microscope by 1860.21

21 H. Helmholtz, “On the motion of the strings of a violin”, Proceedings of the Philosophical Society of Glasgow 5 (1860–64), 17–21, read 19 December 1860 (archive.org).

A flute

A flute plays a steady note on the lines; with every hole closed and an ever faster airstream, it climbs the harmonic series, 1, 2, 3, 4, … times the lowest.23 It is a tube of air open at both ends, kept going by a jet blown across the embouchure hole.24 An open end holds room pressure, so the tube’s spectrum has the string’s numbers, f_n = nc/2L, on every line.25

24 Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapter 16 (flutes); Wolfe, Flute acoustics: the jet.

A clarinet

A clarinet sounds a hollow note nearly an octave below a flute of the same length. Its tube is closed at the reed, which halves the lowest frequency and leaves only odd multiples of it, so in the low register the odd partials are strong and the even ones weak.27

A harmonica

A harmonica reed’s own overtones sit far off the lines, yet its note has partials only on the lines, odd and even both strong. You hear the air, not the reed: the reed lets one identical puff through its slot per cycle, and repeating puffs have only whole-number partials.30

30 C. Pykett, The physics of free reeds, 2023: the sound comes from the puffs; odd and even harmonics.

A theremin

A theremin is played without being touched, and its tone glides as the hand moves.34 It makes the tone with electric circuits, each a coil and a capacitor, an LC circuit, whose charge swings like a mass on a spring: \ddot q = -\frac{1}{LC}\,q . Here q is the charge, L the coil’s inductance and C the capacitance (fold). An amplifier keeps the circuit swinging at a steady size.35

34 Wikipedia, Theremin.

35 Wikipedia, Electronic oscillator: an amplifier feeding a tuned circuit in phase, its amplitude limited by a nonlinearity.

36 Wikipedia, Theremin: two radio-frequency oscillators below 500 kHz.

The spectrum is one frequency, f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}), a radio frequency below 500 kHz.36 The instrument runs two such circuits, one changed by the player’s hand, and outputs the difference of their frequencies, which is audible (heterodyning).

No resonator ticks: the circuits' own frequencies are radio frequencies, far beyond this ruler, and the tone you hear is the difference of two of them. Most players set the pitch with the right hand’s distance from one antenna and the loudness with the left hand’s from the other. Leon Theremin invented it in 1920 and patented it in 1928.37

37 Wikipedia, Theremin: the hands; invented 1920, patented 1928.

DetailsWhere the equation comes from

Here q is the charge on the capacitor, L the coil’s inductance and C the capacitance, so the operator is A = 1/LC, one number. Kirchhoff’s voltage law, which balances voltages as Newton’s law balances forces, says that the voltages around a closed loop add to zero. A capacitor holding charge q has the voltage q/C across it; a coil carrying the current I = \dot q has the voltage L\,\dot I. Around the loop, L\,\ddot q + q/C = 0, that is, \ddot q = -q/LC: the coil plays the mass and 1/C the spring’s stiffness. For ideal parts both laws are linear, so no small-motion step is needed.

DetailsHow two radio frequencies make one audible tone

A mixer multiplies the two oscillators’ signals, and \sin(2\pi f_1t)\sin(2\pi f_2t) = \tfrac12\cos(2\pi(f_1 - f_2)t) - \tfrac12\cos(2\pi(f_1 + f_2)t); a filter keeps the difference. With both near a few hundred kilohertz, a hand that changes one circuit’s capacitance by a small fraction moves the difference across the whole audible range. Real theremins’ mixers and circuits add overtones, on the whole-number lines of the difference frequency, since the output repeats at that rate.
