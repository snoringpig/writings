A kalimba tine’s first overtone sits at about 6.3 times its fundamental, far off the whole numbers, and overtones off the whole numbers are what make a bell clang.1 So why does a kalimba sound like a clean note?

1 Wikipedia, Inharmonicity: bells and other instruments whose overtones are not whole-number multiples of the fundamental.

A kalimba sounds clean because its off-line overtone dies within a fraction of a second, and the fundamental rings on alone. Tap a tine; then tick the box, which keeps the overtones ringing, and tap it again.

Tap a tine, or drag across several.

The tines are drawn to scale: a tine half as long sounds two octaves higher, as this page explains.

The reason, in one sentence: a tine springs back by resisting bending, which puts its overtones far off the whole-number lines, and the strongest of them starts loud but fades almost at once. The first half follows from Newton’s law; the second is measured.

Where the equation comes from

A tine is under no tension: it springs back because steel resists being bent. Let u(x, t) be its height at distance x from the clamp. Bend it, and the steel on the outside of the curve stretches while the inside compresses; together these forces turn each cross-section, a bending moment M = EI\,u_{xx} proportional to the curvature (E is steel’s Young’s modulus, how stiff the material is, and I the second moment of area, how strongly the cross-section’s shape resists bending).

A thin slice of the tine cannot spin, so the moments on its two faces are balanced by a sideways shear force V = -M_x, and the difference of the shear forces on its two faces accelerates it. For a slice of density \rho and cross-sectional area S, Newton’s law gives u_{tt} = -\kappa^2 u_{xxxx}, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\rho S}, the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation, with four derivatives where the string had two.2

2 Wikipedia, Euler–Bernoulli beam theory; N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2 (bars).

3 Wikipedia, Cantilever: a beam supported at one end only.

The ends complete the problem. The base is clamped: it can neither move nor tilt, u = u_x = 0. The tip is free: no moment and no shear force act there, u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0. A bar held like this is a cantilever.3 So the equation reads u_{tt} = -Au with A = \kappa^2\,\frac{d^4}{dx^4}, acting on shapes with u = u_x = 0 at the clamp and u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 at the tip. The model assumes small slopes, a uniform bar clamped at exactly one point, and no friction.

DetailsThe slice, step by step an integral over a cross-section

Bend the bar, and a middle line, the neutral axis, keeps its length. For small slopes the curvature is u_{xx}, and a fiber at height y above the neutral axis is stretched by the fraction -y\,u_{xx}, so by Hooke’s law it carries the force per unit area -E\,y\,u_{xx}. Over the cross-section these forces cancel, but their turning effects add up to \begin{gathered} M = -\int y(-Ey\,u_{xx})\,dA = EI\,u_{xx}, \\ I = \int y^2\,dA . \end{gathered} A thin slice’s rotational inertia is negligible, so the turning effects on it balance: V\,dx + dM = 0, that is, V = -M_x = -EI\,u_{xxx}. The shear forces on the two faces differ by V_x\,dx, which accelerates the slice’s mass \rho S\,dx: \rho S\,u_{tt} = V_x = -EI\,u_{xxxx}. A fourth-order equation needs two conditions at each end, which the clamp and the free tip supply. The model ignores the slices’ rotational inertia and shear deformation, which matter only for thick bars and high modes (Wikipedia, Euler–Bernoulli beam theory).

What four derivatives do. Squeeze a shape into half the length and every rate of change along it doubles. A string’s pull depends on two derivatives, so it grows 2 \times 2 = 4 times; a tine’s push depends on four, so it grows 2^4 = 16 times. Short wiggles are far stiffer in a tine than in a string, and both of the tine’s surprises below come from this.

The tine’s shapes

As for the string, the shapes that matter are the ones A only stretches, AX = \lambda X. Write \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, so that X'''' = \beta^4X. Four functions return to themselves after four derivatives: \cos\beta x and \sin\beta x, and \cosh\beta x and \sinh\beta x, the hyperbolic functions \cosh y = (e^y + e^{-y})/2 and \sinh y = (e^y - e^{-y})/2. The four end conditions are four equations for the four coefficients, and they have a solution other than zero exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1 . Since \cosh\beta L grows fast, \cos\beta L must be close to 0, so \beta L sits near (n - \tfrac12)\pi. Solved numerically, the roots are \beta_nL = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548,\ 10.9955, \dots, and so \lambda_n = \kappa^2\beta_n^4, \qquad f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{\kappa\,\beta_n^2}{2\pi}, in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39, growing like (2n - 1)^2: off the whole-number lines.4 The first root is the furthest from its estimate, \pi/2 \approx 1.571, and that shift is what breaks the pattern: if every root sat exactly at (n - \tfrac12)\pi, the ratios would be the whole numbers 1 : 9 : 25.

4 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2: the clamped–free bar’s frequencies, in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39.

DetailsThe mode condition from the four end conditions a 2×2 determinant

The clamp, X(0) = X'(0) = 0, leaves two constants: \begin{aligned} X = {} & a\,(\cos\beta x - \cosh\beta x) \\ & + b\,(\sin\beta x - \sinh\beta x) . \end{aligned} With the shorthand c_1 = \cos\beta L, s_1 = \sin\beta L, c_2 = \cosh\beta L, s_2 = \sinh\beta L, and dropping the common factors \beta^2 and \beta^3, the free tip’s X''(L) = 0 and X'''(L) = 0 read \begin{gathered} -(c_1 + c_2)\,a - (s_1 + s_2)\,b = 0, \\ (s_1 - s_2)\,a - (c_1 + c_2)\,b = 0 . \end{gathered} Two equations pa + qb = 0 and ra + sb = 0 have a solution other than a = b = 0 exactly when the determinant ps - qr vanishes. Here \begin{aligned} & (c_1 + c_2)^2 + (s_1 + s_2)(s_1 - s_2) \\ & \quad = c_1^2 + s_1^2 + 2c_1c_2 + c_2^2 - s_2^2 \\ & \quad = 2 + 2c_1c_2 , \end{aligned} by c_1^2 + s_1^2 = 1 and c_2^2 - s_2^2 = 1. So a mode exists exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1. The modes are X_n = \cosh\beta_n x - \cos\beta_n x - \sigma_n(\sinh\beta_n x - \sin\beta_n x), with \sigma_n = (\sinh\beta_nL - \sin\beta_nL)/(\cosh\beta_nL + \cos\beta_nL) from the second equation.

Reader questionWould other ends give whole numbers?

Yes, for some. A bar held in place at both ends but free to pivot has u = u_{xx} = 0 at each end. Then \sin(n\pi x/L) satisfies all four conditions, four derivatives multiply it by (n\pi/L)^4, and the frequency ratios are 1 : 4 : 9 : 16, whole numbers. The clamp and the free tip are what move the roots, and the first root most.

The pluck: a click

Released at rest from the bent shape a thumb holds it in, the tine moves as a sum of its shapes, each swinging at its own frequency: u(x, t) = \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} a_n \cos(2\pi f_n t)\,X_n(x) . In how far the tine swings, the first overtone is tiny: a_2 is 2.5% of a_1, because each mode gives way to the thumb in inverse proportion to its stiffness \lambda_n. But the kalimba’s body feels the push of the tine on the bridge, not its swing, and in that push the higher modes weigh more. There the first overtone starts at about 55% of the fundamental. That strong start is the click at the beginning of each note.

DetailsHow strongly a pluck at the tip starts each mode integration by parts
  1. The pluck shape. Before release, a push F at the tip holds the tine still. Inside, nothing pushes, so EI\,u_0'''' = 0 and u_0 is a cubic. The clamp, the moment-free tip and the tip height h leave u_0 = h\,x^2(3L - x)/2L^3, with u_0'''(L) = -3h/L^3 \ne 0: the shear force F = 3EIh/L^3 of the thumb. The start is smooth, as it must be: a shape with a corner has u_x jumping, so u_{xx} contains a spike and the bending energy \tfrac12 EI\int (u_{xx})^2 is infinite, which a finite push cannot store. The start fails the tip condition u_{xxx}(L) = 0, but only where the thumb pushed. That is allowed: in post 2’s weak sense of solution, a solution is tested only against smooth shapes \varphi that satisfy all four end conditions, through \langle u(t), \varphi\rangle, so the series needs only a start of finite bending energy with u_0 = u_0' = 0 at the clamp; the tip’s conditions need not hold.
  2. Split it into modes. Write u_0 = \sum_n a_n X_n, each mode scaled so that \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L, and compute \int_0^L EI\,u_0\,X_n''''\,dx in two ways. By the mode equation EI\,X_n'''' = \rho S\,\omega_n^2X_n, with \omega_n = 2\pi f_n, and because different modes are perpendicular, it equals \rho SL\,\omega_n^2\,a_n. Integrating by parts four times instead, every term vanishes except the tip’s shear force times X_n(L). So \rho SL\,\omega_n^2\,a_n = F\,X_n(L): mode n is a spring of stiffness proportional to \omega_n^2, pushed with a force proportional to its value at the tip.
  3. Tip values. In the form of the fold above, \sigma_1 \approx 0.734, \sigma_2 \approx 1.018, and every mode has X_n(L) = \pm2 (a known property of clamped–free modes, easily confirmed numerically). So a_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2, and a_2/a_1 = 1/6.27^2 \approx 2.5\%.
  4. Forces on the bridge. In this model the tine is clamped right at the bridge, so the forces at the clamp are the forces on the bridge. With X_n''(0) = 2\beta_n^2 and X_n'''(0) = -2\sigma_n\beta_n^3, and a_n \propto 1/\beta_n^4, the second mode’s bending moment at the clamp is (\beta_1/\beta_2)^2 \approx 16\% of the first’s, and its shear force (\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(\beta_1/\beta_2) \approx 1.39 \times 0.40 \approx 55\%. These are two measures of the push, not a range of uncertainty; the demo uses the shear force.

A real tine is held a little behind the bridge and rests on it partway along, rather than being clamped at one point. Chapman found that the measured overtone ratios are “correlated with the subdivided lengths of the vibrating beam”, which is one reason they spread around the ideal 6.27 (D. M. F. Chapman, The sound of the African thumb piano (kalimba), Acoustics’08 Paris, 2008).

Fading: the overtone goes first

The click is gone within a fraction of a second, while the fundamental rings on. This part is observed, not derived: the model has no friction, so in it every mode rings forever. The acoustician D. M. F. Chapman measured a Hugh Tracey kalimba and found one dominant overtone at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, near the ideal 6.27, and found that the off-line overtones die away rapidly.5

5 D. M. F. Chapman, The sound of the African thumb piano (kalimba), Acoustics’08 Paris, paper 5aMUf1, Acoustical Society of America, 2008: measured on “the Hugh Tracey Pentatonic Kalimba”; “a single overtone (f1) whose frequency ratio f1/f0 is about 5.9-6.8”; “the anharmonic overtones die away rapidly”.

The demo’s sound is made this way. It adds the first five modes at the frequencies above, each starting with its strength in the push on the bridge; the fundamental fades over a couple of seconds and the overtones within a small fraction of that. Those fading times are illustrative, chosen to match Chapman’s observation. Tick the box and every mode fades as slowly as the fundamental: the same spectrum then clangs like a bell.

What you hear

A click, then a clean note. What remains after the click is the fundamental alone, a single partial, and a single partial has a clear pitch at its own frequency. The spectrum alone would clang, as a bell’s does; the fading decides. The player sets the loudness by how far the tip is pushed, since every a_n grows in proportion to it.

Half the length, two octaves up. The numbers \beta_nL do not depend on the length, so f_n = \kappa\,(\beta_nL)^2/(2\pi L^2): a tine’s frequency goes as one over its length squared. Half the length gives four times the frequency, two octaves, where a string rises only one. That is why, in the demo, the low tines are not twice as long as the tines an octave above them. It is also why a small change of length goes a long way when a tine is tuned: its length is measured from where it is held down on the bridge, and longer is lower, shorter higher.6

6 Kalimba Magic, on tuning tines: “Making tines longer (from the point where they are held down on the bridge) will lower their pitch, and making them shorter will make their pitch higher.”
