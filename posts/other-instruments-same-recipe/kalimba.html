A kalimba tine’s first overtone sits at about 6.3 times its fundamental, far off the whole numbers, and overtones off the whole numbers are what make a bell clang.1 So why does a kalimba sound like a clean note?

1 Wikipedia, Inharmonicity: bells and other instruments whose overtones are not whole-number multiples of the fundamental.

A kalimba sounds clean because its off-line overtone dies within a fraction of a second, and the fundamental rings on alone. Where the overtone sits follows from Newton’s law; how fast it fades is measured. Tap a tine; then tick the box, which keeps the overtones ringing, and tap again.

Tap a tine, or drag across several.

The tines are drawn to scale: a tine half as long sounds two octaves higher, as this page explains.

What you hear works as in post 1: the frequencies give the pitch, their sizes the loudness and timbre, and their fading the duration. The box and the room keep those frequencies (post 2).

How the sound gets out

The sound reaches the air through the clamp. There the tine passes the kalimba’s box a force, the shear force V(0, t) = -EI\,u_{xxx}(0, t), and a bending moment (a turning push), M(0, t) = EI\,u_{xx}(0, t), where u(x, t) is the tine’s height at distance x from the clamp and EI the tine’s bending stiffness, its resistance to bending (below); the box pushes the air. Acting at each instant, both keep every frequency and weight each mode by its force and moment at the clamp, which grow fast along the modes: in the force, the first overtone starts at about 55% of the fundamental.

The tine’s shapes

The tine’s overtones sit far off the whole-number lines. The tine obeys u_{tt} = -Au with A = \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4 and \kappa^2 = EI/\rho S, for density \rho and cross-section area S; L is its length. The base is clamped: it can neither move nor tilt, u = u_x = 0. The tip is free: no moment and no shear force act there, u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0.

Its modes are the shapes A only stretches, AX = \lambda X. Write \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, so that X'''' = \beta^4X. Four functions return to themselves after four derivatives: \cos\beta x and \sin\beta x, and the hyperbolic functions \cosh\beta x and \sinh\beta x, where \cosh y = (e^y + e^{-y})/2 and \sinh y = (e^y - e^{-y})/2. The four end conditions allow a solution other than zero exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1 . Since \cosh\beta L grows fast, \beta L sits near a zero of the cosine, (n - \tfrac12)\pi. The roots are \beta_nL = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548,\ 10.9955, \dots, and so \lambda_n = \kappa^2\beta_n^4, \qquad f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{\kappa\,\beta_n^2}{2\pi}, in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39.2 Roots exactly at (n - \tfrac12)\pi would give the whole numbers 1 : 9 : 25; the clamp and the free tip shift the first root most from (n - \tfrac12)\pi, to 1.8751 instead of \pi/2 \approx 1.571, and that breaks the pattern. For a plucked note this is the only step that creates frequencies.

2 Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapter 2: the clamped–free bar’s frequencies, in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39.

DetailsThe mode condition from the four end conditions a 2×2 determinant

The clamp, X(0) = X'(0) = 0, leaves two constants: \begin{aligned} X = {} & a\,(\cos\beta x - \cosh\beta x) \\ & + b\,(\sin\beta x - \sinh\beta x) . \end{aligned} With the shorthand c_1 = \cos\beta L, s_1 = \sin\beta L, c_2 = \cosh\beta L, s_2 = \sinh\beta L, and dropping the common factors \beta^2 and \beta^3, the free tip’s X''(L) = 0 and X'''(L) = 0 read \begin{gathered} -(c_1 + c_2)\,a - (s_1 + s_2)\,b = 0, \\ (s_1 - s_2)\,a - (c_1 + c_2)\,b = 0 . \end{gathered} Two equations pa + qb = 0 and ra + sb = 0 have a solution other than a = b = 0 exactly when the determinant ps - qr vanishes. Here \begin{aligned} & (c_1 + c_2)^2 + (s_1 + s_2)(s_1 - s_2) \\ & \quad = c_1^2 + s_1^2 + 2c_1c_2 + c_2^2 - s_2^2 \\ & \quad = 2 + 2c_1c_2 , \end{aligned} by c_1^2 + s_1^2 = 1 and c_2^2 - s_2^2 = 1. So a mode exists exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1. The modes are X_n = \cosh\beta_n x - \cos\beta_n x - \sigma_n(\sinh\beta_n x - \sin\beta_n x), with \sigma_n = (\sinh\beta_nL - \sin\beta_nL)/(\cosh\beta_nL + \cos\beta_nL) from the second equation.

Reader questionWould other ends give whole numbers?

Yes, for some. A bar held in place at both ends but free to pivot has u = u_{xx} = 0 at each end. Then \sin(n\pi x/L) satisfies all four conditions, four derivatives multiply it by (n\pi/L)^4, and the frequency ratios are 1 : 4 : 9 : 16, whole numbers. The clamp and the free tip are what move the roots, and the first root most.

In the panel, each stem is one f_n against the gray whole-number lines, its height the force the mode passes to the box after a pluck; tap a stem to see its shape.

Modes of one tine · tap a stem

Released from a thumb’s push, the tine moves as a sum of its shapes, with amounts a_n: u(x, t) = \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty} a_n \cos(2\pi f_n t)\,X_n(x) .

Where the equation comes from

A tine is under no tension; steel’s resistance to bending springs it back. Bending needs a turning effect at each cross-section; where that changes along the tine, a sideways force must balance it, and the imbalance of those forces accelerates each slice. The turning effect, the bending moment, is M = EI\,u_{xx}, where u_{xx} is the curvature, E steel’s Young’s modulus, how stiff the material is, and I the second moment of area, how strongly the cross-section’s shape resists bending. The sideways force, the shear force, is V = -M_x. For a slice, Newton’s law gives the equation above, the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation, with four derivatives where the string had two.3

3 Wikipedia, Euler–Bernoulli beam theory; N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2 (bars).

DetailsThe slice, step by step an integral over a cross-section

Bend the bar, and a middle line, the neutral axis, keeps its length. For small slopes the curvature is u_{xx}, and a fiber at height y above the neutral axis is stretched by the fraction -y\,u_{xx}, so by Hooke’s law it carries the force per unit area -E\,y\,u_{xx}. Over the cross-section these forces cancel, but their turning effects add up to \begin{gathered} M = -\int y(-Ey\,u_{xx})\,dA = EI\,u_{xx}, \\ I = \int y^2\,dA . \end{gathered} A thin slice’s rotational inertia is negligible, so the turning effects on it balance: V\,dx + dM = 0, that is, V = -M_x = -EI\,u_{xxx}. The shear forces on the two faces differ by V_x\,dx, which accelerates the slice’s mass \rho S\,dx: \rho S\,u_{tt} = V_x = -EI\,u_{xxxx}. A fourth-order equation needs two conditions at each end, which the clamp and the free tip supply. The model assumes small slopes, a uniform bar clamped at exactly one point, and no friction; it ignores the slices’ rotational inertia and shear deformation, which matter only for thick bars and high modes (Wikipedia, Euler–Bernoulli beam theory).

The pluck: a click

The first overtone starts strong in the force on the box: that is the click at the start of each note. In the tine’s displacement it is tiny: a_2 is 2.5% of a_1, because each mode gives way to the thumb in inverse proportion to its stiffness \lambda_n.

DetailsHow strongly a pluck at the tip starts each mode integration by parts
  1. The pluck shape. Before release, a push F at the tip holds the tine still. Inside, nothing pushes, so EI\,u_0'''' = 0 and u_0 is a cubic. The clamp, the moment-free tip and the tip height u_0(L) leave u_0 = u_0(L)\,x^2(3L - x)/2L^3, with u_0'''(L) = -3u_0(L)/L^3 \ne 0: the shear force F = 3EI\,u_0(L)/L^3 of the thumb. The start is smooth, as it must be: a shape with a corner has u_x jumping, so u_{xx} contains a spike and the bending energy \tfrac12 EI\int (u_{xx})^2 is infinite, which a finite push cannot store. The start fails the tip condition u_{xxx}(L) = 0, but only where the thumb pushed. That is allowed: in post 2’s weak sense of solution, a solution is tested only against smooth shapes \varphi that satisfy all four end conditions, through \langle u(t), \varphi\rangle, so the series needs only a start of finite bending energy with u_0 = u_0' = 0 at the clamp; the tip’s conditions need not hold.
  2. Split it into modes. Write u_0 = \sum_n a_n X_n, each mode scaled so that \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L, and compute \int_0^L EI\,u_0\,X_n''''\,dx in two ways. By the mode equation EI\,X_n'''' = \rho S\,\omega_n^2X_n, with \omega_n = 2\pi f_n, and because different modes are perpendicular, it equals \rho SL\,\omega_n^2\,a_n. Integrating by parts four times instead, every term vanishes except the tip’s shear force times X_n(L). So \rho SL\,\omega_n^2\,a_n = F\,X_n(L): mode n is a spring of stiffness proportional to \omega_n^2, pushed with a force proportional to its value at the tip.
  3. Tip values. In the form of the fold above, \sigma_1 \approx 0.734, \sigma_2 \approx 1.018, and every mode has X_n(L) = \pm2 (a known property of clamped–free modes, easily confirmed numerically). So a_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2, and a_2/a_1 = 1/6.27^2 \approx 2.5\%.
  4. Forces on the bridge. In this model the tine is clamped right at the bridge, so the forces at the clamp are the forces on the bridge. With X_n''(0) = 2\beta_n^2 and X_n'''(0) = -2\sigma_n\beta_n^3, and a_n \propto 1/\beta_n^4, the second mode’s bending moment at the clamp is (\beta_1/\beta_2)^2 \approx 16\% of the first’s, and its shear force (\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(\beta_1/\beta_2) \approx 1.39 \times 0.40 \approx 55\%. These are two measures of the push, not a range of uncertainty; the demo uses the shear force.

A real tine is held a little behind the bridge and rests on it partway along, rather than being clamped at one point. Chapman found that the measured overtone ratios are “correlated with the subdivided lengths of the vibrating beam”, which is one reason they spread around the ideal 6.27 (D. M. F. Chapman, The sound of the African thumb piano (kalimba), Acoustics’08 Paris, 2008).

The chain in one line

Put together, partial n reaches your ear with the size c_n = |H_1(f_n)\,V_n + H_2(f_n)\,M_n| \cdot |a_n| , where V_n = -EI\,X_n'''(0) and M_n = EI\,X_n''(0) are mode n’s force and moment at the clamp, and H_1, H_2 the box’s factors for each. Pitch traces back to the spectrum of A; timbre to the pluck, the clamp’s weights, H_1, H_2 and the off-line spacing; loudness to how far the tip is pushed; duration to the losses, which take the overtone first.

Fading: the overtone goes first

The click is gone within a fraction of a second, while the fundamental rings on. This is observed, not derived: in the frictionless model every mode rings forever. D. M. F. Chapman measured a Hugh Tracey kalimba and found one dominant overtone at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, near the ideal 6.27, and that the off-line overtones die away rapidly.4 The demo’s fading times are illustrative, chosen to match Chapman’s observation.

4 D. M. F. Chapman, The sound of the African thumb piano (kalimba), Acoustics’08 Paris, paper 5aMUf1, Acoustical Society of America, 2008: measured on “the Hugh Tracey Pentatonic Kalimba”; “a single overtone (f1) whose frequency ratio f1/f0 is about 5.9-6.8”; “the anharmonic overtones die away rapidly”.

DetailsHalf the length, two octaves up

A tine’s frequency goes as one over its length squared. Squeeze a shape into half the length and its fourth derivative grows 2^4 = 16 times, so \lambda grows 16 times and f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi grows 4 times: two octaves, where a string, with two derivatives, rises one. In the formula, the \beta_nL do not depend on the length, so f_n = \kappa\,(\beta_nL)^2/(2\pi L^2). A tine is tuned by moving the point where it is held down on the bridge: longer is lower, shorter higher, and a small change goes a long way (Kalimba Magic, on tuning tines: “Making tines longer (from the point where they are held down on the bridge) will lower their pitch”).
