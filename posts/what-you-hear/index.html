What in a sound makes a clear pitch, so that a plucked string gives a note you could hum and a struck drum a thud, and what do we hear as a sound’s loudness, timbre and duration?

In one sentence. A clear pitch means the partials sit at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the pitch range, the largest that fits; the sound then nearly repeats at that rate, which we hear even if its own partial is missing.

§4 names the exceptions; a piano’s partials very nearly do this and a drum’s don’t.

1 · A string and a drum

Pluck a string and you get a note you could hum. Strike a drum and you get a thud. Both are a stretched piece of material, set shaking. So why do they sound so different?

Call this the pitch ruler. Its gray lines mark whole-number multiples, 1×, 2×, 3×, …, of one base frequency, here the lowest in each sound. Each blue stem is one frequency in the sound; taller means it starts stronger, for an idealized pluck and strike. Both objects are idealized: a perfectly flexible string at 196 Hz, plucked a fifth of the way along (so 5× is missing), and an ideal round drumhead whose lowest frequency is 140 Hz, struck about a third of the way out from its center. Their frequencies are computed for these ideal objects (post 4 works them out); the fading times are modelling choices.

The stems tell the two apart: the string’s sit on the lines, at exactly 1, 2, 3, … times the lowest; the drum’s sit at 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, … times the lowest, and no frequency in the range we hear as pitch fits them all closely (§3). This post explains, without differential equations, why that decides between a note and a thud.

2 · Four aspects of a sound

Sound. The air pressure p(t) at a listener, as a function of time.

The simplest sound is a pure tone, the sound of a tuning fork: a pressure swing a\cos(2\pi f t + \varphi) about the average air pressure, with frequency f in hertz (cycles per second), size a, and phase \varphi, which says where in its cycle it starts.

Partial. One sinusoidal component of a sound, with its own frequency, size and fading.

The instrument’s own part of the sound, when it is left to ring after a pluck or a strike, is to a good approximation a sum of partials, each fading at its own rate: p(t) = \sum_k a_k\, e^{-t/\tau_k} \cos(2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k), with frequencies f_k, starting sizes a_k, phases \varphi_k and fading times \tau_k (every \tau_k seconds, a partial shrinks by a factor of e \approx 2.7). Each partial comes from one of the instrument’s modes, a shape the object swings in at one frequency, such as a string swinging as one arc, or as two halves in opposite directions; the body and the room pass them on, adding some ringing of their own. Post 3 explains this form; what it leaves out, such as breath, is noise.

Harmonic. A partial at a whole-number multiple n \cdot f of a base frequency f: the n-th harmonic. (For a harmonic sound, the first overtone is the second harmonic.)

An overtone is any partial above the lowest; a harmonic sound is one whose partials are all harmonics of one base.

We hear four aspects, all perceptions measured by asking listeners: what follows is about hearing, not mathematics (Moore 2012; Plomp 1976).

Pitch

Pitch. A perception: how high or low a sound is, measured as the frequency of the pure tone that listeners match it to.

This is the standard definition (Moore 2012, chapter 6; ASA terminology, 11.01). Pitch that can carry a melody is heard for repetition rates (§3) from about 30 Hz (Pressnitzer, Patterson and Krumbholz 2001), and for pure tones up to 4 or 5 kHz (the traditional limit; Oxenham and colleagues 2011): the range we hear as pitch.

The second sound has ten partials, at 1, 2, …, 10 times 220 Hz, with sizes 1, 1/2, …, 1/10. It sounds richer than the first and just as high, so its pitch is 220 Hz. The third is an octave higher.

Loudness

Loudness. A perception: how strong a sound seems. Mainly set by the sound pressure level; it also depends on frequency (equal-loudness contours, ISO 226) and on the spread of partials, and it grows roughly as a power of intensity (about twice as loud per 10 dB; Stevens).

In short, 10 dB more is ten times the power and sounds about twice as loud (Stevens 1957), and at the same level a low tone sounds quieter than a mid-range one (ISO 226); the fold gives the numbers.

DetailsLoudness: decibels, equal-loudness contours and the power law

The sound pressure level is 20\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{rms}}/20\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}) decibels (dB), with p_{\mathrm{rms}} the root-mean-square pressure, the square root of the average squared pressure swing; the intensity, the power carried through each square metre, grows as p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2, so 10 dB more is ten times the intensity. By the equal-loudness contours of ISO 226, which give the level at which pure tones of each frequency sound equally loud to young listeners, a 100 Hz tone needs about 79 dB to match a 1 kHz tone at 60 dB. At equal intensity, partials spread beyond a certain width in frequency sound louder than partials bunched together (Zwicker, Flottorp and Stevens 1957); and loudness grows roughly as the 0.3 power of intensity, so 10 dB more sounds about twice as loud, since 10^{0.3} \approx 2 (Stevens 1957).

Timbre

Timbre. What distinguishes sounds that agree in pitch (if they have one), loudness and duration: which partials are present, their relative sizes, how far they sit from whole-number multiples, how the sound starts and fades (attack and decay), noise and transients that are not modes (bow noise, breath, hammer thump), and fixed resonances that stay put whatever note is played (formants: a violin’s body, a clarinet’s tone-hole cutoff).

Mostly timbre is the partials’ sizes and how they change, the start especially (Wikipedia, Timbre; Moore 2012, chapter 8); the fold gives the evidence and the formants.

DetailsTimbre: the evidence, and the formants

The standard definition is shorter: what tells apart two sounds of the same loudness and pitch (ASA terminology, 12.09). Mostly it is the partials’ sizes and how they change (Wikipedia, Timbre; Moore 2012, chapter 8); removing the onsets of recorded instrument tones cut listeners’ accuracy at naming the instruments by about 15 percentage points (Saldanha and Corso 1964, summarized by Siedenburg 2019). Listeners also hear a finger hitting a piano key and the key hitting bottom (Goebl, Bresin and Fujinaga 2014). A violin’s body favors certain bands of frequency whatever the note, as a voice’s formants do (Schleske); above a cutoff frequency, roughly 1 to 1.5 kHz on a clarinet, sound waves travel on past the open tone holes instead of being turned back by them, and that cutoff depends little on the fingering (Moers and Kergomard 2011; Wolfe, clarinet acoustics).

Four sounds with the same pitch, 220 Hz. The first three are steady and have the same power, which makes them roughly, not exactly, equally loud: one partial; twelve harmonics with sizes 1, 1/2, 1/3, …; and the odd ones of those, which sounds hollow, like a clarinet. The fourth has the second's partials but starts suddenly and fades, the higher partials faster, like a pluck.

Duration

Duration. How long a note sounds. For a ringing instrument: its fading times and when the player damps it (key release, mute, a hand or leg on the head). For a bowed or blown instrument: as long as the player keeps going.

How a note fades is part of its timbre; how long it lasts is its duration. A bass drummer stops a note with a leg or a towel on the head (Aleo, Interlochen 2023).

A string at 196 Hz. Plucked, it fades on its own unless damped; the damped note has the same partials, all made to die within about a tenth of a second. Bowed, it is a steady tone (partials 1, 1/2, 1/3, … of a repeating motion, §5) that lasts as long as the bow moves.

From the partials to the four aspects. For an instrument left to ring, the four aspects are heard from three lists, the frequencies f_k, the sizes a_k and the fading times \tau_k, through the ear’s own weighting: pitch mainly from where the f_k sit; loudness from the sizes, weighted by frequency; timbre from all three lists, especially early on, plus noise that is not a mode; duration from the fading and from when the player stops or damps the note. Bowed and blown instruments are different (§5).

The rest of this post asks where the f_k must sit for a clear pitch.

3 · Why whole numbers repeat

A sum of steady partials repeats exactly when all its frequencies are whole-number multiples of one frequency, and then at the largest such frequency. A signal p is periodic with period P > 0 if p(t+P) = p(t) for every t.

Proposition (repetition and whole numbers). Let p(t) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k\cos(2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k), with distinct frequencies f_k > 0 and sizes a_k \ne 0. A time P > 0 is a period of p exactly when every f_k is a whole-number multiple of 1/P. So p is periodic exactly when every ratio f_k/f_1 is a fraction, and then its shortest period is 1/g, where g is the largest frequency of which every f_k is a whole-number multiple.

In words: steady partials add up to a sound that repeats only if they all sit on the lines of one pitch ruler, and it repeats at the base of the coarsest such ruler.

The smallest case is two pure tones with frequency ratio f_2/f_1 = j/k, a fraction in lowest terms. After P = k/f_1 seconds the lower tone has completed f_1P = k whole cycles and the upper one f_2P = j, so the sum begins again: it repeats every k cycles of the lower tone, at the rate g = f_1/k. At 1.59 : 1, which is 159/100, that is every 100 cycles: 2.2 times a second for a lower tone at 220 Hz, far below the pitch range. That the sum repeats only for fractions needs one more fact, that sinusoids of different frequencies cannot cancel one another (the fold).

ProofThe proposition: sinusoids of different frequencies are independent, so a period must fit every partial uses integrals of products of cosines, and divisibility

Lemma. Let 0 < g_1 < \dots < g_m, and suppose that c + \sum_{j=1}^m \big(\alpha_j\cos(2\pi g_j t) + \beta_j\sin(2\pi g_j t)\big) = 0 for every t. Then c = 0 and every \alpha_j and \beta_j is 0.

Proof of the lemma. For a function h, write \langle h\rangle = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T} h(t)\,dt for its long-run average, when the limit exists. For \gamma \ne 0, \int_{-T}^{T}\cos(2\pi\gamma t)\,dt = \sin(2\pi\gamma T)/(\pi\gamma) stays bounded as T grows, so \langle\cos(2\pi\gamma t)\rangle = 0; and \langle 1\rangle = 1. The product formulas 2\cos x\cos y = \cos(x-y) + \cos(x+y) and 2\sin x\sin y = \cos(x-y) - \cos(x+y) then give, for a, b > 0, \langle\cos(2\pi a t)\cos(2\pi b t)\rangle = \langle\sin(2\pi a t)\sin(2\pi b t)\rangle = \tfrac12 if a = b and 0 if a \ne b. A product of a sine and a cosine is an odd function, so its integral over [-T, T] is 0, and so is its average. Multiply the assumed identity by \cos(2\pi g_j t) and average: every term vanishes except \alpha_j/2, by the averages just computed, so \alpha_j = 0. Multiplying by \sin(2\pi g_j t) gives \beta_j = 0 in the same way. What remains is c = 0.

Proof of the proposition. If every f_kP is a whole number, each cosine completes whole cycles in time P, so p(t + P) = p(t). Conversely, suppose p(t+P) = p(t) for every t, and write \theta_k = 2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k and \delta_k = 2\pi f_k P. By the identity \cos(\theta + \delta) - \cos\theta = -2\sin(\delta/2)\sin(\theta + \delta/2), 0 = p(t+P) - p(t) = \sum_{k=1}^n -2a_k\sin(\pi f_kP)\,\sin\!\big(2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k + \pi f_kP\big). Expanding each term into \alpha_k\cos(2\pi f_k t) + \beta_k\sin(2\pi f_k t) gives \alpha_k^2 + \beta_k^2 = 4a_k^2\sin^2(\pi f_kP). The lemma, with c = 0, makes every \alpha_k and \beta_k zero, so a_k\sin(\pi f_kP) = 0; since a_k \ne 0 by hypothesis, \sin(\pi f_k P) = 0, which means f_kP is a whole number. This proves the first sentence.

The shortest period. If p has a period P, the first sentence makes every f_kP a whole number, so f_k/f_1 = (f_kP)/(f_1P) is a fraction. Conversely, suppose every f_k/f_1 is a fraction. Over a common denominator N, f_k = n_k h with h = f_1/N and whole numbers n_k. Let d be the greatest common divisor of n_1, \dots, n_n and g = dh; then f_k = m_kg with whole numbers m_k = n_k/d whose greatest common divisor is 1. By the first sentence, P is a period exactly when every m_k\,gP is a whole number. If gP is a whole number, so is every m_k\,gP. Conversely, Bézout’s identity (whole numbers whose greatest common divisor is 1 have a combination with whole-number coefficients equal to 1) gives whole numbers u_k with \sum_k u_km_k = 1, so gP = \sum_k u_k\,(m_k\,gP) is a whole number. So the periods are exactly the whole-number multiples of 1/g, and the shortest is 1/g. Finally, if every f_k is a whole-number multiple of some g', then 1/g' is a period, hence a multiple of 1/g, so g' \le g: g is the largest such frequency.

Check on the examples. Partials at 440, 660 and 880 Hz have h = 220, n_k = 2, 3, 4, d = 1, so g = 220 Hz although no partial sits there. Tones at 100 and 159 Hz have g = 1 Hz: the sum repeats once a second.

The repeating rate gets a name.

Repetition rate. For a sound whose partials are steady and exact whole-number multiples of a common frequency: 1 over its shortest period, which is the largest such common frequency. For partials that fade, or sit only near multiples: the largest frequency in the range we hear as pitch that they fit within a stated tolerance.

The second half covers real instruments. A sound that fades cannot repeat exactly, but it can nearly: if every partial sits on a multiple of g and fades with the same time \tau, each period is the one before shrunk by the factor e^{-1/(g\tau)}, by 0.45% at g = 220 Hz and \tau = 1 s. How near the multiples is near enough is a fact about hearing: a low partial mistuned by more than about 1 to 3%, depending on the listener, stands out as a separate tone (Moore, Glasberg and Peters 1986). Within 2%, the middle of that range, the lowest strong partials of §1’s drum fit no frequency above about 20 Hz, below the pitch range; the string’s fit 196 Hz exactly.

PreciselyHow the drum fails to fit, at different tolerances

Take the partials of the drum in §1 up to 3.2 times the lowest that start with at least a tenth of the strongest one’s size: 1, 1.593, 2.295, 2.653 and 3.155 times 140 Hz. (Its partials at 2.136 and 2.917 start with only about 2% of the strongest’s size, because the strike point or the listening point sits near a still line of their modes.) Call a frequency g a fit within a tolerance \epsilon if each partial lies within the fraction \epsilon of a whole-number multiple ng with n \ge 1. A computer search over g gives the largest fit; adding the drum’s higher partials could only lower it.

Why a fading sound cannot repeat exactly. If p(t+P) = p(t) for every t, then p(t) = p(t + mP) for every whole number m, and for a sound that dies away this tends to 0 as m grows, so p is zero. A piano’s partials, slightly sharp of the lines, also nearly repeat.

Within 3%, a base of about 46 Hz fits as its 3rd, 5th, 7th, 8th and 10th multiples, each off by up to 3%, with nothing at the base or at its 2nd or 4th multiple. The 3% figure is generous. It comes from a different measurement by the same authors: in an earlier study, a single mistuned low partial of an otherwise exact harmonic sound kept counting fully toward its pitch up to about 3% (Moore, Glasberg and Peters 1985, as summarized by Darwin, Buffa and Smits 1990), whereas the 1 to 3% of §3 is where a partial starts to be heard out on its own (their 1986 study), and here every partial is off at once. What the ear makes of such a sound is a question about hearing; in the demo of §1, it is a thud.

4 · The missing fundamental

For a single tone, the ear hears the repetition rate as the pitch, even when no partial sits at it. Play the note, then the same note without its 220 Hz partial, and watch the wave.

Mix your own

The wave over three cycles of 220 Hz; gray ticks mark each 1/220 of a second. Stems: how strong each partial is, at 1, 2, 3, … times 220 Hz, on the pitch ruler. The readout gives the repetition rate, the largest frequency of which every partial present is a whole-number multiple.

The wave changes shape but still repeats every 1/220 of a second, since 440, 660, 880, … Hz are all multiples of 220 Hz, and the pitch stays. Hearing the pitch without its fundamental is the missing-fundamental effect; it keeps a deep voice’s pitch over a telephone that carries little below about 300 Hz (Wikipedia, Missing fundamental). Keep only 2×, 4×, 6× and 8× in “Mix your own”, and the pitch jumps an octave.

This finding about hearing (Moore 2012, chapter 6) relates the two notions:

Pitch and repetition rate. For a single tone whose prominent partials lie near a harmonic series in the pitch range, pitch ≈ that series’ base, even when its own partial is missing.

Beats

Two close partials fit no series in the pitch range; the ear hears one tone that swells and fades. For two pure tones of equal size, the sum-to-product formula gives \cos(2\pi f_1 t) + \cos(2\pi f_2 t) = 2\cos\big(\pi (f_2 - f_1) t\big)\,\cos\big(\pi (f_1 + f_2) t\big), a tone at the average frequency whose size swells and fades |f_2 - f_1| times a second: beats. Tones at 220 and 222 Hz repeat together exactly twice a second, far below the pitch range; you hear a 221 Hz tone wobbling twice a second. Faster beats sound rough, and tones far enough apart sound as two smooth tones (Plomp and Levelt 1965).

The sum of two equal pure tones over one second. Its outline swells and shrinks as many times a second as the two frequencies differ: 2, 8 and 25 times; at 110 Hz apart the swells come too fast to see here, and you hear two smooth tones.

Exceptions to the pitch rule

The rule is about a single tone. Three kinds of sound break it.

Chords. The ear sorts the partials into several series and hears several pitches. C4 and G4 tuned to an exact 3 : 2 have all their partials on multiples of 130.8 Hz (C3), so together they repeat 130.8 times a second; yet you hear two notes, since the ear hears two sets of harmonics with different bases as two sounds (Bregman 1990).

Each note has eight harmonics, with sizes 1, 1/2, …, 1/8: C4 at 261.6 Hz, G4 at exactly 3/2 of that, 392.4 Hz. Together they repeat 130.8 times a second, the rate of the last sound, which is not what the pair sounds like.

Bells and kettledrums. A harmonic subset sets the pitch, and the ear may choose an octave above the base. A bell is heard roughly an octave below its main partial, the nominal, often with no partial at the pitch heard (Hibbert); a kettledrum is named an octave above the base its main partials fit (HyperPhysics; kettledrum page). The fold gives the partials.

Strongly stretched partials. Partials running sharp of the lines, as on a stiff piano string, pull the pitch up (Jaatinen and Pätynen 2022); beyond the tolerance of §3, a partial is heard out on its own (Moore, Glasberg and Peters 1986).

DetailsThe bell’s and the kettledrum’s partials

In short. A bell’s strike note, the pitch it is heard at, is set mainly by three partials at nearly 2, 3 and 4 times its frequency, often with no partial exactly at it; its other main partials include the hum, an octave below, and the tierce, a minor third above, off the series. Without the tierce, the main partials fit the hum as base, yet the bell is heard an octave higher (Hibbert; Wikipedia, Strike tone). A kettledrum’s air pulls one set of its modes near 2, 3, 4 and 5 times a base, yet players name the note an octave higher, at the lowest of the set, the drum’s principal tone (HyperPhysics; Vienna’s Institute of Musical Acoustics).

The bell. In a well-tuned church bell the main partials, the hum, prime, tierce, quint and nominal, sit near 0.5, 1, 1.2, 1.5 and 2 times the prime, and two more, the superquint and the octave nominal, near 3 and 4 times (Hibbert, upper partials); the strike note is close to the prime (Wikipedia, Strike tone). The nominal, superquint and octave nominal, nearly in the ratios 2 : 3 : 4, are heard as harmonics of a missing fundamental, the strike note, which lies roughly an octave below the nominal (Hibbert, strike note). It can differ from half the nominal by about a quarter of a semitone, and often no partial sits exactly at it, a puzzle Rayleigh noted in 1890; which partials set the note also depends on the bell’s size (Hibbert). Measured from the hum, the main partials sit at 1, 2, 2.4, 3, 4, 6 and 8: whole numbers, except the tierce.

The kettledrum. A kettledrum measured by Benade, tuned to C3, had its principal tone at 130.8 Hz and partials at about 1.50, 2.00, 2.49 and 2.98 times it, close to 3, 4, 5 and 6 times half of it, together with others off this pattern (HyperPhysics, tabulating Benade). On C2, 65.4 Hz, that set forms a near-harmonic series, and HyperPhysics notes that the missing-fundamental effect might then give the pitch C2; the drum is named for its principal tone, C3. Vienna’s Institute of Musical Acoustics names the principal tone (“Grundton”) as the mode with one still line through the center, and notes that the drum’s many partials off whole-number ratios make its exact pitch hard to pin down.

5 · Four routes to a clear pitch

Instruments reach a clear pitch by four routes. A mode, in plain words, is a shape the object swings in at one frequency; each gives one partial.

  1. Modes on the whole numbers by nature: strings and air columns. The n-th mode fits n half-waves (the picture), and all waves travel at one speed, so an ideal string swings at exactly n times its lowest frequency; so does air in a flute (a clarinet: odd multiples). See the violin, clarinet and piano pages.
  2. Modes moved there by design. The kettledrum’s air loading (drum page), the marimba bar’s undercut, the tabla’s loaded patch, and a bell’s profile, which puts all its principal partials but the minor-third tierce on whole-number multiples of one base.
  3. Off-line partials suppressed. Weak or quick to die, as on a kalimba (kalimba page) or a glockenspiel, or never started, as where a timpanist strikes (a strike near a mode’s still line doesn’t start that mode).
  4. A periodic drive: bow and breath. A motion that repeats has its partials at exact whole-number multiples, whatever the object’s own modes; the resonator’s modes shape which partials are strong (harmonica page).
DetailsThe four routes, with their sources

  1. Modes on the whole numbers by nature: strings and air columns. A string’s n-th mode fits n half-waves between its ends (the picture), and every wave travels along the string at the same speed, so on an ideal string the n-th mode swings at exactly n times the lowest frequency. A flute’s air column, open at both ends, resonates at every whole-number multiple, and a clarinet’s, closed at the reed, at the odd ones (Wolfe). Guitars and harps take this route, and the piano nearly, since stiffness pushes its partials slightly sharp; bowed strings and winds start here, and route 4 makes their partials exact. See the violin, clarinet and piano pages.

  2. Modes moved there by design. A kettledrum’s air slows its coarse shapes most, pulling one set of modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5 (drum page). A marimba bar’s arch, cut underneath, moves its first overtone from 2.76 times the lowest, for a uniform bar, to 4 times; a xylophone’s shallower cut, to 3 times (HyperPhysics; Scavone). A patch of paste loading the middle of a tabla’s head brings its modes near whole-number ratios (Raman and Kumar 1920). Founders shape and tune a bell so that most of its principal partials sit on multiples of half the strike note, the minor-third tierce apart (series index).

  3. Off-line partials suppressed. Some are weak or die fast: a kalimba tine’s overtones, at 6.27 and 17.55 times its lowest, die within a fraction of a second (Chapman 2008; kalimba page), and a struck glockenspiel bar’s modes all die out quickly except the lowest (Scavone). Others are not started: a strike at a still point of a mode does not set that mode going, and timpanists strike between halfway and three-quarters of the way out, near the still lines of several modes that do not fit the note (the playing spot: HyperPhysics; the still lines: drum page).

  4. A periodic drive: bow and breath. A bow or a breath keeps feeding the motion, which settles into a single repeating cycle, so its partials sit at exact whole-number multiples, whatever the object’s own modes (Wolfe, How harmonic are harmonics?; §3 proves this for a finite sum): a bow’s stick and slip drive all of a string’s resonances at exactly harmonic ratios, and a harmonica reed, whose own overtones are off the lines, chops the air into identical puffs (harmonica page). The resonator’s modes shape which partials are strong, and the player sets loudness and duration.

6 · Dynamics change timbre too

A harder blow sounds louder and brighter: a piano hammer’s felt gets harder the more it is squashed, so a fast hammer stays on the string for a shorter time and starts more high partials (Askenfelt and Jansson 1990; Russell 1997); with a felt that stayed equally soft, a loud note would be a soft note turned up. Post 2 models the felt, and post 4’s piano page takes it up.

Middle C on a model piano string. The hard blow has stronger high partials and an average level 10 dB higher; the third button plays the soft blow 10 dB louder, at the hard blow's average level, and it still sounds duller. How fast the partials fall off with number is an illustrative choice, not computed from a model of the felt.

What we found

A clear pitch is a matter of where the partials sit: at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the pitch range, so the sound nearly repeats at the largest such rate, which the ear hears as the pitch of a single tone, chords, bells, kettledrums and stretched partials aside. A string’s partials sit on the lines and a piano’s very nearly; an ideal drum’s fit nothing in the pitch range.
