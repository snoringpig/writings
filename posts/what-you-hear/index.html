A sound has a clear pitch when its partials, the pure tones it is made of, sit at or very near whole-number multiples of one frequency. A plucked string’s partials do, so it gives a note you could hum; a struck drum’s don’t, so it gives a thud.

The pitch ruler: gray lines mark 1×, 2×, 3×, … the lowest frequency of each sound, and each blue stem is one partial, taller if it starts stronger. An ideal string at 196 Hz, plucked a fifth of the way along (so 5× is missing), and an ideal round drumhead at 140 Hz, struck a third of the way out. Post 2 computes the string's frequencies and post 3 the drum's; the fading times are modelling choices.

The reason: partials on the multiples of one frequency add up to a sound that repeats at that rate, and the ear hears the repetition rate as the pitch.

Four aspects of a sound

We hear four things in a sound: pitch, loudness, timbre and duration. They are perceptions, measured by asking listeners,1 and each comes from different numbers in one formula for the sound.

1 B. C. J. Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., Emerald, Bingley, 2012 (reissued by Brill, Leiden, 2013), chapters 4, 6 and 8 (loudness, pitch, timbre); R. Plomp, Aspects of Tone Sensation: A Psychophysical Study, Academic Press, 1976.

Sound is the air pressure p(t) at your ear at time t, measured from the average air pressure. An instrument plucked or struck and then left to ring sounds, to a good approximation, p(t) = \sum_n c_n e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n). Term n is a partial: a pure tone, like a tuning fork’s, with frequency f_n in hertz (cycles per second), numbered from the lowest, starting size c_n and phase \varphi_n, the point in its cycle where it starts. It shrinks by a factor of e \approx 2.7 every \tau_n seconds, its fading time. Each partial comes from one of the instrument’s modes, a shape the object swings in at one frequency. A bowed or blown note is the same sum without the fading, for as long as the player keeps going (route 4 below).

The four aspects come from these numbers:

  • Pitch, how high or low the sound is, comes from the f_n: the pitch is the largest frequency of which they are all (nearly) whole-number multiples; often f_1, but not when the common frequency is missing (below).
  • Loudness, how strong it seems, comes from the overall size of the c_n.
  • Timbre, what tells apart two sounds of the same pitch, loudness and length,2 comes from three things:
    • the ratios c_n/c_1 (bright versus mellow; missing partials);
    • how far the f_n sit from whole-number multiples (smooth, then shimmer, then clang);
    • how each partial’s size changes over time, especially at the start.3
  • Duration comes from the \tau_n, and from the player’s damping.

2 Adapted from the ANSI/ASA definition of timbre (terminology 12.09), which names loudness and pitch, as quoted by Wikipedia, Timbre.

3 Wikipedia, Timbre; Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., 2012, chapter 8.

Pitch

Pitch is measured as the frequency of the pure tone that listeners match a sound to.4 Pitch that can carry a melody is heard from about 30 Hz5 up to about 4 or 5 kHz for pure tones;6 call this the range we hear as pitch. The partials at 1, 2, 3, … times a frequency are its harmonics.

6 A. J. Oxenham, C. Micheyl, M. V. Keebler, A. Loper and S. Santurette, Pitch perception beyond the traditional existence region of pitch, PNAS 108(18) (2011), 7629–7634.

Loudness

Loudness is set mainly by the sound pressure level: 10 dB more is ten times the power and sounds about twice as loud.7 At the same level, a low tone sounds quieter than a mid-range one.8

7 S. S. Stevens, “On the psychophysical law”, Psychological Review 64 (1957), 153–181; Wikipedia, Sone (twice as loud per 10 dB) and Stevens’s power law.

8 ISO 226:2023, Acoustics — Normal equal-loudness-level contours; HyperPhysics, Equal loudness curves.

DetailsDecibels, equal-loudness contours and the power law

The sound pressure level is 20\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{rms}}/20\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}) decibels (dB), where p_{\mathrm{rms}}, the root-mean-square pressure, is the square root of the long-run average of p(t)^2. For steady partials, p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 = \tfrac12\sum_n c_n^2, by the averages in the proof below. The intensity, the power carried through each square metre, grows as p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2, so 10 dB more is ten times the intensity. By the equal-loudness contours of ISO 226, which give the level at which pure tones of each frequency sound equally loud to young listeners, a 100 Hz tone needs about 79 dB to match a 1 kHz tone at 60 dB (ISO 226:2023, Acoustics — Normal equal-loudness-level contours). At equal intensity, partials spread beyond a certain width in frequency sound louder than partials bunched together (E. Zwicker, G. Flottorp and S. S. Stevens, “Critical band width in loudness summation”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 29 (1957), 548–557). Loudness grows roughly as the 0.3 power of intensity, so 10 dB more sounds about twice as loud, since 10^{0.3} \approx 2 (S. S. Stevens, “On the psychophysical law”, Psychological Review 64 (1957), 153–181; Wikipedia, Stevens’s power law).

Timbre

Timbre also depends on two things the formula leaves out: noise and transients that are not modes, such as bow noise, breath and a hammer’s thump; and fixed resonances that favor the same bands of frequency whatever note is played, such as a violin’s body or a clarinet’s tone-hole cutoff (formants).9

9 M. Schleske, Introduction to violin acoustics: sound, on the violin body’s fixed resonances; E. Moers and J. Kergomard, “On the cutoff frequency of clarinet-like instruments. Geometrical versus acoustical regularity”, Acta Acustica united with Acustica 97 (2011), 984–996 (arXiv:1101.4742), and J. Wolfe and colleagues, Clarinet acoustics: F3, UNSW, on the clarinet’s tone-hole cutoff.

Removing the onsets of recorded instrument tones cut listeners’ accuracy at naming the instruments by about 15 percentage points (E. Saldanha and J. F. Siedenburg, Specifying the perceptual relevance of onset transients for musical instrument identification, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 145 (2019), 1078–1087). Listeners also hear a finger hitting a piano key and the key hitting bottom (W. Goebl, R. Bresin and I. Fujinaga, Perception of touch quality in piano tones, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 136 (2014), 2839–2850). A violin’s body favors certain bands of frequency whatever the note, as a voice’s formants do (M. Schleske, Introduction to violin acoustics: sound). Above a cutoff frequency, roughly 1 to 1.5 kHz on a clarinet, sound waves travel on past the open tone holes instead of being turned back by them, and that cutoff depends little on the fingering (E. Moers and J. Kergomard, “On the cutoff frequency of clarinet-like instruments. Geometrical versus acoustical regularity”, Acta Acustica united with Acustica 97 (2011), 984–996, arXiv:1101.4742; J. Wolfe and colleagues, Clarinet acoustics: F3, UNSW).

Dynamics change timbre too. A harder piano blow is brighter as well as louder: the hammer’s felt gets harder the more it is squashed, so a fast hammer stays on the string for a shorter time and starts more high partials.10 With a felt that stayed equally soft, a loud note would be a soft note turned up.11

Duration

A ringing note lasts as long as its fading times allow, unless the player damps it: a key released, a mute, a hand on a drumhead, or a bass drummer’s leg or towel.12

12 “How to play the bass drum properly”, with Keith Aleo, Interlochen Center for the Arts, 3 April 2023.

Why whole numbers repeat

Steady partials add up to a sound that repeats exactly when their frequencies are all whole-number multiples of one frequency. Take two pure tones at 220 and 330 Hz, in the ratio 3 : 2: after two cycles of the lower tone the upper has completed three, so the sum starts again, 110 times a second. At the ratio 1.59 : 1, which is 159/100, the sum starts again only after 100 cycles of the lower tone, 2.2 times a second.

A signal p is periodic with period P > 0 if p(t+P) = p(t) for every t. The repetition rate of a periodic sound is 1 over its shortest period.

Precisely. Let p(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} c_n\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n), with distinct frequencies f_n > 0 and sizes c_n \ne 0. A time P > 0 is a period of p exactly when every f_nP is a whole number. So p is periodic exactly when every ratio f_n/f_1 is a fraction, and then its shortest period is 1/g, where g is the largest frequency of which every f_n is a whole-number multiple; its repetition rate is g.

ProofSinusoids of different frequencies are independent, so a period must fit every partial integrals of products of sines and cosines, and divisibility

Lemma. Let 0 < g_1 < \dots < g_m, and suppose that \sum_{j=1}^m \big(\alpha_j\cos(2\pi g_j t) + \beta_j\sin(2\pi g_j t)\big) = 0 for every t. Then every \alpha_j and \beta_j is 0.

Proof of the lemma. For a function w, write \langle w\rangle = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T} w(t)\,dt for its long-run average, when the limit exists. For \gamma \ne 0, \int_{-T}^{T}\cos(2\pi\gamma t)\,dt = \sin(2\pi\gamma T)/(\pi\gamma) stays bounded as T grows, so \langle\cos(2\pi\gamma t)\rangle = 0; and \langle 1\rangle = 1. The product formulas 2\cos x\cos y = \cos(x-y) + \cos(x+y) and 2\sin x\sin y = \cos(x-y) - \cos(x+y) then give, for a, b > 0, \langle\cos(2\pi a t)\cos(2\pi b t)\rangle = \langle\sin(2\pi a t)\sin(2\pi b t)\rangle = \tfrac12 if a = b and 0 if a \ne b. A product of a sine and a cosine is an odd function, so its integral over [-T, T] is 0, and so is its average. Multiply the assumed identity by \cos(2\pi g_j t) and average: every term vanishes except \alpha_j/2, by the averages just computed, so \alpha_j = 0. Multiplying by \sin(2\pi g_j t) gives \beta_j = 0 in the same way.

Proof of the statement. If every f_nP is a whole number, each sine completes whole cycles in time P, so p(t + P) = p(t). Conversely, suppose p(t+P) = p(t) for every t. By the identity \sin(\theta + \delta) - \sin\theta = 2\sin(\delta/2)\cos(\theta + \delta/2), with \theta = 2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n and \delta = 2\pi f_n P, 0 = p(t+P) - p(t) = \sum_{n=1}^N 2c_n\sin(\pi f_nP)\,\cos\!\big(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n + \pi f_nP\big). Expanding each term into \alpha_n\cos(2\pi f_n t) + \beta_n\sin(2\pi f_n t) gives \alpha_n^2 + \beta_n^2 = 4c_n^2\sin^2(\pi f_nP). The lemma makes every \alpha_n and \beta_n zero, so c_n\sin(\pi f_nP) = 0; since c_n \ne 0 by hypothesis, \sin(\pi f_n P) = 0, which means f_nP is a whole number. This proves the first sentence.

The shortest period. If p has a period P, the first sentence makes every f_nP a whole number, so f_n/f_1 = (f_nP)/(f_1P) is a fraction. Conversely, suppose every f_n/f_1 is a fraction. Over a common denominator D, f_n = q_n h with h = f_1/D and whole numbers q_n. Let d be the greatest common divisor of q_1, \dots, q_N and g = dh; then f_n = m_ng with whole numbers m_n = q_n/d whose greatest common divisor is 1. By the first sentence, P is a period exactly when every m_n\,gP is a whole number. If gP is a whole number, so is every m_n\,gP. Conversely, Bézout’s identity (whole numbers whose greatest common divisor is 1 have a combination with whole-number coefficients equal to 1) gives whole numbers u_n with \sum_n u_nm_n = 1, so gP = \sum_n u_n\,(m_n\,gP) is a whole number. So the periods are exactly the whole-number multiples of 1/g, and the shortest is 1/g. Finally, if every f_n is a whole-number multiple of some g', then 1/g' is a period, hence a multiple of 1/g, so g' \le g: g is the largest such frequency.

Check on the examples. Partials at 440, 660 and 880 Hz have h = 220, q_n = 2, 3, 4 and d = 1, so g = 220 Hz although no partial sits there. Tones at 100 and 159 Hz have g = 1 Hz: the sum repeats once a second.

Real partials fade, or sit only near the multiples, so the sound only nearly repeats. Its repetition rate is then the largest frequency g in the range we hear as pitch such that every strong partial lies within a set tolerance of a whole-number multiple of g. The tolerance comes from hearing: a low partial mistuned by more than about 1 to 3%, depending on the listener, stands out as a separate tone.13 Within 2%, the drum’s strong partials in the first demo fit no g above about 20 Hz, below the range we hear as pitch; the string’s fit 196 Hz exactly.

13 B. C. J. Moore, B. R. Glasberg and R. W. Peters, Thresholds for hearing mistuned partials as separate tones in harmonic complexes, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 80 (1986), 479–483; Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., 2012, chapter 3, on hearing out partials.

Take the partials of the drum in the first demo up to 3.2 times the lowest that start with at least a tenth of the strongest one’s size: 1, 1.593, 2.295, 2.653 and 3.155 times 140 Hz. (Its partials at 2.136 and 2.917 start at only about 2% of the strongest’s size, because the strike point or the listening point sits near a still line of their modes.) Call a frequency g a fit within a tolerance \epsilon if each partial lies within the fraction \epsilon of a whole-number multiple kg with k \ge 1. A computer search over g gives the largest fit; adding the drum’s higher partials could only lower it.

Within 3%, a base of about 46 Hz fits, as its 3rd, 5th, 7th, 8th and 10th multiples, with nothing at the base or at its 2nd or 4th multiple. That tolerance is generous: the thresholds above are for one mistuned partial among exact harmonics, and here every partial is off at once. In the first demo the sound is a thud.

Fading sounds. A sound that dies away cannot repeat exactly: if p(t+P) = p(t) for every t, then p(t) = p(t + rP) for every whole number r, which tends to 0 as r grows, so p is zero. It can nearly repeat: if every partial sits on a multiple of g and fades with the same time \tau, each period is the one before shrunk by the factor e^{-1/(g\tau)}, by 0.45% at g = 220 Hz and \tau = 1 s.

The missing fundamental

The pitch of a single note (not a chord) is its repetition rate, even when no partial sits there;14 call this the pitch rule.

14 Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., 2012, chapter 6.

Mix your own

The wave over three cycles of 220 Hz, each marked by a gray tick, and its partials on the pitch ruler. The readout gives the repetition rate.

Remove its 220 Hz partial (second button): the wave changes shape but still repeats every 1/220 of a second, and the pitch stays. Hearing the pitch with no partial at the repetition rate, the fundamental, is the missing-fundamental effect; it keeps a deep voice’s pitch over a telephone line, which carries little below about 300 Hz.15 In “Mix your own”, keep only 2×, 4×, 6× and 8×, and the pitch jumps an octave.

15 Wikipedia, Missing fundamental.

Beats

Two partials close in frequency are heard as one tone that swells and fades. For two equal pure tones at f_1 and f_2, \sin(2\pi f_1 t) + \sin(2\pi f_2 t) = 2\cos\big(\pi (f_2 - f_1) t\big)\,\sin\big(\pi (f_1 + f_2) t\big), a tone at the average frequency whose size swells and fades |f_2 - f_1| times a second: beats.16 Faster beats sound rough, and tones far enough apart sound as two smooth tones.17

16 Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., 2012, chapter 1.

17 R. Plomp and W. J. M. Levelt, Tonal consonance and critical bandwidth, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 38 (1965), 548–560.

The sum of two equal pure tones over one second. At 110 Hz apart the swells come too fast to see here, and you hear two smooth tones.

Exceptions to the pitch rule

The pitch rule fails for three kinds of sound.

Chords. C4 and G4 (note names) tuned to exactly 3 : 2 together repeat 130.8 times a second, the rate of C3, yet you hear two notes: the ear sorts the partials into two sets of harmonics, with different bases, and hears each set as one sound.18

18 A. S. Bregman, Auditory Scene Analysis: The Perceptual Organization of Sound, MIT Press, 1990; and his page Introduction to auditory scene analysis.

Each note has eight harmonics, with sizes 1, 1/2, …, 1/8: C4 at 261.6 Hz, G4 at exactly 3/2 of that, 392.4 Hz.

Bells and kettledrums. A harmonic subset of the partials sets the pitch, and the ear may choose an octave above that subset’s base. A bell is heard roughly an octave below its main partial, the nominal, often with no partial at the pitch heard.19 A kettledrum is named an octave above the base its main partials fit.20

19 W. A. Hibbert, What note do we hear when a bell rings?

20 HyperPhysics, The Timpani, tabulating A. H. Benade, Fundamentals of Musical Acoustics, Oxford University Press, 1976.

21 J. Jaatinen and J. Pätynen, Effect of inharmonicity on pitch perception and subjective tuning of piano tones, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 152 (2022), 1146–1157.

Strongly stretched partials. Partials running sharp of the multiples, as on a stiff piano string, pull the pitch up.21

DetailsThe bell’s and the kettledrum’s partials

The bell. In a well-tuned church bell the main partials, the hum, prime, tierce, quint and nominal, sit near 0.5, 1, 1.2, 1.5 and 2 times the prime, and two more, the superquint and the octave nominal, near 3 and 4 times (W. A. Hibbert, The upper partials of bells). The nominal, superquint and octave nominal, nearly in the ratios 2 : 3 : 4, are heard as harmonics of a missing fundamental, the strike note: the pitch the bell is heard at, roughly an octave below the nominal and close to the prime (W. A. Hibbert, The strike note of bells, first published in The Ringing World, 20 June 2003, p. 586; Wikipedia, Strike tone). It can differ from half the nominal by about a quarter of a semitone, and often no partial sits exactly at it (W. A. Hibbert, What note do we hear when a bell rings?). Measured from the hum, the main partials sit at 1, 2, 2.4, 3, 4, 6 and 8: whole numbers, except the tierce, a minor third above the prime. Without the tierce they fit the hum as base, yet the bell is heard an octave higher.

The kettledrum. A kettledrum’s air pulls one set of its modes near 2, 3, 4 and 5 times a base, yet players name the note an octave higher, at the lowest of the set, the drum’s principal tone. A kettledrum measured by Benade, tuned to C3, had its principal tone at 130.8 Hz and partials at about 1.50, 2.00, 2.49 and 2.98 times it, close to 3, 4, 5 and 6 times half of it, together with others off this pattern (HyperPhysics, The Timpani, tabulating A. H. Benade, Fundamentals of Musical Acoustics, Oxford University Press, 1976). So the set forms a near-harmonic series on C2, 65.4 Hz, yet the drum is named for its principal tone, C3. The drum’s many partials off whole-number ratios make its exact pitch hard to pin down (Institute of Musical Acoustics (Wiener Klangstil), University of Music and Performing Arts Vienna, The Viennese timpani).

Four routes to a clear pitch

Instruments reach a clear pitch by four routes: modes on the whole numbers by nature, modes moved there by design, off-line partials suppressed, or a drive that repeats.

  1. By nature: strings and air columns. A string’s n-th mode fits n half-waves between its ends (the picture), and waves of every length travel along the string at the same speed, so an ideal string’s n-th mode swings at exactly n times the lowest frequency. A flute’s air column, open at both ends, resonates at every whole-number multiple, and a clarinet’s, closed at the reed, at the odd ones.22
  2. By design. A marimba bar’s arch, cut underneath, moves its first overtone from 2.76 times the lowest, for a uniform bar, to 4 times.23 A kettledrum’s air slows its coarse shapes most, pulling one set of modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5 (post 3).
  3. By suppression. Some off-line partials die fast: a kalimba tine’s overtones, at 6.27 and 17.55 times its lowest, die within a fraction of a second.24 Others are never started: a strike at a still point of a mode does not set that mode going, and timpanists strike between halfway and three-quarters of the way out,25 near the still lines of several modes that do not fit the note.
  4. By a repeating drive: bow and breath. A bow or a breath keeps feeding the motion, which settles into one repeating cycle, so its partials sit at exact whole-number multiples, whatever the object’s own modes.26 A harmonica reed’s own overtones are off the lines, yet blown it chops the air into identical puffs (post 3). The object’s modes only shape which partials are strong.

The sound is p = h \ast F: copies of h, the sound at your ear of one sharp tap on the instrument’s body, delayed and scaled by F(t), the force with which the vibrating object pushes the body. The force comes from the object’s motion u, which obeys u_{tt} = -Au, where u_{tt} is its acceleration; the spectrum of the operator A sets the f_n.
