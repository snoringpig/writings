Question. Which part of the sound sets each of the four aspects?

Answer. Write the sound as a sum of fading pure tones. Pitch comes from their frequencies, loudness from their overall size, timbre from their relative sizes and spacing, and duration from how fast they fade.

The sound as a sum of pure tones

The sound at your ear is the air pressure there, measured from the average air pressure: a function p(t) of the time t in seconds. Page 1 let you hear the four aspects; this page looks inside p(t) for what sets each one.

The simplest sound is a pure tone, p(t) = \sin(2\pi f t), the sound of a tuning fork. It repeats f times a second; f is its frequency, in hertz (Hz).

A note played on an instrument and left to ring is, to a good approximation, a sum of pure tones that fade:1 p(t) = \sum_{n} c_n\, e^{-t/\tau_n} \sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n). Term n is a partial, numbered from the lowest frequency up. It has four numbers:

1 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapters 1 and 2 (free vibrations with damping; the modes of strings and bars). Pages 3 to 5 derive this form.

  • f_n, its frequency;
  • c_n, its starting size;
  • \tau_n, its fading time: the factor e^{-t/\tau_n} shrinks it by e \approx 2.7 every \tau_n seconds;
  • \varphi_n, its phase: where in its cycle it starts.

A bowed or blown note is the same sum without the fading, for as long as the player keeps going. Why an instrument’s sound has this form, and where its numbers come from, is pages 3 to 5. This page asks only which numbers you hear as what.

Reading the four aspects off p(t)

Pitch: the common base of the f_n

When every partial’s frequency f_n is a whole-number multiple of one frequency in the range we hear as pitch (about 30 Hz to 5 kHz),2 or very nearly, the pitch of the note is the largest such frequency f.3 The reason: if every f_n is a multiple of f, then after 1/f seconds every term has completed whole cycles, so the steady sum starts again. The sound repeats f times a second, and the ear hears that rate as the pitch. In the mixer’s close-up, the wave starts again at every gray line.

2 Melodic pitch is heard from about 30 Hz (D. Pressnitzer, R. D. Patterson and K. Krumbholz, The lower limit of melodic pitch, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 109 (2001), 2074–2084) up to about 4 or 5 kHz for pure tones (A. J. Oxenham and colleagues, Pitch perception beyond the traditional existence region of pitch, PNAS 108 (2011), 7629–7634). Without this range the rule would give tones at 100 and 159 Hz, whose largest common base is 1 Hz, a pitch of 1 Hz.

3 B. C. J. Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., Emerald, 2012, chapter 6, on the pitch of complex tones. The rule is for a single note: in a chord the ear sorts the partials into several notes, and some sounds, such as bells, are heard at a pitch set by only some of their partials.

Two consequences you can hear in the mixer:

  • No partial needs to sit at f. Preset No 1st partial: c_1 = 0, so the lowest partial is at 2f, yet the sound still repeats f times a second and the pitch stays.4
  • Only the partials that are there count. Preset Even only: the partials at 2f, 4f, 6f, 8f are all multiples of 2f, so the pitch jumps up an octave.

4 This is the missing fundamental; it keeps a deep voice’s pitch over a telephone line, which carries little below about 300 Hz. Wikipedia, Missing fundamental.

When the f_n are not whole-number multiples of one such frequency (move the stretch), the sum never repeats at a rate we hear as pitch, and the pitch blurs.

ProofA sum of sines repeats exactly when every frequency is a multiple of one frequency averages of products of sines, and divisibility

Claim. Let p(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} c_n\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n) with distinct frequencies f_n > 0 and sizes c_n \ne 0. A time P > 0 is a period of p, meaning p(t+P) = p(t) for every t, exactly when every f_nP is a whole number. Hence p is periodic exactly when every ratio f_n/f_1 is a fraction, and its shortest period is then 1/g, where g is the largest frequency of which every f_n is a whole-number multiple.

Proof. If every f_nP is a whole number, each term completes whole cycles in time P, so p(t+P) = p(t). Conversely, suppose p(t+P) = p(t) for every t. The identity \sin(\theta+\delta) - \sin\theta = 2\sin(\delta/2)\cos(\theta + \delta/2) gives, with s_n = \sin(\pi f_n P) and \theta_n = \varphi_n + \pi f_nP, 0 = p(t+P) - p(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} 2c_n s_n \cos(2\pi f_n t + \theta_n). Write \langle w\rangle = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T} w(t)\,dt for the long-run average. For \gamma \ne 0 the integral of \cos(2\pi\gamma t + \delta) over [-T, T] stays bounded, so its average is 0. By 2\cos A\cos B = \cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B), for a, b > 0 the average \langle\cos(2\pi a t + \alpha)\cos(2\pi b t + \beta)\rangle is \tfrac12\cos(\alpha - \beta) if a = b and 0 if a \ne b. Multiply the displayed sum by \cos(2\pi f_k t + \theta_k) and average: every term with n \ne k vanishes, leaving c_k s_k = 0. Since c_k \ne 0, \sin(\pi f_kP) = 0, so f_kP is a whole number.

For the shortest period: if p has a period P, each f_n/f_1 = (f_nP)/(f_1P) is a fraction. Conversely, if every f_n/f_1 is a fraction, a common denominator gives f_n = q_nh with whole numbers q_n and h = f_1/D. Let d be the greatest common divisor of the q_n and g = dh, so f_n = m_ng with whole numbers m_n whose greatest common divisor is 1. By the first part, P is a period exactly when every m_n gP is a whole number. That holds if gP is a whole number; conversely, Bézout’s identity gives whole numbers u_n with \sum u_nm_n = 1, so gP = \sum u_n(m_ngP) is a whole number. So the periods are the whole-number multiples of 1/g. If every f_n is a multiple of some g', then 1/g' is a period, hence a multiple of 1/g, so g' \le g. \square

Fading sounds cannot repeat exactly: p(t) = p(t + rP) for every whole number r, and the right side tends to 0 as r grows, so p would be 0. They nearly repeat: when every f_n is a multiple of g and every term fades with the same time \tau, each cycle is the one before shrunk by the factor e^{-1/(g\tau)}.

Loudness: the overall size of the c_n

Multiplying every c_n by the same factor k multiplies p(t) by k and changes nothing else: the same f_n, the same ratios, the same \tau_n. For steady partials the sound’s power, the long-run average of p(t)^2, is \tfrac12\sum_n c_n^2 (the cross terms average to 0, by the averages in the proof above), so it grows by k^2, and the level by 20\log_{10}k decibels. In the pictures, the wave and every stem grow by the same factor. Ten decibels more sounds roughly twice as loud.5 Moving one c_n alone changes the overall size and the ratios at once, so it changes both loudness and timbre.

5 S. S. Stevens, “On the psychophysical law”, Psychological Review 64 (1957), 153–181; Wikipedia, Sone. How loud a given power sounds also depends on the frequencies, as page 1 noted.

Timbre: the ratios of the c_n, the spacing of the f_n, and how they start

With pitch, loudness and duration fixed, three things in p(t) are left, and they make the timbre:6

6 Wikipedia, Timbre; Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., 2012, chapter 8.

  1. The ratios c_n/c_1. Presets Pure, Mellow, Bright and Hollow, page 1’s four timbres, have the same pitch and the same power and differ only in these ratios: compare their stems. Strong high partials sound bright; only odd partials sound hollow, like a clarinet.
  2. How close the f_n sit to whole-number multiples. Move the stretch: smooth, then shimmering, then a clang (preset Bell-like).
  3. How the sizes change in time, especially at the start. In the formula every term starts at full size at t = 0, a sudden start like a pluck, and fades at its own rate. Tick high partials fade faster: the note starts bright and turns mellow, as a plucked string does. The start is a strong cue: without it, listeners name instruments much less well.7

7 E. Saldanha and J. F. Corso, “Timbre cues and the identification of musical instruments”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 36 (1964), 2021–2026, as summarized in K. Siedenburg, Specifying the perceptual relevance of onset transients for musical instrument identification, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 145 (2019), 1078–1087.

8 R. Plomp and H. J. M. Steeneken, “Effect of phase on the timbre of complex tones”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 46 (1969), 409–421, as summarized by J. W. Beauchamp, AUDITORY list, 2010.

The phases \varphi_n matter much less: changing them changes the shape of the wave, but the timbre far less than changing the sizes does.8 Sounds outside the formula add to the timbre too: the scrape of a bow, a breath, a hammer’s thump, and the gradual start of a bowed or blown note.

Duration: the \tau_n

Term n shrinks by a factor of 1000 in about 7\tau_n seconds, since e^{-6.9} \approx 1/1000; in the mixer, the whole note shortens or lengthens with \tau. So a ringing note lasts a few times its longest fading time, unless the player damps it: a key released, a hand on a drumhead. A bowed or blown note does not fade while the player keeps going; it lasts as long as they play.

Takeaway. A note is a sum of fading pure tones, p(t) = \sum_n c_n\, e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n), and each aspect is read off different numbers in it: pitch off the frequencies f_n (their common base), loudness off the overall size of the c_n, timbre off the ratios of the c_n, the spacing of the f_n and how the sizes change over time, and duration off the fading times \tau_n.
