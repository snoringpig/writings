A sound has a clear pitch when its partials, the pure tones it is made of, sit at or very near whole-number multiples of one frequency. A plucked string’s partials do, so it gives a note you could hum; a struck drum’s don’t, so it gives a thud.

Call this the pitch ruler. Its gray lines mark whole-number multiples, 1×, 2×, 3×, …, of one base frequency, here the lowest in each sound. Each blue stem is one frequency in the sound; taller means it starts stronger, for an idealized pluck and strike. Both objects are idealized: a perfectly flexible string at 196 Hz, plucked a fifth of the way along (so 5× is missing), and an ideal round drumhead whose lowest frequency is 140 Hz, struck about a third of the way out from its center. Their frequencies are computed for these ideal objects (post 2 works out the string, post 3 the drum); the fading times are modelling choices.

The reason, in one sentence: partials on the multiples of one frequency add up to a sound that repeats at that rate, and the ear hears the repetition rate as the pitch, even when no partial sits there. In the demo, the string’s stems sit on the lines, at exactly 1, 2, 3, … times the lowest; the drum’s sit at 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, … times the lowest, and no frequency in the range we hear as pitch fits them all closely. This post explains why that decides between a note and a thud, using no differential equations.

Four aspects of a sound

We hear four things in a sound: pitch, loudness, timbre and duration. All four are facts about hearing, measured by asking listeners, not mathematics.1

1 B. C. J. Moore, An Introduction to the Psychology of Hearing, 6th ed., Emerald, Bingley, 2012 (reissued by Brill, Leiden, 2013), chapters 4, 6 and 8 (loudness, pitch, timbre); R. Plomp, Aspects of Tone Sensation: A Psychophysical Study, Academic Press, 1976.

Sound is the air pressure p(t) at a listener, as a function of time. The simplest sound is a pure tone, the sound of a tuning fork: a pressure swing a\cos(2\pi f t + \varphi) about the average air pressure, with frequency f in hertz (cycles per second), size a, and phase \varphi, which says where in its cycle it starts.

A partial is one pure-tone component of a sound, with its own frequency, size and fading. An instrument left to ring after a pluck or a strike sounds, to a good approximation, a sum of partials: p(t) = \sum_k a_k\, e^{-t/\tau_k} \cos(2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k), with frequencies f_k, starting sizes a_k, phases \varphi_k and fading times \tau_k: every \tau_k seconds, partial k shrinks by a factor of e \approx 2.7. Each partial comes from one of the instrument’s modes, a shape the object swings in at one frequency, such as a string swinging as one arc, or as two halves in opposite directions; the body and the room pass them on, adding some ringing of their own. Post 2 derives this form for a string, and the appendix When does an equation have a spectrum? in general; what it leaves out, such as breath, is noise.

A harmonic is a partial at a whole-number multiple n f of a base frequency f, the n-th harmonic; a harmonic sound is one whose partials are all harmonics of one base. An overtone is any partial above the lowest, so for a harmonic sound the first overtone is the second harmonic.

Pitch

Pitch is how high or low a sound is heard, measured as the frequency of the pure tone that listeners match it to.2 Pitch that can carry a melody is heard for repetition rates (defined below) from about 30 Hz,3 and for pure tones up to about 4 or 5 kHz, the traditional upper limit.4 Call this the range we hear as pitch.

Loudness

Loudness is how strong a sound seems. It is set mainly by the sound pressure level: 10 dB more is ten times the power and sounds about twice as loud.5 It also depends on frequency: at the same level, a low tone sounds quieter than a mid-range one.6

5 S. S. Stevens, “On the psychophysical law”, Psychological Review 64 (1957), 153–181; Wikipedia, Sone (twice as loud per 10 dB) and Stevens’s power law.

6 ISO 226:2023, Acoustics — Normal equal-loudness-level contours; HyperPhysics, Equal loudness curves.

DetailsDecibels, equal-loudness contours and the power law

The sound pressure level is 20\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{rms}}/20\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}) decibels (dB), with p_{\mathrm{rms}} the root-mean-square pressure, the square root of the average squared pressure swing; the intensity, the power carried through each square metre, grows as p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2, so 10 dB more is ten times the intensity. By the equal-loudness contours of ISO 226, which give the level at which pure tones of each frequency sound equally loud to young listeners, a 100 Hz tone needs about 79 dB to match a 1 kHz tone at 60 dB (ISO 226:2023, Acoustics — Normal equal-loudness-level contours; the level is computed from the formula of the 2003 edition, from which the 2023 values differ by at most 0.6 dB, as the standard’s foreword states). At equal intensity, partials spread beyond a certain width in frequency sound louder than partials bunched together (E. Zwicker, G. Flottorp and S. S. Stevens, “Critical band width in loudness summation”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 29 (1957), 548–557). Loudness grows roughly as the 0.3 power of intensity, so 10 dB more sounds about twice as loud, since 10^{0.3} \approx 2 (S. S. Stevens, “On the psychophysical law”, Psychological Review 64 (1957), 153–181; Wikipedia, Stevens’s power law).

Timbre

Timbre is what tells apart two sounds of the same loudness and pitch.7 It is mostly the partials’ sizes and how they change, the start especially.8 It also includes how far the partials sit from whole-number multiples, how the sound starts and fades (attack and decay), noise and transients that are not modes (bow noise, breath, hammer thump), and fixed resonances that stay put whatever note is played (formants: a violin’s body, a clarinet’s tone-hole cutoff).9

Duration

Duration is how long a note sounds. A ringing instrument’s note lasts as long as its fading times allow, unless the player damps it: a key released, a mute, or a hand on the head; a bass drummer stops a note with a leg or a towel on the head.10 A bowed or blown note lasts as long as the player keeps going. How a note fades is part of its timbre; how long it lasts is its duration.

10 “How to play the bass drum properly”, with Keith Aleo, Interlochen Center for the Arts, 3 April 2023.

A string at 196 Hz. Plucked, it fades on its own unless damped; the damped note has the same partials, all made to die within about a tenth of a second. Bowed, it is a steady tone (partials 1, 1/2, 1/3, … of a repeating motion) that lasts as long as the bow moves.

For an instrument left to ring, all four are heard from three things about its partials: where they sit, how strong they are and how fast they fade. Pitch is heard mainly from where they sit; loudness from their strengths, weighted by frequency; timbre from all three, especially early on, plus sounds such as breath or bow noise; duration from the fading and from when the player stops or damps the note. Bowed and blown notes are different (route 4 below). The rest of this post asks where the partials must sit for a clear pitch.

Why whole numbers repeat

Two pure tones repeat together only when their frequency ratio is a fraction. Take 220 Hz and 330 Hz, in the ratio 3 : 2: after two cycles of the lower tone the upper one has completed three, so the sum begins again, 110 times a second. In general, for a ratio j/k in lowest terms, the sum repeats every k cycles of the lower tone, at the rate f_1/k. At 1.59 : 1, which is 159/100, that takes 100 cycles: 2.2 times a second for a lower tone at 220 Hz, far below the pitch range.

Two pure tones, the lower at 220 Hz. Gray ticks mark cycles of the lower tone. At 2 : 1 the sum repeats every cycle; at 3 : 2 every two; at 1.59 : 1 it lines up only after 100 cycles, far off the right of this picture.

The same holds for any number of steady partials: their sum repeats exactly when all their frequencies are whole-number multiples of one frequency, and then it repeats at the largest such frequency. A signal p is periodic with period P > 0 if p(t+P) = p(t) for every t, and the repetition rate of a sound whose partials are steady and exact whole-number multiples of a common frequency is 1 over its shortest period, which is that largest common frequency. That the sum repeats only for fractions needs one more fact, that sinusoids of different frequencies cannot cancel one another.

Precisely. Let p(t) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k\cos(2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k), with distinct frequencies f_k > 0 and sizes a_k \ne 0. A time P > 0 is a period of p exactly when every f_k is a whole-number multiple of 1/P. So p is periodic exactly when every ratio f_k/f_1 is a fraction, and then its shortest period is 1/g, where g is the largest frequency of which every f_k is a whole-number multiple.

ProofSinusoids of different frequencies are independent, so a period must fit every partial integrals of products of cosines, and divisibility

Lemma. Let 0 < g_1 < \dots < g_m, and suppose that c + \sum_{j=1}^m \big(\alpha_j\cos(2\pi g_j t) + \beta_j\sin(2\pi g_j t)\big) = 0 for every t. Then c = 0 and every \alpha_j and \beta_j is 0.

Proof of the lemma. For a function h, write \langle h\rangle = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T} h(t)\,dt for its long-run average, when the limit exists. For \gamma \ne 0, \int_{-T}^{T}\cos(2\pi\gamma t)\,dt = \sin(2\pi\gamma T)/(\pi\gamma) stays bounded as T grows, so \langle\cos(2\pi\gamma t)\rangle = 0; and \langle 1\rangle = 1. The product formulas 2\cos x\cos y = \cos(x-y) + \cos(x+y) and 2\sin x\sin y = \cos(x-y) - \cos(x+y) then give, for a, b > 0, \langle\cos(2\pi a t)\cos(2\pi b t)\rangle = \langle\sin(2\pi a t)\sin(2\pi b t)\rangle = \tfrac12 if a = b and 0 if a \ne b. A product of a sine and a cosine is an odd function, so its integral over [-T, T] is 0, and so is its average. Multiply the assumed identity by \cos(2\pi g_j t) and average: every term vanishes except \alpha_j/2, by the averages just computed, so \alpha_j = 0. Multiplying by \sin(2\pi g_j t) gives \beta_j = 0 in the same way. What remains is c = 0.

Proof of the statement. If every f_kP is a whole number, each cosine completes whole cycles in time P, so p(t + P) = p(t). Conversely, suppose p(t+P) = p(t) for every t, and write \theta_k = 2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k and \delta_k = 2\pi f_k P. By the identity \cos(\theta + \delta) - \cos\theta = -2\sin(\delta/2)\sin(\theta + \delta/2), 0 = p(t+P) - p(t) = \sum_{k=1}^n -2a_k\sin(\pi f_kP)\,\sin\!\big(2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k + \pi f_kP\big). Expanding each term into \alpha_k\cos(2\pi f_k t) + \beta_k\sin(2\pi f_k t) gives \alpha_k^2 + \beta_k^2 = 4a_k^2\sin^2(\pi f_kP). The lemma, with c = 0, makes every \alpha_k and \beta_k zero, so a_k\sin(\pi f_kP) = 0; since a_k \ne 0 by hypothesis, \sin(\pi f_k P) = 0, which means f_kP is a whole number. This proves the first sentence.

The shortest period. If p has a period P, the first sentence makes every f_kP a whole number, so f_k/f_1 = (f_kP)/(f_1P) is a fraction. Conversely, suppose every f_k/f_1 is a fraction. Over a common denominator N, f_k = n_k h with h = f_1/N and whole numbers n_k. Let d be the greatest common divisor of n_1, \dots, n_n and g = dh; then f_k = m_kg with whole numbers m_k = n_k/d whose greatest common divisor is 1. By the first sentence, P is a period exactly when every m_k\,gP is a whole number. If gP is a whole number, so is every m_k\,gP. Conversely, Bézout’s identity (whole numbers whose greatest common divisor is 1 have a combination with whole-number coefficients equal to 1) gives whole numbers u_k with \sum_k u_km_k = 1, so gP = \sum_k u_k\,(m_k\,gP) is a whole number. So the periods are exactly the whole-number multiples of 1/g, and the shortest is 1/g. Finally, if every f_k is a whole-number multiple of some g', then 1/g' is a period, hence a multiple of 1/g, so g' \le g: g is the largest such frequency.

Check on the examples. Partials at 440, 660 and 880 Hz have h = 220, n_k = 2, 3, 4, d = 1, so g = 220 Hz although no partial sits there. Tones at 100 and 159 Hz have g = 1 Hz: the sum repeats once a second.

For real instruments, whose partials fade or sit only near the multiples, the repetition rate is the largest frequency in the range we hear as pitch that the partials fit within a stated tolerance. A sound that fades cannot repeat exactly, but it can nearly: if every partial sits on a multiple of g and fades with the same time \tau, each period is the one before shrunk by the factor e^{-1/(g\tau)}, by 0.45% at g = 220 Hz and \tau = 1 s. How near the multiples is near enough is a fact about hearing: a low partial mistuned by more than about 1 to 3%, depending on the listener, stands out as a separate tone.11 Within 2%, the middle of that range, the lowest strong partials of the drum in the first demo fit no frequency above about 20 Hz, below the pitch range; the string’s fit 196 Hz exactly.

Why a fading sound cannot repeat exactly. If p(t+P) = p(t) for every t, then p(t) = p(t + mP) for every whole number m, and for a sound that dies away this tends to 0 as m grows, so p is zero. A piano’s partials, slightly sharp of the lines, also nearly repeat.

Within 3%, a base of about 46 Hz fits as its 3rd, 5th, 7th, 8th and 10th multiples, each off by up to 3%, with nothing at the base or at its 2nd or 4th multiple. The 3% figure is generous. It comes from a different measurement by the same authors: in an earlier study, a single mistuned low partial of an otherwise exact harmonic sound kept counting fully toward its pitch up to about 3% (B. C. J. Moore, B. R. Glasberg and R. W. Peters, “Relative dominance of individual partials in determining the pitch of complex tones”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 77 (1985), 1853–1860, as summarized in C. J. What the ear makes of such a sound is a question about hearing; in the first demo, it is a thud.

The missing fundamental

For a single tone, the ear hears the repetition rate as the pitch, even when no partial sits at it.12 Play the note, then the same note without its 220 Hz partial, and watch the wave.

Beats

Two close partials fit no series in the pitch range, and the ear hears one tone that swells and fades. For two pure tones of equal size, the sum-to-product formula gives \cos(2\pi f_1 t) + \cos(2\pi f_2 t) = 2\cos\big(\pi (f_2 - f_1) t\big)\,\cos\big(\pi (f_1 + f_2) t\big), a tone at the average frequency whose size swells and fades |f_2 - f_1| times a second: beats.14 Tones at 220 and 222 Hz repeat together exactly twice a second, far below the pitch range; you hear a 221 Hz tone wobbling twice a second. Faster beats sound rough, and tones far enough apart sound as two smooth tones.15

Exceptions to the pitch rule

Three kinds of sound break the rule.

Chords. The ear sorts the partials into several series and hears several pitches. C4 and G4 tuned to an exact 3 : 2 have all their partials on multiples of 130.8 Hz (C3), so together they repeat 130.8 times a second; yet you hear two notes, because the ear hears two sets of harmonics with different bases as two sounds.16

16 A. S. Bregman, Auditory Scene Analysis: The Perceptual Organization of Sound, MIT Press, 1990; and his page Introduction to auditory scene analysis.

Each note has eight harmonics, with sizes 1, 1/2, …, 1/8: C4 at 261.6 Hz, G4 at exactly 3/2 of that, 392.4 Hz. Together they repeat 130.8 times a second, the rate of the last sound, which is not what the pair sounds like.

Bells and kettledrums. A harmonic subset of the partials sets the pitch, and the ear may choose an octave above the base. A bell is heard roughly an octave below its main partial, the nominal, often with no partial at the pitch heard.17 A kettledrum is named an octave above the base its main partials fit.18 The fold gives the partials; the drums in post 3 give the kettledrum’s modes.

DetailsThe bell’s and the kettledrum’s partials

The bell. In a well-tuned church bell the main partials, the hum, prime, tierce, quint and nominal, sit near 0.5, 1, 1.2, 1.5 and 2 times the prime, and two more, the superquint and the octave nominal, near 3 and 4 times (W. A. Hibbert, The upper partials of bells). The strike note, the pitch the bell is heard at, is close to the prime (Wikipedia, Strike tone). The nominal, superquint and octave nominal, nearly in the ratios 2 : 3 : 4, are heard as harmonics of a missing fundamental, the strike note, which lies roughly an octave below the nominal (W. A. Hibbert, The strike note of bells, first published in The Ringing World, 20 June 2003, p. 586). It can differ from half the nominal by about a quarter of a semitone, and often no partial sits exactly at it, a puzzle Rayleigh noted in 1890; which partials set the note also depends on the bell’s size (W. A. Hibbert, What note do we hear when a bell rings?). Measured from the hum, the main partials sit at 1, 2, 2.4, 3, 4, 6 and 8: whole numbers, except the tierce, a minor third above the prime. Without the tierce, the main partials fit the hum as base, yet the bell is heard an octave higher.

The kettledrum. A kettledrum’s air pulls one set of its modes near 2, 3, 4 and 5 times a base, yet players name the note an octave higher, at the lowest of the set, the drum’s principal tone. A kettledrum measured by Benade, tuned to C3, had its principal tone at 130.8 Hz and partials at about 1.50, 2.00, 2.49 and 2.98 times it, close to 3, 4, 5 and 6 times half of it, together with others off this pattern (HyperPhysics, The Timpani, tabulating A. H. Benade, Fundamentals of Musical Acoustics, Oxford University Press, 1976). On C2, 65.4 Hz, that set forms a near-harmonic series, and HyperPhysics notes that the missing-fundamental effect might then give the pitch C2; the drum is named for its principal tone, C3. Vienna’s Institute of Musical Acoustics names the principal tone (“Grundton”) as the mode with one still line through the center, and notes that the drum’s many partials off whole-number ratios make its exact pitch hard to pin down (Institute of Musical Acoustics (Wiener Klangstil), University of Music and Performing Arts Vienna, The Viennese timpani).

Four routes to a clear pitch

Instruments reach a clear pitch by four routes: modes on the whole numbers by nature, modes moved there by design, off-line partials suppressed, or a drive that repeats.

  1. By nature: strings and air columns. A string’s n-th mode fits n half-waves between its ends (the picture), and every wave travels along the string at the same speed, so an ideal string’s n-th mode swings at exactly n times the lowest frequency. A flute’s air column, open at both ends, resonates at every whole-number multiple, and a clarinet’s, closed at the reed, at the odd ones.21 Guitars and harps take this route, and the piano nearly, since stiffness pushes its partials slightly sharp. Bowed strings and winds start here too; route 4 makes their partials exact.
  2. By design. A kettledrum’s air slows its coarse shapes most, pulling one set of modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5 (the drums in post 3). A marimba bar’s arch, cut underneath, moves its first overtone from 2.76 times the lowest, for a uniform bar, to 4 times; a xylophone’s shallower cut, to 3 times.22 A patch of paste loading the middle of a tabla’s head brings its modes near whole-number ratios.23 Founders shape and tune a bell so that most of its principal partials sit on multiples of half the strike note, all but the minor-third tierce.24
  3. By suppression. Some off-line partials are weak or die fast: a kalimba tine’s overtones, at 6.27 and 17.55 times its lowest, die within a fraction of a second,25 and a struck glockenspiel bar’s modes all die out quickly except the lowest.26 Others are never started: a strike at a still point of a mode does not set that mode going, and timpanists strike between halfway and three-quarters of the way out,27 near the still lines of several modes that do not fit the note.
  4. By a periodic drive: bow and breath. A bow or a breath keeps feeding the motion, which settles into a single repeating cycle, so its partials sit at exact whole-number multiples, whatever the object’s own modes:28 a bow’s stick and slip drive all of a string’s resonances at exactly harmonic ratios, and a harmonica reed, whose own overtones are off the lines, chops the air into identical puffs (the harmonica in post 3). The resonator’s modes only shape which partials are strong, and the player sets loudness and duration.

Dynamics change timbre too

A harder blow sounds louder and brighter. A piano hammer’s felt gets harder the more it is squashed, so a fast hammer stays on the string for a shorter time and starts more high partials;29 with a felt that stayed equally soft, a loud note would be a soft note turned up.30 The piano in post 3 takes it up.
