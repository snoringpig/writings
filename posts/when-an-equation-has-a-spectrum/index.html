An optional appendix for mathematical readers; it recaps what it uses from post 2. Uses matrices, eigenvalues and complex numbers; the deeper theory and the folds use analysis. About 25 minutes. Written in collaboration with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model.

An equation’s solutions are exactly all combinations of a fixed set of modes e^{st}W, with s a number and W a fixed vector, with any amounts, when and only when the equation can be written U' = BU, for the state U(t) and a fixed linear B with a basis of eigenvectors. The free vibration u'' = -Au of the shape u(t) of a stable, frictionless object of finite size always passes once any rigid drifts are split off: its operator A is self-adjoint, positive and has compact resolvent, and its frequencies are f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi for the eigenvalues \lambda_n of A.

The reason: for a linear equation that does not change in time, the time derivative of a solution is again a solution, so there are solutions e^{st}W that differentiation only rescales; they give every solution exactly when the vectors W form a basis.

Recap: the equation of motion

For an object held in a given way, the operator A is the linear map that sends a shape the holding allows to minus the acceleration it causes. Newton’s law for small motions then reads u'' = -A\,u, with u(t) the shape at time t and primes time derivatives. Here A includes its constants, \lambda is an eigenvalue of A and f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi.

Post 2’s two beads, of mass m on a string of tension T, spaced \ell apart and \ell from the tied ends, have heights u_1, u_2 and A = \frac{T}{m\ell}\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right), with eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, -1) and eigenvalues T/m\ell and 3T/m\ell. So the sum S = u_1 + u_2 and the difference D = u_1 - u_2 swing as independent springs, with frequencies in the ratio 1 : \sqrt3.

The smallest examples

Closing post 2’s beads into a ring gives a motion that is not a combination of modes; closing its string into a loop puts the frequencies exactly on whole-number multiples.

The two-bead ring: a drift. Join the string’s ends to each other, and each bead has the other as its neighbor on both sides: A_{\text{ring}} = \frac{T}{m\ell}\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right). Now A_{\text{ring}}(1, 1) = 0: when both beads move up together, nothing pulls them back. Started moving, they drift away silently, (u_1, u_2) = (1, 1)(a + bt) for numbers a and b. The other eigenvalue is 4T/m\ell.

Slowed down. Tied ends: S swings at 1×, D at 1.73× (√3). Ring: S, started at rest, just stays shifted, and D swings at 2×. Right: the plane of the two heights (u₁ across, u₂ up); a mix traces a tangle, S and D each a straight line. The sound puts S at 196 Hz and fades only so that it stops.

The loop of string: exactly on the whole numbers. Join a continuous string into a loop, and measure position by the angle \theta, in units where the circumference is 2\pi. With \mu the mass per unit length and subscripts partial derivatives, post 2’s string equation becomes u_{tt} = -A\,u, \qquad A = -c^2\,\frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}, on shapes that close up after one turn, X(\theta + 2\pi) = X(\theta). A mode’s shape satisfies AX = \lambda X, that is, X'' = -(\lambda/c^2)\,X, primes now in \theta. For \lambda = c^2k^2 with k > 0, X = a\cos k\theta + b\sin k\theta, which closes up exactly when k is a whole number n. For \lambda = 0 only a constant closes up: the whole loop lifted, which makes no sound. No \lambda is negative (fold). So mode n, with shapes \cos n\theta and \sin n\theta, sounds at f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{nc}{2\pi} = n\,f_1 .

It lands exactly on a multiple for two reasons. Each wave has a whole number of crests, because it must close up. And every wave travels at the same speed: u = g(\theta - ct) solves the equation for every shape g, since u_{tt} and c^2u_{\theta\theta} both equal c^2g''. Mode n is two such waves running opposite ways, \cos n\theta\cos nct = \tfrac12\cos n(\theta - ct) + \tfrac12\cos n(\theta + ct), so its n crests pass a point n times while one crest goes around.

A ring of beads with the loop's size, tension and total mass, the mass shared among more and more beads. Stems: the first ten frequencies, as multiples of the lowest; the slider's last position is the continuous loop, where every stem sits on its line. Higher modes are drawn and played quieter, so they are not shrill; the lowest plays at 196 Hz.

Every shape is a sum of these waves,1 its Fourier series u_0(\theta) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n\ge1}(a_n\cos n\theta + b_n\sin n\theta), with amounts a_n and b_n (fold). Released from rest in that shape, each wave swings at its own frequency, u(\theta, t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_n (a_n\cos n\theta + b_n\sin n\theta)\cos(nct), so the motion repeats every 2\pi/c, and so does its sound (post 1).

1 E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis: An Introduction, Princeton University Press, 2003, chapters 1–3; Wikipedia, Fourier series.

PreciselyNo negative eigenvalues, and in what sense every shape is a sum of waves uses complex numbers and analysis

No negative eigenvalue. If X'' = qX with q > 0 on the loop, multiply by X and integrate over one turn. Integration by parts, with no boundary terms on a closed loop, gives \int X''X\,d\theta = -\int (X')^2\,d\theta \le 0, while q\int X^2\,d\theta \ge 0. Both are then zero, so X = 0.

The amounts. For a shape f of the loop, \begin{gathered} f(\theta) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos n\theta + b_n\sin n\theta\big), \\ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f\cos n\theta\,d\theta, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f\sin n\theta\,d\theta . \end{gathered} The amounts come from perpendicularity: \int_0^{2\pi}\cos n\theta\cos m\theta\,d\theta = 0 for n \ne m, likewise for sines and for a sine against a cosine, so multiplying by \cos m\theta and integrating leaves \pi a_m. Whether every shape, a plucked corner included, is such a sum was argued over from the 1740s by d’Alembert, Euler and Daniel Bernoulli and settled much later (J. O. Smith III, Daniel Bernoulli’s modal decomposition, CCRMA, Stanford; Wikipedia, Fourier series).

The series. With Euler’s formula e^{in\theta} = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta, the series reads f = \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z} \hat f_n e^{in\theta} with \hat f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta, so \hat f_{\pm n} = (a_n \mp i b_n)/2 for n \ge 1 and \hat f_0 = a_0/2. The functions e^{in\theta}/\sqrt{2\pi} form an orthonormal basis of L^2(S^1), the functions on the circle with \int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta finite, for the inner product \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f\bar g\,d\theta: for every f in L^2(S^1), \Big\| f - \sum_{|n|\le M} \hat f_n e^{in\theta}\Big\| \to 0 \quad (M \to \infty), \qquad \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta = \sum_n |\hat f_n|^2 , the second identity being Parseval’s. Perpendicularity is the easy half. Completeness, that no nonzero f is perpendicular to every e^{in\theta}, is the real content: trigonometric sums approximate every continuous periodic function uniformly (Fejér’s theorem), and continuous functions are dense in L^2 (E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis: An Introduction, Princeton University Press, 2003, chapters 1–3).

At single points. Some continuous functions have Fourier series that diverge at a point, and Carleson proved in 1966 that the series of every L^2 function converges at almost every point. For shapes that are continuous and made of straight pieces it converges at every point (Dirichlet, 1829); the plucked triangle’s coefficients shrink like 1/n^2, which is why a few terms already look right in the demo below.

Why an equation that doesn’t change in time has modes

Modes exist because the time derivative of a solution is again a solution. Three steps show it, for the two beads first, with x = (u_1, u_2) the column of their heights.

1. The derivative of a solution is a solution. Differentiate x'' = -Ax in time: because A is constant, (x')'' = -A\,x'. Both ingredients matter: if A changed in time, differentiating would add -A'x, and the derivative of a nonlinear force on x is not the force on x'.

2. Look for a solution that is a multiple of its own derivative, x' = s\,x for a number s. Then (e^{-st}x)' = 0, so x(t) = e^{st}X for a fixed column X.

3. Substitute. For x = e^{st}X, the equation x'' = -Ax reads s^2e^{st}X = -e^{st}AX, so AX = -s^2X: X is an eigenvector of A with eigenvalue \lambda = -s^2. For the beads \lambda > 0, so s = \pm i\sqrt{\lambda}; by Euler’s formula, e^{i\omega t} = \cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t, the real part of e^{i\omega t}X is \cos(\omega t)\,X: the shape X swinging at angular frequency \omega = \sqrt{\lambda}. Two eigenvectors and two signs give four modes, as many as the numbers it takes to start two beads.

Every such equation, in one form. Collect the shape and its velocity into the state U = (u, u'). Then u'' = -Au becomes U' = B\,U, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I \\ -A & 0 \end{pmatrix}, with I the identity. The matrix B, the equation’s generator, turns the state into its rate of change. The same three steps give its modes.

Definition 1 (mode). For a linear system U' = BU with B constant: a nonzero solution U(t) = e^{st}W, i.e. BW = sW. If s is not real, its frequency is |\operatorname{Im} s|/2\pi and it fades at rate -\operatorname{Re} s, shrinking by the factor e in its fading time \tau = -1/\operatorname{Re} s; real motions are real parts, pairing s with its complex conjugate.

The swing of step 3 is the older notion.2

2 Wikipedia, Normal mode.

Definition 2 (normal mode). For u'' = -Au: a real solution u(t) = \cos(\omega t + \varphi)\,X with X \ne 0, \omega > 0 and a phase \varphi. It exists if and only if AX = \omega^2X.

How they meet. A normal mode is the real part of the pair of Definition-1 modes s = \pm i\omega, W = (X, \pm i\omega X). At \omega = 0 the pair collapses into a Jordan block, the smallest way for a matrix to lack a basis of eigenvectors: the rigid drift X(a + bt), b \ne 0, is a solution but not a mode. So in finite dimensions B has a basis of eigenvectors exactly when A has one and is invertible (fold).

ProofNormal modes, drifts, and when B has a basis of eigenvectors uses linear algebra

Definition 2’s condition. For u = \cos(\omega t + \varphi)X, u'' = -\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \varphi)X and -Au = -\cos(\omega t + \varphi)AX. These agree for all t if and only if AX = \omega^2X: if they agree, take a t with \cos(\omega t + \varphi) \ne 0 and divide; the converse is the computation itself.

Eigenvectors of B. Let W = (X, Y). Then BW = sW says Y = sX and -AX = sY, that is, W = (X, sX) with AX = -s^2X and X \neq 0. So each eigenvalue \lambda of A gives the eigenvalues s of B with s^2 = -\lambda: two of them, \pm\sqrt{-\lambda}, when \lambda \neq 0 (for \lambda = \omega^2 > 0, s = \pm i\omega), and only s = 0 when \lambda = 0. The eigenvectors of B for s are the (X, sX) with X in A’s eigenspace for \lambda = -s^2.

The real part of the pair. With W_\pm = (X, \pm i\omega X), \tfrac12\big(e^{i\varphi}e^{i\omega t}W_+ + e^{-i\varphi}e^{-i\omega t}W_-\big) = \big(\cos(\omega t + \varphi)X,\ -\omega\sin(\omega t + \varphi)X\big), which equals \operatorname{Re}\big(e^{i(\omega t + \varphi)}W_+\big) and is the state (u, u') of the normal mode.

The drift. If AX = 0 with X \ne 0, then B(X, 0) = (0, 0) and B(0, X) = (X, 0). The solution starting at (aX, bX) is (X(a + bt), bX), as differentiating checks. The only eigenvectors of B in the span of (X, 0) and (0, X) are the multiples of (X, 0), so for b \neq 0 this solution is not a combination of modes.

Counting. Work over the complex numbers, with A of size n, and let g_\lambda be the dimension of A’s eigenspace for \lambda. By the second paragraph, and since eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are independent, B, of size 2n, has exactly 2\sum_{\lambda\ne0} g_\lambda + g_0 independent eigenvectors. Since \sum_\lambda g_\lambda \le n, with equality exactly when A is diagonalizable, this is at most 2(n - g_0) + g_0 = 2n - g_0, and it equals 2n exactly when g_0 = 0 (A invertible) and \sum_\lambda g_\lambda = n (A diagonalizable).

How much this guarantees. In finite dimensions, at least one mode: BW = sW has a solution W \ne 0 exactly when the determinant \det(B - s), a polynomial in s, vanishes, and every polynomial has a complex root. One mode is not a basis: x'' = 0 has only one (counterexample 1). In infinite dimensions there may be none: on an infinite string the solutions e^{\pm ikx} of -c^2X'' = \lambda X have infinite energy, so none is an allowed shape.

The criterion

The criterion. The solutions of an equation are exactly all combinations of a fixed set of modes, with any amounts, when and only when the equation is linear, unchanging in time, and its generator has a full set of eigenvectors.

On the two beads. Their A has two independent eigenvectors and the positive eigenvalues \lambda_1 = T/m\ell and \lambda_2 = 3T/m\ell, so B has the four eigenvectors (X, \pm i\omega X), and every solution is x(t) = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\big(a_1\cos\omega_1 t + b_1\sin\omega_1 t\big) + \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\big(a_2\cos\omega_2 t + b_2\sin\omega_2 t\big), \qquad \omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}, with four real numbers a_k, b_k that the starting heights and velocities fix. On the two-bead ring the criterion fails: A_{\text{ring}} has the eigenvalue 0, so B has only three independent eigenvectors, and the drift (1, 1)(a + bt), b \neq 0, is a solution no combination of modes produces. Split it off, and the rest passes.

Definition 3 (modal system). One whose modes form a basis of its states: every state is uniquely a (convergent) combination of mode vectors. (Infinite dimensions: a Riesz basis of the energy space, not merely a complete set.)

ProofThe criterion as an equality of sets uses linear algebra

Statement. Let F: \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^n, and call a differentiable U: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}^n a solution of U' = F(U) if U'(t) = F(U(t)) for every t. Let W_1, \dots, W_n be a basis of \mathbb{C}^n, let s_1, \dots, s_n be complex numbers, and let W be the matrix with columns W_k. Then the solutions of U' = F(U) are exactly all combinations U(t) = \sum_{k=1}^n c_k\,e^{s_k t}\,W_k , with every coefficient vector c = (c_1, \dots, c_n) \in \mathbb{C}^n allowed, if and only if F(U) = BU for every U, with B = W\operatorname{diag}(s)\,W^{-1}.

Proof of “if”. Suppose F(U) = BU with B = W\operatorname{diag}(s)W^{-1}. Then BW = W\operatorname{diag}(s), which says BW_k = s_kW_k for each k. For any c, the function U(t) = \sum_k c_ke^{s_kt}W_k has U'(t) = \sum_k c_ks_ke^{s_kt}W_k = \sum_k c_ke^{s_kt}BW_k = BU(t), so it is a solution. Conversely, let U be any solution, and set c = W^{-1}U(0) and V(t) = \sum_k c_ke^{s_kt}W_k, a solution with V(0) = U(0). The difference Z = W^{-1}(U - V) satisfies Z' = W^{-1}B(U - V) = \operatorname{diag}(s)Z, so each entry obeys z_k' = s_kz_k; then (e^{-s_kt}z_k)' = 0, so z_k(t) = e^{s_kt}z_k(0) = 0. Hence U = V.

Proof of “only if”. Suppose the solutions are exactly the combinations, every c allowed. Take any vector Y \in \mathbb{C}^n and choose c = W^{-1}Y, which is allowed. The solution U(t) = \sum_kc_ke^{s_kt}W_k has U(0) = Wc = Y and U'(0) = \sum_kc_ks_kW_k = W\operatorname{diag}(s)\,c = W\operatorname{diag}(s)W^{-1}Y. Because U is a solution, U'(0) = F(U(0)) = F(Y). So F(Y) = W\operatorname{diag}(s)W^{-1}Y for every Y: the equation at time 0, applied to solutions starting at every state, forces F to be this linear map.

Remarks. (1) The same argument at any time t_0 in place of 0 shows that even an equation allowed to change in time, U' = F(t, U), whose solutions are exactly these combinations must have F(t, U) = BU for every t: linearity and time-invariance both follow, as the criterion in words says. (2) For a real equation, such as u'' = -Au with A real, the real solutions are those whose coefficients pair each mode with its complex conjugate, c_k with \bar c_k. (3) For continuous objects the same holds with a Riesz basis of the energy space in place of a basis, and square-summable coefficients (see the deeper theory below).

PreciselyWhy “with any amounts” matters uses derivatives

Exponentials with fixed exponents alone do not force an equation to be linear. On the half-line U > -\tfrac14, take U' = 2U - \frac{\sqrt{1 + 4U} - 1}{2} , whose right side is smooth there. Every solution is U(t) = c\,e^{t} + c^2\,e^{2t} for one real number c, for as long as it stays in U > -\tfrac14: for c \ge 0 that is all t, and for c < 0 the solution reaches the edge U = -\tfrac14 when c\,e^t = -\tfrac12.

Proof. Put w = (\sqrt{1 + 4U} - 1)/2, which maps U > -\tfrac14 one-to-one onto w > -\tfrac12, with inverse U = w^2 + w. In terms of w the equation reads (2w + 1)\,w' = 2(w^2 + w) - w = w\,(2w + 1). Since 2w + 1 > 0, this is w' = w, so w = c\,e^t, and U = w^2 + w = c\,e^t + c^2e^{2t}, valid while c\,e^t > -\tfrac12.

Every solution is built from the same two exponentials, e^t and e^{2t}, yet the equation is not linear: the coefficients are tied, the second always the square of the first, and the sum of two solutions, (c + d)e^t + (c^2 + d^2)e^{2t}, is not a solution unless cd = 0. In the criterion, “every coefficient vector allowed” rules this out.

Three levels, from the familiar to the general

The criterion holds at three nested levels: pure tones, then fading, then shapes whose parts lag behind one another.

Level 1 · Free vibration without friction

The equation is u'' = -Au with A self-adjoint and positive. For beads, self-adjoint means symmetric, entry (i, j) equal to entry (j, i), and positive, A > 0, means x\cdot Ax > 0 for every x \ne 0.

Result (level 1, any number of beads). Let A be a real symmetric n\times n matrix with A > 0.

  1. A has eigenvectors X_1, \dots, X_n forming an orthonormal basis, with eigenvalues \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n > 0.
  2. Every solution of x'' = -Ax is x(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big(a_i\cos\omega_i t + \frac{b_i}{\omega_i}\sin\omega_i t\Big), \qquad \omega_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}, with a_i = X_i\cdot x(0) and b_i = X_i\cdot x'(0): mode i sounds at f_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}/2\pi.
  3. The energy E = \tfrac12\,x'\cdot x' + \tfrac12\,x\cdot Ax is the same at every moment.
ProofLevel 1 for any number of beads, and where symmetry comes from uses linear algebra

Part 1 is the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices; \lambda_i = X_i\cdot AX_i > 0 because A > 0.

For part 2, write x(t) = \sum_j c_j(t)X_j with c_j = X_j\cdot x, the amount of X_j in x. Take the dot product of x'' = -Ax with X_i. On the left, X_i\cdot x'' = c_i''. On the right, X_i\cdot Ax = (AX_i)\cdot x, because both sides equal \sum_{j,k}(X_i)_jA_{jk}x_k once A_{jk} = A_{kj}; so X_i\cdot Ax = \lambda_iX_i\cdot x = \lambda_ic_i. Hence c_i'' = -\lambda_ic_i for each i separately: n independent springs. Each has exactly one solution with c_i(0) = a_i and c_i'(0) = b_i, namely a_i\cos\omega_it + (b_i/\omega_i)\sin\omega_it (uniqueness as in the criterion’s proof).

For part 3, E' = x'\cdot x'' + \tfrac12(x'\cdot Ax + x\cdot Ax') = x'\cdot(x'' + Ax) = 0, using x\cdot Ax' = x'\cdot Ax for symmetric A.

Drifts. If A is symmetric with A \ge 0 and has the eigenvalue 0, its eigenvectors for 0 (the drifts) are perpendicular to the others, and A maps the perpendicular complement of the drifts to itself and is positive there. The motion splits accordingly: along each drift X, c'' = 0, so c = a + bt; on the rest, the result above.

Symmetry from energy. Near rest the stored energy is V(x) \approx \tfrac12\,x\cdot Kx, where the stiffness matrix K is the matrix of second derivatives of V, symmetric because mixed partial derivatives don’t depend on the order of differentiation. The restoring force is -\nabla V \approx -Kx, keeping the first-order term; that is where linearity comes from. A stable rest means x\cdot Kx > 0 except along rigid drifts.

Unequal masses. Newton’s law reads Mx'' = -Kx with M the diagonal matrix of masses, and A = M^{-1}K is symmetric in the mass-weighted dot product \sum_i m_ix_iy_i; equivalently v = M^{1/2}x obeys v'' = -M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}v with a symmetric matrix.

Why instruments qualify. A stretch of string feels only the difference of its two beads’ heights, so bead j pulls bead i as hard as i pulls j: A is symmetric. Displacing a held object stores energy, so A is positive, except on rigid drifts (\lambda = 0), which are split off. For strings, tubes, bars and drums, integration by parts gives both (fold); that every shape is a sum of modes needs finite size (the deeper theory).

ProofSymmetry and energy for strings, bars and drums uses integration by parts

With \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^L f\,g\,dx for shapes f and g of the string with tied ends, integrate by parts once: \langle Af, g\rangle = -c^2\int_0^L f''\,g\,dx = -c^2\big[f'\,g\big]_0^L + c^2\int_0^L f'\,g'\,dx = c^2\int_0^L f'\,g'\,dx , since g(0) = g(L) = 0. The last expression does not change when f and g swap, so \langle Af, g\rangle = \langle f, Ag\rangle, and with g = f it is c^2\int (f')^2 \ge 0, zero only for a constant shape, which a tied end forbids. The bracket also vanishes on a loop and at a closed tube end, where f' = 0. For a bar, integrating by parts twice gives \kappa^2\int f''g''\,dx, every boundary term killed by clamped, pinned or free ends; for a drumhead, the two-dimensional version gives c^2\iint\nabla f\cdot\nabla g\,dA, the rim term killed by the fixed rim.

Two consequences follow as for matrices. Modes with different eigenvalues are perpendicular: if AX = \lambda X and AY = \mu Y, then \lambda\langle X, Y\rangle = \langle AX, Y\rangle = \langle X, AY\rangle = \mu\langle X, Y\rangle. And energy is conserved: with E = \tfrac12\langle u_t, u_t\rangle + \tfrac12\langle Au, u\rangle, symmetry gives E' = \langle u_t, u_{tt} + Au\rangle = 0; times the mass per unit length, the two terms are the kinetic energy and the energy stored in the stretched string, \tfrac12T\int u_x^2\,dx.

Level 2 · Friction that the same shapes split

The equation is u'' + C\,u' + A\,u = 0 , with the friction C symmetric and commuting with A: AC = CA. For example C = \alpha + \beta A for numbers \alpha and \beta, with \alpha standing for \alpha I. The standing shapes stay real, and each fades.

Result (level 2). Let A and C be real symmetric n\times n matrices with AC = CA.

  1. There is an orthonormal basis X_1, \dots, X_n of shared eigenvectors: AX_k = \lambda_kX_k and CX_k = \gamma_kX_k.
  2. Every solution of u'' + Cu' + Au = 0 is u(t) = \sum_k q_k(t)X_k, where each amount obeys its own equation, q_k'' + \gamma_kq_k' + \lambda_kq_k = 0.
  3. The system is modal exactly when, for every shared eigenvector (with values \lambda for A and \gamma for C), s^2 + \gamma s + \lambda = 0 has distinct roots.

For light friction, \gamma_k^2 < 4\lambda_k, mode k is the real shape X_k swinging at f_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k - \gamma_k^2/4}\,/2\pi and fading with the time \tau_k = 2/\gamma_k.

Critical damping of one mode, \gamma^2 = 4\lambda, breaks part 3 (counterexample 2).

ProofLevel 2 uses linear algebra

Part 1. Each eigenspace E_\lambda of A is mapped to itself by C: if AX = \lambda X then A(CX) = CAX = \lambda\,CX. On E_\lambda, C is symmetric, so E_\lambda has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of C. Together over all \lambda these give part 1, since the eigenspaces of A are perpendicular and fill out \mathbb{R}^n.

Part 2. Take the dot product of the equation with X_k and use symmetry as in level 1: X_k\cdot Cu' = \gamma_kq_k' and X_k\cdot Au = \lambda_kq_k, with q_k = X_k\cdot u.

Part 3. In the state U = (u, u') the generator is B = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & I \\ -A & -C\end{smallmatrix}\right). In the basis (X_1, 0), (0, X_1), \dots, (X_n, 0), (0, X_n) it splits into 2\times2 blocks B_k = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\lambda_k & -\gamma_k\end{smallmatrix}\right), with characteristic polynomial s^2 + \gamma_ks + \lambda_k. A 2\times2 matrix with two distinct eigenvalues has two independent eigenvectors. If the eigenvalue s_0 is repeated, B_k - s_0 is not zero (its top right entry is 1), so it has rank 1 and B_k has only one eigenvector: a Jordan block. B has a basis of eigenvectors exactly when every block does.

Light friction. For \gamma_k^2 < 4\lambda_k the roots are s = -\gamma_k/2 \pm i\sqrt{\lambda_k - \gamma_k^2/4}, and the real solutions are q_k = e^{-\gamma_kt/2}\big(a\cos\tilde\omega_kt + b\sin\tilde\omega_kt\big) with \tilde\omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k - \gamma_k^2/4}.

Unequal masses. For Mu'' + Cu' + Ku = 0, with M the diagonal matrix of masses and K the stiffness matrix, the condition is CM^{-1}K = KM^{-1}C (T. K. Caughey and M. E. J. O’Kelly, “Classical normal modes in damped linear dynamic systems”, Journal of Applied Mechanics 32 (1965), 583–588).

Why C must be symmetric. Friction that turns motion into heat comes from a dissipation function \tfrac12\,u'\cdot Cu', as Rayleigh introduced it, and is symmetric. An antisymmetric velocity term, such as a spinning object’s gyroscopic force, does no work and can commute with A (for A = \omega^2I it commutes with everything), yet its modes are rotating motions with complex shapes such as (1, \pm i), not real standing shapes.

Two piano strings in tune. Two strings tuned alike on one bridge, one mode each at angular frequency \omega_0, have A = \omega_0^2I and, through the bridge they both drive, the friction C = r\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right) for a rate r, which commutes with A. The shared eigenvectors are (1, 1), swinging together and fading at the rate r, and (1, -1), whose pushes on the bridge cancel, so the bridge does not drain it. An unequal strike starts both, so the note fades fast, then slow.3

3 G. Weinreich, “Coupled piano strings”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 62 (1977), 1474–1484, and The coupled motion of piano strings, in Five Lectures on the Acoustics of the Piano, Royal Swedish Academy of Music, 1990, especially Two or more strings.

DetailsThe two-string model, and what it leaves to level 3

A piano note has two or three strings tuned alike on one bridge. Keep one mode per string, one number q_j each; in tune, A = \omega_0^2I on the pair. The bridge, driven by both strings at once, gives each a friction in proportion to its motion, C = r\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right), symmetric (the strings’ own losses aside). For (1, 1), \gamma = 2r: both push the yielding bridge, and the mode fades at the rate \gamma/2 = r; for (1, -1), \gamma = 0. An even hammer starts only (1, 1); an unequal strike starts both, in real modes, exactly in or out of step. This is the first half of Weinreich’s explanation (G. Weinreich, The coupled motion of piano strings, in Five Lectures on the Acoustics of the Piano, 1990): in this exact model the slow mode does not push the bridge at all, so it would hardly be heard; he showed that the strings’ slight mistuning, together with the hammer’s small irregularities, governs the aftersound, which is level 3.

Level 3 · The general case

The equation is any U' = BU. With a basis of eigenvectors it is modal, its modes in general complex: the entries of W have different phases, so parts of the shape peak at different moments.

The same two strings, mistuned. With A = \operatorname{diag}(\omega_1^2, \omega_2^2), \omega_1 \ne \omega_2, A no longer commutes with C. For a mistuning \delta = \omega_1 - \omega_2 about as large as r, the strings in each mode swing out of step by an angle other than 0^\circ or 180^\circ. Both modes then push the bridge, so the slow one is heard: the aftersound (fold).

DetailsThe two strings, in tune and mistuned, in numbers uses complex numbers

This is a toy model: each string reduced to one mode, the bridge to the friction C = r\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right), and the strings’ own losses left out. For light friction and small mistuning, write u = \operatorname{Re}\big(a(t)\,e^{i\bar\omega t}\big) with \bar\omega = (\omega_1 + \omega_2)/2 and a slowly changing complex column a. Keeping first-order terms in r and \delta, the equation becomes a' = M a, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} i\delta/2 - r/2 & -r/2 \\ -r/2 & -i\delta/2 - r/2\end{pmatrix} , with eigenvalues -\tfrac r2 \pm \tfrac12\sqrt{r^2 - \delta^2}.

  • In tune, \delta = 0: eigenvalues 0 and -r, eigenvectors (1, -1) and (1, 1), real.
  • Mistuned by less than r (at \delta = 0.6\,r: the strings swing equally far in each mode, 37^\circ apart in the nearly-together mode, fading at 0.9\,r, and 143^\circ apart in the nearly-opposite one, fading at 0.1\,r): both modes at one frequency, fading at the different rates \tfrac r2 \mp \tfrac12\sqrt{r^2 - \delta^2}. The eigenvector ratio a_2/a_1 = (i\delta/2 \mp \tfrac12\sqrt{r^2 - \delta^2})\cdot 2/r has modulus 1 and a phase other than 0 and 180^\circ. For \delta = 0.6\,r: rates 0.1\,r and 0.9\,r, ratios -0.8 + 0.6i and 0.8 + 0.6i, phases 143^\circ and 37^\circ.
  • Mistuned by exactly r: the two eigenvalues coincide at -r/2 with the single eigenvector (1, i), a Jordan block.
  • Mistuned by more than r: the frequencies split by \sqrt{\delta^2 - r^2} and both modes fade at the rate r/2: the strings beat.

A numerical check of the full equations u'' + Cu' + Au = 0, with r at 2% of \omega_0 and \delta = 0.6\,r, gives the same phases to within 0.01^\circ.

The deeper theory

Self-adjoint A \ge 0: the spectral theorem. On a Hilbert space of shapes, a space with an inner product in which limits exist, von Neumann’s spectral theorem4 represents A as multiplication by \lambda on its spectral parts. That defines \varphi(A) for a function \varphi: multiply the part at \lambda by \varphi(\lambda). So the motion with starting shape f and velocity g is always u(t) = \cos\!\big(t\sqrt{A}\big)\,f + \frac{\sin\!\big(t\sqrt{A}\big)}{\sqrt{A}}\,g , reading \sin(t\sqrt A)/\sqrt A as t on drifts. The spectrum is the set of numbers z for which A - z has no bounded inverse; for self-adjoint A \ge 0 it lies in [0, \infty).

4 J. von Neumann, “Allgemeine Eigenwerttheorie Hermitescher Funktionaloperatoren”, Mathematische Annalen 102 (1930), 49–131, and Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik, Springer, 1932; Wikipedia, Spectral theorem.

A list of notes exactly when the resolvent is compact. The resolvent (A - z)^{-1} solves Au - zu = f for u; a compact operator is a limit in norm of operators with finite-dimensional range. For self-adjoint A \ge 0, (A + 1)^{-1} is compact exactly when the spectrum is a list of eigenvalues \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \to \infty of finite multiplicity whose eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis (fold). Then the formula is a sum over modes, a list of notes, and for A > 0 the system is modal. For continuous objects, this is what “the object is finite” means; in finite dimensions it always holds.

Which objects have it. Strings and tubes, by Sturm–Liouville theory;5 bars, through a compact Green’s operator; drums on a bounded region with a reasonable edge, by Rellich’s theorem6 (fold).

5 Wikipedia, Sturm–Liouville theory.

6 F. Rellich, “Ein Satz über mittlere Konvergenz”, Nachr. Ges. Wiss. Göttingen (1930), 30–35.

The infinite string: spectral, but no list of notes. On the whole line, A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 is self-adjoint and \ge 0, with spectrum [0, \infty) and no eigenvectors. The formula still holds, the sum over modes becoming an integral over the Fourier transforms \hat f and \hat g of the start, a continuous spread of frequencies: u(x, t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\hat f(k)\cos(ckt) + \hat g(k)\,\frac{\sin(ckt)}{ck}\Big)e^{ikx}\,dk .

Without symmetry (friction, radiation), for losses that only remove energy, the motion still exists and is unique (a semigroup), but whether the eigenvectors form a Riesz basis has to be proved case by case.7 For a string with friction varying along it they do,8 and decaying modes can still make the motion grow for a while.9

PreciselyWhich objects have a compact resolvent, and solutions in the energy sense uses functional analysis
  • Strings and tubes: Sturm–Liouville theory. On a finite interval, -(pu')' + qu = \lambda\rho u with p, \rho > 0 and a condition at each end has simple eigenvalues \lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \cdots \to \infty and eigenfunctions forming an orthogonal basis.
  • Bars: the same theorem through a compact Green’s operator. The fourth-order bar is not a classical Sturm–Liouville problem, but (A + 1)^{-1} is f \mapsto \int G(x, y)f(y)\,dy with a kernel G, the Green’s function, continuous on the square [0, L]^2, which makes it compact.
  • Drums: Rellich’s theorem (F. Rellich, “Ein Satz über mittlere Konvergenz”, 1930; Wikipedia, Rellich–Kondrachov theorem), on a bounded region with a reasonable edge: any set of shapes whose energies \iint(|\nabla u|^2 + u^2) are bounded by one number has compact closure in L^2, so the resolvent is compact. For a head fixed at its rim any bounded region will do; with a free edge, very irregular regions can fail.

Solutions in the energy sense. For a start of finite energy the formula gives the one motion of finite energy, which conserves it. A plucked string’s kink has no second derivative, so the plucked string solves u_{tt} = -Au in this sense, not point by point.

ProofA list of notes exactly when the resolvent is compact uses functional analysis

Let A \ge 0 be self-adjoint on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space H. Claim: R = (A + 1)^{-1} is compact if and only if H has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors X_k of A whose eigenvalues \lambda_k have finite multiplicity and tend to \infty.

If R is compact. R is bounded, with \|R\| \le 1 because \langle(A + 1)u, u\rangle \ge \|u\|^2; it is self-adjoint; and it is one-to-one, having the inverse A + 1. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators gives an orthonormal basis of the closure of its range, which is H because R is self-adjoint and one-to-one, made of eigenvectors RX_k = \mu_kX_k with \mu_k \to 0, each nonzero \mu_k of finite multiplicity, and no \mu_k = 0. Applying A + 1 gives X_k = \mu_k(A + 1)X_k, so AX_k = \lambda_kX_k with \lambda_k = 1/\mu_k - 1 \to \infty.

If such a basis exists. List the \lambda_k in increasing order. Then Rf = \sum_k(1 + \lambda_k)^{-1}\langle f, X_k\rangle X_k. Its truncations R_N, summing over k \le N, have finite-dimensional range, and \|R - R_N\| = \sup_{k > N}(1 + \lambda_k)^{-1} \to 0. A limit in norm of such operators is compact.

The energy space. Measure the energy of a state by \|(u, v)\|_E^2 = \langle Au, u\rangle + \langle v, v\rangle. For A > 0 with such a basis, the states W_{k,\pm} = (X_k, \pm i\omega_kX_k)/\sqrt{2\lambda_k}, \omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}, have \|W_{k,\pm}\|_E^2 = (\lambda_k + \omega_k^2)/2\lambda_k = 1, are mutually perpendicular (for the pair, \lambda_k + (i\omega_k)\overline{(-i\omega_k)} = \lambda_k - \omega_k^2 = 0), and span the energy space; so they form an orthonormal basis of it, and the system is modal in the sense of Definition 3.

PreciselyWithout symmetry: semigroups, Riesz bases and growth before decay uses functional analysis

Semigroups. For a damped string or drum, energy can only decrease, \operatorname{Re}\langle BU, U\rangle_E \le 0 in the energy inner product, and B generates a contraction semigroup e^{tB}: bounded operators with e^{(t + t')B} = e^{tB}e^{t'B}, e^{0B} = I, continuous in t, sending the starting state to the state at time t, provided also that 1 - B is onto (together: B is maximally dissipative; the Lumer–Phillips theorem). This gives existence and uniqueness without any modes.

Riesz bases. In finite dimensions the only way to lack a basis of eigenvectors is a Jordan block. In infinite dimensions the eigenvectors can be complete (no state is perpendicular to all of them) and still not be a basis, or be a basis in which expansions are unstable. The property that makes mode expansions behave like orthonormal ones is a Riesz basis: the image of an orthonormal basis under a bounded operator with a bounded inverse; equivalently, every state is uniquely U = \sum_kc_kW_k with m\sum_k|c_k|^2 \le \|U\|^2 \le M\sum_k|c_k|^2 for fixed 0 < m \le M. I. C. Gohberg and M. G. Krein, Introduction to the Theory of Linear Nonselfadjoint Operators (Translations of Mathematical Monographs 18, American Mathematical Society, 1969), is the classical account of when the eigenvectors of a non-self-adjoint operator are complete or form a basis. For the string u_{tt} + 2a(x)\,u_t = u_{xx} on a finite interval, ends tied, with a friction coefficient a of bounded variation, S. Cox and E. Zuazua proved that the eigenvectors form a Riesz basis of the energy space, and that the energy decays at exactly the rate set by the slowest-fading mode (“The rate at which energy decays in a damped string”, Communications in Partial Differential Equations 19 (1994), 213–243, DOI).

Growth before decay. Even with a basis of decaying modes, a non-normal B, one that does not commute with its adjoint, so that its eigenvectors are not perpendicular, can make the size of the state grow for a while. The smallest example: B = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} -1 & 10 \\ 0 & -2\end{smallmatrix}\right) has modes fading at the rates 1 and 2, with the nearly parallel eigenvectors (1, 0) and (-10, 1). Starting from U(0) = (0, 1) = 10\,(1, 0) + (-10, 1), a near cancellation of two large modes, U(t) = \big(10\,(e^{-t} - e^{-2t}),\ e^{-2t}\big), which reaches size about 2.5 near t = 0.7 before it decays: the two modes fade at different rates and stop cancelling. Pseudospectra, the sets of z where \|(B - z)^{-1}\| is large, measure this (L. N. Trefethen and M. Embree, Spectra and Pseudospectra: The Behavior of Nonnormal Matrices and Operators, Princeton University Press, 2005).

Radiation. For an object radiating into unbounded air, the slowly fading “modes” are resonances: complex frequencies at which the resolvent, continued past the real axis, has poles. Expansions in them hold only in restricted senses.

Counterexamples, smallest first

Each of these six equations breaks one hypothesis of the criterion, and you hear the difference.

1. A drift: x'' = 0. The generator B = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0 & 1\\0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right) has only the eigenvectors along (1, 0), so x = a + bt with b \ne 0 is not a combination of modes: a Jordan block. You hear nothing. In instruments, a free bar or drum sliding as a whole; it is split off.

2. Critical damping: u'' + 2u' + u = 0. The roots of s^2 + 2s + 1 = (s + 1)^2 coincide, B = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0 & 1\\-1 & -2\end{smallmatrix}\right) has the single eigenvector (1, -1), and the solutions are (a + bt)\,e^{-t}: the same Jordan block, moved by friction. You hear no ring: released, the mass creeps back to rest without swinging.

3. A property that changes in time. In Mathieu’s equation x'' = -(1 + \varepsilon\cos 2t)\,x, for a small number \varepsilon, the stiffness wobbles,10 and differentiating a solution adds the term 2\varepsilon\sin(2t)\,x: no modes e^{st}W. By Floquet’s theorem the solutions are combinations of e^{\mu t}p(t) with p periodic, and for every small \varepsilon \neq 0 one exponent \mu has real part about \varepsilon/4: parametric resonance, as when a child pumps a swing.11 A slow change is gentler: a talking drum’s player squeezes its cords while it rings,12 and when the tension changes slowly compared with one swing, each mode keeps its shape and its frequency follows the tension, to a good approximation. You hear the pitch glide.

4. A force not proportional to the displacement. The wide pendulum, x'' = -\sin x, is nonlinear and softening: wider swings are slower.13 Strings and drumheads stiffen instead, so a loud stroke starts sharp and its pitch drops as it fades.14 You hear pitch that moves with loudness: a hard guitar pluck’s twang, a gong’s glide; and a tamtam’s shimmer builds up as energy moves into higher modes.15

DetailsSoftening, stiffening, and what you hear uses no extra mathematics

The wide pendulum is nonlinear because \sin 2x \neq 2\sin x, so twice a solution is not one; out to 90^\circ a swing takes 18% longer than a small one (Wikipedia, Pendulum (mechanics)). A wide swing stretches a string or drumhead and raises its average tension. You hear the slight pitch drop of a loud kettledrum stroke (Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapters 5 and 18); a gong’s pitch glide, up for some gongs and down for others, depending on the shell’s curvature and thickness (N. H. Fletcher, “Nonlinear frequency shifts in quasispherical-cap shells: Pitch glide in Chinese gongs”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 78 (1985), 2069–2073); and a tamtam’s or cymbal’s shimmer building up after the strike, as the nonlinearity passes energy from low modes into high ones (K. A. Legge and N. H. Fletcher, “Nonlinearity, chaos, and the sound of shallow gongs”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 86 (1989), 2439–2442). In a modal system each mode’s amount evolves on its own, so energy cannot flow between modes.

5. Rayleigh’s self-sustained oscillator. In Rayleigh’s equation,16 x'' - \varepsilon\,\big(x' - \tfrac13 x'^3\big) + x = 0 with a small number \varepsilon > 0, the middle term feeds energy in when the motion is slow and takes it out when it is fast. Every motion except rest settles into one repeating cycle, a limit cycle.17 It has no modes; being periodic, its partials sit at exact whole-number multiples. You hear the steady, exactly harmonic tone of a bowed string, a reed or a player’s lips.

6. The infinite string. On the whole line, u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx} meets every hypothesis except finite size, and has no allowed eigenvectors (the deeper theory). You hear a click: a pluck splits into two half-height bumps that run apart, and each point falls still once they have passed.

PreciselyThe counterexamples in equations uses differential equations

Stability. An unstable rest, x'' = +x (A = -1), is modal, with the real exponents s = \pm1, but one mode runs away exponentially: the opening statement’s “stable” is A \ge 0, and after any drifts (\lambda = 0) are split off, A > 0.

The pumped swing. For x'' + (1 + \varepsilon\cos 2t)x = 0 the map from the state at time 0 to the state at time \pi is a fixed matrix P (the coefficient has period \pi). Floquet’s theorem (G. Floquet, “Sur les équations différentielles linéaires à coefficients périodiques”, Annales scientifiques de l’École Normale Supérieure, 2e série, 12 (1883), 47–88) writes every solution, when P has two distinct eigenvalues as here, as a combination of e^{\mu t}p(t) with p of period \pi and e^{\mu\pi} an eigenvalue of P (\mu may be complex). A numerical computation of P gives the growth rate \operatorname{Re}\mu = 0.0125, 0.0250, 0.0499 for \varepsilon = 0.05, 0.1, 0.2: about \varepsilon/4.

The slow change. For x'' + \omega(t)^2x = 0 with \omega changing slowly compared with one swing, x \approx (a/\sqrt{\omega(t)})\cos\big(\int_0^t\omega\,dt' + \varphi\big): the frequency follows \omega(t), and the energy divided by the frequency stays nearly constant (an adiabatic invariant).

The wide pendulum. The period of x'' = -\sin x with amplitude x_0 is 4K(\sin^2(x_0/2)), where K(m) = \int_0^{\pi/2}(1 - m\sin^2\phi)^{-1/2}\,d\phi, against 2\pi for small swings; at x_0 = 90^\circ the ratio is 1.180.

Rayleigh’s oscillator. A numerical run of x'' - \varepsilon(x' - x'^3/3) + x = 0 settles to angular frequency 0.99938, 0.9944, 0.9847 for \varepsilon = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, against 1 - \varepsilon^2/16 = 0.99938, 0.99438, 0.98438, with amplitude about 2. By contrast, a linear system driven by a periodic force is solved by its modes, and without friction the response at an exact resonance grows in proportion to time.

Spinning objects. Gyroscopic forces Gu' with G antisymmetric do no work and need no friction, yet the equation is not of the form Mu'' = -Ku: the classical theory of small oscillations covers “natural” mechanical systems, whose energy is kinetic plus stored, without such terms.

The surrounding air. Treated as not squeezable near a drumhead, the air adds a fixed, symmetric, positive added mass, a generalized eigenvalue problem AX = \omega^2(1 + M_{\text{air}}/\sigma)X inside level 1 once lengths are measured with the masses as weights, \langle x, (\sigma + M_{\text{air}})y\rangle, as in the unequal-masses fold. Exactly, the added mass depends on the frequency and is complex, because sound radiates away into unbounded air, and the modes become resonances that fade at the rate the head radiates energy.

No edges. On the whole line, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 has the continuous spectrum [0, \infty) and no eigenfunctions with finite \int|X|^2. With u_t(x, 0) = 0, d’Alembert’s solution u(x, t) = \tfrac12\big(f(x - ct) + f(x + ct)\big) shows the two half-height bumps; once both have passed a point, the string there is at rest.

Back to what you hear

For an instrument left to ring, your ear receives every frequency f_n and fading time \tau_n of its modes; only the amounts and phases change. Top down:

What you hear is the sound pressure at your ear, p(t) = \sum_n c_n\,e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_nt + \varphi_n), with amounts c_n and phases \varphi_n; post 1 reads pitch, loudness, timbre and duration off it.

p = h * F. The instrument’s body (a violin’s box, a piano’s soundboard), the air and the room form one linear filter that does not change in time, with tap response h and input F, so p(t) = \int h(t - t')F(t')\,dt'. For the reason in why modes exist, the filter sends e^{st} to a multiple of e^{st}: each partial keeps its f_n and \tau_n, and the filter only adds its own ringing, the body’s resonances and the room’s reverberation18 (fold).

18 Wikipedia, Reverberation.

F \leftarrow u. The input, such as a string’s push on its bridge, is read off the motion u at each instant, so it keeps every frequency and weights each mode.

u \leftarrow A. The motion is a sum of modes, one pair s = -1/\tau_n \pm 2\pi i f_n per eigenvalue of the operator A, shifted by friction and radiation. For an instrument left to ring, this is the only step that creates the frequencies; the playing, the route to the air and where you listen fix the c_n. Bowed and blown notes differ: the drive locks the motion into one repeating cycle, at exact multiples of the playing frequency.

ProofA filter keeps every exponent and adds its own uses integrals

A linear filter that is the same at every moment, at rest before the input F starts at t = 0, has output p(t) = \int_0^t h(t - t')\,F(t')\,dt', where h is its response to a sudden tap. Take a body with finitely many decaying resonances, h(t) = \sum_jb_je^{r_jt} with \operatorname{Re} r_j < 0, and the input F(t) = e^{st} with s different from every r_j. Then p(t) = \sum_j b_je^{r_jt}\int_0^te^{(s - r_j)t'}\,dt' = H(s)\,e^{st} - \sum_j\frac{b_j}{s - r_j}\,e^{r_jt}, \qquad H(s) = \sum_j\frac{b_j}{s - r_j} . The input’s exponent s, and with it its frequency and fading time, comes out unchanged, multiplied by the number H(s): its size scaled by |H(s)| and its phase shifted by the angle of H(s). The other terms are the filter’s own ringing, at its own exponents r_j. By linearity a sum of modes passes term by term, and a real input, the sum of a mode and its conjugate, gives a real output. A propagation delay d multiplies e^{st} by e^{-sd}, again the same exponent. A room is such a filter to a very good approximation at the pressures of music, as long as nothing in it moves (D. Masovic, Room Acoustics, lecture notes, TU Berlin, 2021).

DetailsPost 1’s four routes to a clear pitch, as statements about these lists

Post 1 lists four ways to partials at or very near whole-number multiples of one frequency.

  1. By nature: strings and air columns. On an interval with tied or open ends, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 gives f_n = nc/2L, for the two reasons given for the loop of string; a tube closed at one end, like a clarinet’s, gives the odd multiples (2n - 1)c/4L.
  2. By design. Added stiffness never lowers an eigenvalue and added mass never raises one (the min–max principle), so makers reshape the operator until the modes that matter land near the lines: a kettledrum’s air loading (R. S. Christian, R. E. Davis, A. Tubis, C. A. Anderson, R. I. Mills and T. D. Rossing, “Effects of air loading on timpani membrane vibrations”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 76 (1984), 1336–1345), a marimba bar’s undercut (N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 19), the tabla’s loaded patch (C. V. Raman, “The Indian musical drums”, Proceedings of the Indian Academy of Sciences A 1 (1934), 179–188), a bell’s profile, which puts most principal partials on a harmonic series but not the tierce, a minor third above the prime (Wikipedia, Strike tone).
  3. By suppression. A short \tau_n, like a kalimba tine’s fast-dying off-line overtone (D. M. F. Chapman, The sound of the African thumb piano (kalimba), Acoustics’08 Paris, 2008), or a small c_n and short \tau_n together, like a kettledrum’s off-line round modes, which the usual playing spot starts more weakly than the spoke modes (the drums in post 3) and which radiate their energy away fast (Fletcher and Rossing, chapter 18; T. A. Packer, Acoustics of the Orchestral Kettledrum, master’s thesis, University of Durham, 1993).
  4. By a periodic drive: bow and breath. Rayleigh’s oscillator, counterexample 5: a limit cycle, exactly periodic and so exactly harmonic; the resonator’s modes shape which partials are strong.
