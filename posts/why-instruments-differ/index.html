Question. Why does a piano have a pitch you can hum while a drum gives a thud? And why do the families of instruments sound so different?

Answer. The frequencies you hear are the instrument’s natural frequencies (page 3). A string’s or an air column’s natural frequencies are whole-number multiples of the lowest, so its sound repeats and has a clear pitch; a drum skin’s or a metal bar’s are not. The families also differ in which partials are strong and in how they start and fade, set by how the note is started, how the instrument pushes the air, and how it loses energy.

Natural frequencies

A natural frequency of an object is a frequency at which it can swing on its own, once set going and left alone, all of it in one fixed shape, its mode. A plucked or struck object swings in a sum of its modes, so by page 3 the frequencies f_n you hear are its natural frequencies. A bowed or blown note is different, and comes up below.

Question 1: a clear pitch

Put pages 2 and 3 together. A sound has a clear pitch when its partials sit at whole-number multiples of one frequency, or very nearly; and its partials are the instrument’s natural frequencies. So the question is why a string’s natural frequencies are whole-number multiples of the lowest and a drum’s are not.

A string. On a flexible, uniform string, a wave of any shape travels at the same speed, c = \sqrt{T/\mu}, where T is the tension and \mu the mass per unit length.1 A pluck sends waves running both ways. Each runs to an end, flips over, and comes back, and after the time 2L/c, there and back along the string’s length L, the whole string is back where it started. So the motion repeats exactly every 2L/c seconds, and a motion that repeats every 2L/c seconds is a sum of pure tones at whole-number multiples of c/(2L). These are the natural frequencies: f_n = n\,\frac{c}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots Mode n fits n half-waves between the ends, as the string’s row in the table shows.

1 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2 (the string: wave speed, d’Alembert’s solution and modes); Wikipedia, d’Alembert’s formula.

ProofThe string’s motion repeats every 2L/c seconds derivatives, integrals

The string’s height u(x, t) satisfies u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx} for 0 < x < L, with u = 0 at both ends (page 5 derives this equation). Let \phi and \psi be the starting height and speed, and let \Phi and \Psi be their extensions to the whole line that are odd about 0 and repeat with period 2L. Then d’Alembert’s formula u(x, t) = \tfrac12\big(\Phi(x + ct) + \Phi(x - ct)\big) + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{x - ct}^{x + ct}\Psi(y)\,dy solves the equation, matches the start, and is 0 at both ends because \Phi and \Psi are odd about 0 and about L; it is the only solution (W. A. Strauss, Partial Differential Equations: An Introduction, 2nd ed., Wiley, 2008, sections 2.1 and 3.2). Replace t by t + 2L/c: the arguments x \pm ct move by \pm 2L, one period of \Phi, and the integral gains two full periods of \Psi, whose integral over a period is 0 because \Psi is odd. So u(x, t + 2L/c) = u(x, t). A function of t with period P = 2L/c is a sum of sines and cosines of frequencies k/P = kc/(2L), its Fourier series.

The same holds for the air in a tube open at both ends, with c the speed of sound, because the pressure is 0 at an open end, as the height is at a fixed end of a string. A tube closed at one end, where the pressure’s slope is 0 instead, repeats every 4L/c and contains only the odd multiples of c/(4L): 1, 3, 5, …

An air column works the same way: sound in the tube travels at one speed for every wavelength. A flute, open at both ends, has natural frequencies close to 1 : 2 : 3 : 4; a clarinet, closed at the reed, close to 1 : 3 : 5 : 7.2 Odd multiples only, but whole numbers all the same.

2 J. Wolfe, Open vs closed pipes (flutes vs clarinets), UNSW.

3 D. A. Russell, Vibrational mode shapes of a circular membrane, Penn State: the modes (1,1), (2,1) and (0,2) at 1.593, 2.135 and 2.295 times the lowest.

A drum has no single length for waves to run along and back. Waves spread across the skin in two directions and reflect from a round rim, and the natural frequencies of an ideal round skin come out at 1 : 1.59 : 2.14 : 2.30 : 2.65 : \dots times the lowest:3 no whole-number pattern. Page 5 computes them.

A metal bar, such as a kalimba’s tine, is a single length but resists bending, not stretching, and bending waves are faster the shorter they are. The higher modes, with shorter waves, swing disproportionately fast: 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 for a bar clamped at one end.4 A piano wire is a string with a little of this stiffness, which pushes its higher partials slightly sharp: 1 : 2.001 : 3.005 : 4.013 at middle C.5

4 Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, chapter 2 (bars).

5 H. Fletcher, “Normal vibration frequencies of a stiff piano string”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 36 (1964), 203–209. The numbers here are for a steel wire 1 mm thick and 0.6 m long, tuned to middle C, with B = 4.4 \times 10^{-4}.

DerivationWhy stiffness spreads the frequencies derivatives

A wave \sin(kx - 2\pi f t), with k = 2\pi/\text{wavelength}, travels at the speed 2\pi f/k. On a string, u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx} requires 2\pi f = ck: the same speed c for every k. On a bar, the bending equation u_{tt} = -(EI/\rho S)\,u_{xxxx} (with E the steel’s Young’s modulus, I the second moment of area of its cross-section, \rho its density and S its cross-sectional area) requires 2\pi f = \sqrt{EI/\rho S}\,k^2: the speed grows in proportion to k. A bar clamped at x = 0 and free at x = L fits the waves with kL = \beta_n, the roots of \cos\beta\cosh\beta = -1, which are 1.875, 4.694, 7.855, \dots, close to (n - \tfrac12)\pi. So f_n \propto \beta_n^2, in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55. A piano wire has both terms, (2\pi f)^2 = c^2k^2 + (EI/\mu)k^4, and with k = n\pi/L this gives f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, where f_0 = c/(2L) and B = \pi^2EI/(TL^2) is small (Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., 1998, chapter 2; H. Fletcher, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 36 (1964), 203–209).

Four ways to a clear pitch

Not every instrument with a pitch has whole-number natural frequencies. Instruments get there in four ways.

  1. By nature. Strings and air columns, as above.
  2. By design. A uniform bar’s second mode is 2.76 times its first; a marimba bar is cut away underneath until it is 4 times.6
  3. By suppression. Off-line partials can be kept weak. A kalimba tine’s overtones, at 6.27 and 17.55 times its lowest, die within a fraction of a second, leaving the fundamental to ring for seconds.7 A strike at a still point of a mode does not start that mode at all.
  4. By a repeating drive. A bow or a breath feeds the motion all the time, and the motion settles into one repeating cycle, so its partials sit at exact whole-number multiples whatever the object’s own natural frequencies.8 The natural frequencies then decide which notes the instrument can lock onto and which partials are strong: a clarinet’s tube resonates at the odd multiples, so its odd partials are strong and its even ones weak.

8 J. Wolfe, How harmonic are harmonics?, UNSW.

A kettledrum combines these. The air in its bowl and around it shifts the skin’s modes with lines across it close to 2 : 3 : 4 : 5 times a common base; the round modes, which push net volume and so radiate strongly (page 3), give their energy away fast and die. What rings on is nearly harmonic, and players name the note an octave above that base.9

Question 2: why the families sound so different

Page 2 found three things in p(t) that make timbre, and page 3 traced where each comes from.

How the f_n are spaced: the natural frequencies, above. Whole numbers sound smooth (strings, winds); slightly stretched adds a shimmer (piano); far off the lines, a clang (bells, and the first instant of a kalimba note); no pattern at all, a thud (drum).

Which partials are strong, the ratios c_n/c_1, is a product of three factors, by page 3, c_n = |H(f_n)| \cdot (\text{route weight of mode } n) \cdot a_n:

  • How the note is started, the a_n. A pluck or strike at a still point of a mode does not start it: the table’s string, plucked a fifth of the way along, has no 5th partial, and the piano, struck an eighth of the way along, no 8th. A harder piano blow starts more high partials, because a fast hammer’s felt is stiffer and stays on the string for a shorter time.10
  • How the mode pushes the air. A string pushes the bridge in proportion to its slope there, which grows with n; a drum’s modes with lines across the skin push no net volume and radiate weakly; a tube passes on most strongly the partials at its natural frequencies, the odd ones for a clarinet.
  • The body. Fixed resonances of the body, such as a violin’s, favour the same bands of frequency whatever note is played, much as a voice’s formants do.11

10 A. Askenfelt and E. Jansson, “From touch to string vibrations” (the nonlinear hammer), in Five Lectures on the Acoustics of the Piano, Royal Swedish Academy of Music, 1990.

11 M. Schleske, Introduction to violin acoustics: sound.

How the sound starts and fades. Plucked and struck instruments start suddenly, and each partial then fades at its own rate; bowed and blown instruments build up over tens of milliseconds and hold steady while played. What radiates well dies fast: the kettledrum’s round modes, above.

In the table, compare the string with the clarinet: both have whole numbers, but different strong partials and a different start. Compare the piano with the kalimba: both start suddenly, but one has whole numbers and the other has overtones far off the lines that die almost at once.

Takeaway. The frequencies you hear are the instrument’s natural frequencies. Strings and air columns have natural frequencies at whole-number multiples of the lowest, because waves of every length travel along them at one speed, so their sound repeats and has a clear pitch; a drum skin, spread in two directions, and a stiff bar do not. Instruments without whole numbers get a pitch by design, by suppressing the off-line partials, or by a repeating drive. The families also differ in which partials are strong, set by the start, the route to the air and the body, and in how the sound starts and fades.
