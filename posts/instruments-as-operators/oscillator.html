Why does a fading note stay in tune?

1 · The spring

Modified

October 27, 2026

← 0 Note · The Shape of a Sound · Spectrum of an operator · Map · 2 Beads →

Builds on page 0, the note. Math: up to second derivatives. About 2 minutes for the summary; about 15 with the mathematics.

Pluck a guitar string and let it ring. Over a few seconds the vibration shrinks to almost nothing, yet the note’s pitch barely sags. Why doesn’t a smaller swing go slower? Start with the simplest thing that vibrates: a mass on a spring.

Listen first: change how far you pull the mass, and watch how long each bob takes.

The animation runs in real time. A real mass on a spring bobs a few times a second, too slowly to hear, so the sound plays the same motion 200 times faster.

The recipe on this instrument, in brief.

  1. Newton’s law with Hooke’s pull gives m\ddot x = -kx for one mass on a spring.
  2. The operator is the simplest there is: multiply the displacement by a number, k/m, the stiffness over the mass.
  3. Its spectrum is that one number, \lambda = k/m, giving one frequency, f = \sqrt{k/m}/2\pi, and no other.
  4. On the diagram: a single stem, a pure tone.
  5. What we hear: the size of the swing appears nowhere in the frequency, so a fading swing keeps its pitch, as long as the pull stays proportional to the stretch.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

1 · Newton’s law: the equation of motion

Mathematics used: second derivatives.

Every page of this series runs the same recipe: apply Newton’s law, mass times acceleration equals force, to one piece of the instrument; find that the force is a fixed rule applied to the instrument’s shape; read off that rule, the operator; find its spectrum; and from the spectrum, what we hear. The spectrum of an operator page sets out the recipe in general. For one mass on a spring, the “shape” is a single number, how far the mass is from rest.

1. The spring pulls back in proportion to the stretch. Twice as far, twice the pull: this is Hooke’s law. With x the distance from the resting position (for a hanging spring, the position where the spring’s pull balances gravity) and k the spring’s stiffness, the force is F = -k\,x , where the minus sign says the pull points back toward rest.

2. The pull sets the acceleration. Force equals mass times acceleration. Writing \ddot x for the acceleration, the second derivative of x with respect to time, m\,\ddot x = -k\,x .

What we assumed. The pull is exactly proportional to the stretch (Hooke’s law holds), the spring itself has no mass, and nothing else acts on the mass: no friction for now.

2 · The operator

Divide by the mass. The acceleration is the displacement multiplied by one fixed number, k/m, with its direction reversed: \ddot x = -\frac{k}{m}\,x .

The operator. \ddot x = -A\,x, where A is the rule “multiply by k/m”. For any displacement x, A\,x is the restoring force per unit mass with its direction reversed; the minus sign stays in the equation, as on every later page. There is nothing to hold at the ends here: a single mass has no boundary. In words: this is the smallest possible operator, acting on a single number.

3 · Its spectrum

A mode is a motion in which the shape keeps its form and only its size swings, at one frequency. In operator language, a mode’s shape X is one the operator only rescales, A X = \lambda X; the rescaling factor \lambda is an eigenvalue, and the list of all eigenvalues is the operator’s spectrum. Here every displacement is simply multiplied by k/m, so the spectrum is one number, \lambda = k/m. Now find the motion it allows.

1. Try a steady swing. Guess x = a\cos(\omega t + \phi), with amplitude a, starting phase \phi and angular frequency \omega in radians per second.

2. Differentiate twice and see what comes back. \cos \to -\omega\sin \to -\omega^2\cos: two derivatives give -\omega^2 times the start, \ddot x = -\omega^2\,a\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2\,x .

3. Match the equation. This equals -\lambda x exactly when \omega^2 = \lambda. The cosine repeats each time \omega t grows by 2\pi, so the motion repeats f = \omega/2\pi times a second: f = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} . The amplitude a and the phase \phi are free: they record how you started the motion, and they do not appear in f.

The spectrum. One eigenvalue, \lambda = k/m. One angular frequency, \omega = \sqrt{\lambda}, and one frequency, f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}, whatever the amplitude. In words: the same recipe, frequency = square root of an eigenvalue, divided by 2\pi, gives every frequency in the rest of the series.

ProofEvery motion is of this form, and the pitch cannot depend on amplitude uses the uniqueness of solutions of a differential equation

Every solution is a\cos(\omega t + \phi). The equation \ddot x = -\lambda x is linear: sums and multiples of solutions are solutions. A solution is determined by its starting position x(0) and starting velocity \dot x(0); this is the uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations. The functions \cos\omega t and \sin\omega t, with \omega = \sqrt\lambda, are solutions with starting data (1, 0) and (0, \omega). So the solution with data (x_0, v_0) is x_0\cos\omega t + (v_0/\omega)\sin\omega t. By the formula \cos(\omega t + \phi) = \cos\omega t\cos\phi - \sin\omega t\sin\phi, this equals a\cos(\omega t + \phi) with a = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2}, \cos\phi = x_0/a and \sin\phi = -v_0/(\omega a).

Amplitude cannot change the pitch. If x(t) is a solution, so is 2x(t), by linearity, and it has the same period.

4 · On the spectrum diagram

One eigenvalue means one stem on the pitch ruler: a pure tone, at f = \sqrt{k/m}/2\pi. The readout in the demo above is exactly this formula. The frequency goes as the square root of stiffness over mass: four times the stiffness doubles it, four times the mass halves it, and the size of the pull never enters. A note needs more than one stem; the next page finds where the others come from.

5 · What we hear

Twice the swing, the same time. The frequency formula contains k and m but not the amplitude. In plain words: take any motion of the mass and double it, twice as far from rest at every moment. The pull doubles too, so the acceleration doubles, which is exactly what the doubled motion needs. So the doubled motion is also a motion of the mass, and it repeats just as often. That is why a fading note keeps its pitch.

The motion is the one mode, x = a\cos(2\pi f t + \phi), and so is the sound: the demo plays exactly this cosine, sped up 200 times, fading slowly.

Friction fades the note but keeps the rate. Friction shrinks the swing, which is why a note fades, but light friction of the usual kind, proportional to speed, does not make the rate depend on the size of the swing either.

Step-by-step derivationThe motion with friction uses derivatives

Add a friction force proportional to the velocity, -2m\gamma\,\dot x, so that \ddot x = -(k/m) x - 2\gamma\dot x. Then x = a e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi) with \omega_d = \sqrt{k/m - \gamma^2} is a solution, as long as the friction is light (\gamma^2 < k/m); differentiating twice checks it. The swing shrinks, so the motion no longer repeats exactly, but the rate of its swings, \omega_d, still does not depend on a.

Why “barely” sags, not “never”. All of this needs the pull to be exactly proportional to the stretch. Pulled very far, a real spring stops obeying Hooke’s law, and then a bigger swing does change the rate (anharmonicity). A string shows a small version of this: a hard pluck stretches it, raising its tension, so the note starts very slightly sharp and settles as it fades (J. O. Smith, Physical Audio Signal Processing). That is why the pitch barely sags, rather than not at all. For the gentle vibrations of most playing, the linear law holds well.

What we found

Newton’s law with Hooke’s pull gave the equation m\ddot x = -kx; the operator is the smallest there is, multiply by k/m; its spectrum is that one number, and its square root, divided by 2\pi, is the one frequency, f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}: a single stem, a pure tone. The amplitude is not in it, which is why a fading note keeps its pitch, very nearly. One mass gives one stem on the pitch ruler; a note needs many. On the next page the one number becomes a table of numbers, a matrix, with many eigenvalues, and so many frequencies.
