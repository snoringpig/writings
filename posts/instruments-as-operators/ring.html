Builds on page 1: a restoring force of -\lambda x per unit mass gives the frequency \sqrt\lambda/2\pi. About 7 minutes, plus 3 for the folds.

The puzzle. Imagine beads threaded on a tight string whose two ends are joined into a ring, so that every bead has a neighbor on each side and none is special, and pluck one of them. The motion looks like a tangle. Yet it is made of a handful of perfectly regular motions, all happening at once. What are they, and how do you find them?

The demo starts with one bead plucked; watch the tangle. Then press the mode buttons one at a time. In each mode, every bead swings at the same frequency and the overall shape never changes, and the tangle is all of the modes at once. In the 1730s Daniel Bernoulli studied systems like this, weights strung along a flexible thread and a hanging chain, and came to believe that every vibration is built from such simple motions; in 1753 he put this forward as a general principle.

Displacements are drawn in and out of the circle so you can see them; on a real ring the beads move up and down, out of the page. Mode k is a wave that fits k times around the ring. Stems (the vertical lines): frequencies of the modes as multiples of mode 1's frequency, with height showing how much of each mode the current motion contains.

Untangling the motion

The force on one bead. Let u_j be the height of bead j, with beads spaced h apart along a string of tension T, each of mass m. Because the loop closes, bead N is next to bead 1, so indices are counted around the ring. (This is an idealization: a real loop of string would need something to hold it out against its own tension. The model keeps only the sideways pulls between neighbors, exactly as for a straight string whose two ends are joined.) For small displacements, Newton’s law for bead j is m\,\ddot u_j = \frac{T}{h}\left(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}\right).

Step-by-step derivationWhy the force is the difference between a bead and its neighbors
  1. The string between beads j and j+1 pulls bead j along itself with force T.
  2. Its slope is (u_{j+1} - u_j)/h. Let \theta be the angle that piece of string makes with the horizontal. For small slopes the vertical part of the pull is T\sin\theta \approx T\tan\theta = T(u_{j+1} - u_j)/h.
  3. The string on the other side contributes T(u_{j-1} - u_j)/h.
  4. Adding the two and applying Newton’s law gives the equation above. The bracket u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1} is twice the gap between the average of the neighbors and bead j itself: a bead sitting above its neighbors is pulled down, one sitting below is pulled up.

The assumptions are small slopes and a tension that does not change as the string flexes.

The matrix. Collect the heights into a column u = (u_1, \dots, u_N). The equations for all beads at once read \ddot u = -\frac{T}{mh}\,K u, where K is the N\times N matrix with 2 on the diagonal, -1 just above and below it, and -1 in the two corners, because bead 1 and bead N are neighbors. For N = 5, K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 & -1\\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & -1\\ -1 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Here Ku is the column whose j-th entry is row j of K times u: multiply each entry of row j by the matching entry of u, and add. Row j has 2 in place j and -1 in places j - 1 and j + 1, so (Ku)_j = 2u_j - u_{j-1} - u_{j+1}, and the matrix equation is the bead equation for every bead at once. Each row of K is the row above shifted one place to the right, wrapping around; a matrix with this property is called circulant.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues. A nonzero column v is an eigenvector of K, with eigenvalue \lambda, if Kv = \lambda v: the matrix only rescales it, possibly by zero. The set of all eigenvalues is the spectrum of K.

Modes. If Kv = \lambda v, then u(t) = v\cos(\omega t) is a motion of the beads, provided \omega^2 = \frac{T}{mh}\lambda. To check, differentiate twice: \ddot u = -\omega^2 v\cos\omega t, while -\frac{T}{mh}Ku = -\frac{T}{mh}\lambda v\cos\omega t. Every bead swings at the same angular frequency \omega (radians per second; page 1), in the fixed shape v. This is the mass on a spring again, once for each eigenvector.

A small example, three beads. For N = 3, K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1\\ -1 & 2 & -1\\ -1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Row j of this K gives (Kv)_j = 3v_j - (v_1 + v_2 + v_3). The column (1, 1, 1) gives K(1,1,1) = (0,0,0), so it is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0: every bead moves up together, nothing stretches, nothing pulls back, and the frequency is 0, so the ring just drifts. Any column whose entries add up to zero gives (Kv)_j = 3v_j, so Kv = 3v. Every such column is a combination a(1, -1, 0) + b(0, 1, -1), so these eigenvectors fill a whole plane: every one is built from those two shapes, and neither of the two is a multiple of the other. The eigenvalues are 0, 3, 3, with 3 counted twice: it has multiplicity 2.

Any number of beads. Try the column v_j = \cos(2\pi jk/N), a wave with k crests around the ring. The identity \cos(a + b) + \cos(a - b) = 2\cos a\cos b, with a = 2\pi jk/N and b = 2\pi k/N, gives v_{j+1} + v_{j-1} = 2\cos(2\pi k/N)\,v_j, so (Kv)_j = 2v_j - v_{j+1} - v_{j-1} = \big(2 - 2\cos(2\pi k/N)\big)\,v_j = 4\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi k}{N}\Big)\,v_j , using 1 - \cos 2x = 2\sin^2 x. The same holds for v_j = \sin(2\pi jk/N). These are the shapes the mode buttons play, and the eigenvalues are 4\sin^2(\pi k/N). At the beads, a wave with N - k crests looks exactly like one with k crests (the cosine is the same and the sine changes sign), so the different eigenvalues come from k = 0, 1, \dots up to N/2, rounded down, and each one from k = 1 up to just below N/2 has two eigenvectors, a cosine and a sine. (At k = 0, and at k = N/2 when N is even, the sine is zero at every bead, so only the cosine is left.) Writing any motion as a sum of these waves splits it into separate masses on springs, one per wave; that splitting is the discrete Fourier transform.

Complex formThe same waves as e^{2\pi i jk/N} uses complex numbers

With Euler’s formula e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x, the cosine and sine waves combine into v_j = e^{2\pi i jk/N} for k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1 (here i is the imaginary unit and j the bead number). Then (Kv)_j = \big(2 - e^{2\pi ik/N} - e^{-2\pi ik/N}\big)v_j = 4\sin^2(\pi k/N)\,v_j in one line. These N waves are the basis vectors of the discrete Fourier transform, and they are eigenvectors of every circulant matrix, not only this one. Combined with the time factor e^{\mp i\omega t}, the waves with k and N - k crests travel around the ring in opposite directions.

Not quite whole numbers. The frequencies are proportional to \sin(\pi k/N), which is not proportional to k, so the beads are not exactly harmonic. For small k, \sin(\pi k/N) \approx \pi k/N, so the low modes are nearly harmonic: raise N in the demo and watch the low stems approach the whole-number grid. The modes near k = N/2 stay far from it.

ProofEvery motion is a sum of modes uses linear algebra

The matrix K is symmetric: the entry in row i, column j equals the entry in row j, column i. By the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices, K has a basis of N mutually orthogonal (perpendicular) eigenvectors v^{(1)}, \dots, v^{(N)} with real eigenvalues \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N. Write the motion as u(t) = \sum_k a_k(t)\,v^{(k)}. Substituting into \ddot u = -\frac{T}{mh}Ku and taking the dot product with v^{(k)}, which removes every other term because they are perpendicular, gives \ddot a_k = -\omega_k^2 a_k with \omega_k^2 = \frac{T}{mh}\lambda_k: each coefficient obeys its own mass-on-a-spring equation. With starting shape u(0) = \sum_k c_k v^{(k)} and starting velocity \dot u(0) = \sum_k d_k v^{(k)}, and by the uniqueness of solutions from page 1, every motion is u(t) = \sum_k \big(c_k\cos\omega_k t + \frac{d_k}{\omega_k}\sin\omega_k t\big)v^{(k)}, read as c_k + d_k t when \omega_k = 0. The eigenvalues are nonnegative because u \cdot Ku = \sum_j u_j(2u_j - u_{j+1} - u_{j-1}) = \sum_j (u_{j+1} - u_j)^2 \ge 0, by regrouping the terms around the ring; for an eigenvector this equals \lambda\,(u\cdot u).

What we found

Many coupled masses move in a few special shapes, called modes, each swinging at its own frequency. Finding them means finding the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of a matrix, the operator \frac{T}{mh}K, whose list of eigenvalues, its spectrum, gives the frequencies. On a ring, the modes are exactly the waves of the discrete Fourier transform.

The next puzzle

The low frequencies of the beads are almost whole-number multiples of the lowest, and they get closer as you add beads. What happens with infinitely many, a continuous loop of string? Do the frequencies become exactly whole numbers? And can every shape of the loop be built from these waves?
