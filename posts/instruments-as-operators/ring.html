How can a tangled wobble be a few simple motions?

2 · Beads on a ring

Modified

October 27, 2026

← 1 Spring · The Shape of a Sound · Spectrum of an operator · Map · 3 Loop →

Builds on page 1, the spring. Math: up to matrices (explained). About 3 minutes for the summary; about 35 with the mathematics.

Thread beads on a tight string and join the ends into a ring. Pluck one bead and the ring wobbles in a tangle, and sounds like a jangle. Start it in just the right shape, though, and it swings cleanly and sounds one pure tone. Where were the clean tones hiding in the jangle?

Listen first: press “Hear this motion” on the tangle, then press a mode button and hear it again.

Mode k is a wave that fits k times around the ring; in mode 0 all the beads just move together. The beads are drawn moving in and out of the circle; real ones move out of the page.

In one sentence. With many masses the operator is a table of numbers (a matrix); the shapes it only rescales (its eigenvectors) swing as independent springs, and any motion is a mix of them.

The recipe on this instrument, in brief. Stages 2 and 3 start with just two beads, the smallest case, then go on to any number.

  1. Newton’s law for one bead: it is pulled toward the average of its two neighbors.
  2. The operator is a matrix, A = \frac{T}{mh}K, acting on the list of all the beads’ heights; the ring closing on itself is its boundary condition.
  3. Its spectrum: the eigenvectors are waves around the ring; the wave with k crests has eigenvalue \frac{4T}{mh}\sin^2(\pi k/N), so its frequency grows more slowly than the number of crests.
  4. On the diagram: the low modes sit near the whole-number lines, the high ones far off.
  5. What we hear: a pluck starts many waves at once, each at its own frequency, so the jangle is a mix of pure tones off the whole-number lines, and each clean tone is one eigenvector.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

1 · Newton’s law: the equation of motion

Mathematics used: the small-angle approximation and multiplying a table of numbers by a list (explained).

As on page 1, apply Newton’s law to one piece of the instrument, here one bead, and find the force. It will turn out to depend on the bead’s neighbors as well as on the bead itself.

Let u_j be the height of bead j, with N beads, each of mass m, spaced h apart along a string of tension T. Because the loop closes, bead N is next to bead 1, so bead numbers are counted around the ring. (An idealization: the model keeps only the sideways pulls between neighbors, as for a straight string whose two ends are joined.)

1. The string on one side pulls the bead toward its neighbor. The string between beads j and j+1 pulls bead j along itself with force T. That piece of string rises by u_{j+1} - u_j over a distance h, so its slope is (u_{j+1} - u_j)/h. If the string makes a small angle \theta with the horizontal, the upward part of the pull is T\sin\theta; for a small angle \sin\theta \approx \tan\theta (both are about \theta in radians), and \tan\theta is the slope. So the vertical part of the pull is T\sin\theta \approx T\tan\theta = \frac{T}{h}\,(u_{j+1} - u_j) .

2. The string on the other side does the same. Its vertical part is \frac{T}{h}\,(u_{j-1} - u_j) .

3. The two pulls set the bead’s acceleration. Adding them and setting the total equal to mass times acceleration, m\,\ddot u_j = \frac{T}{h}\left(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}\right). The bracket is twice the gap between the average of the two neighbors and bead j itself: a bead sitting above its neighbors is pulled down, one sitting below is pulled up. So each bead is a mass on a spring, except that where its spring pulls it depends on where its neighbors are.

A bead next to a tied-down end is pulled the same way, with that end counting as a neighbor at height 0.

What we assumed. Small slopes, a tension that does not change as the string flexes, point-like beads on a massless string, no friction, and the idealized closed ring described above.

2 · The operator

Start with two beads, ends tied. Take two beads on a string whose two ends are tied down, at heights x_1 and x_2. Step 3 of stage 1, with the tied ends as neighbors at height 0, gives m\,\ddot x_1 = \frac{T}{h}\,(0 - 2x_1 + x_2), \qquad m\,\ddot x_2 = \frac{T}{h}\,(x_1 - 2x_2 + 0) . Each bead’s acceleration depends on where the other bead is: the two equations are tangled together. Collect the two heights into one column x = (x_1, x_2), and the two equations become one, \ddot x = -A\,x, \qquad A = \frac{T}{mh}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . The product Ax is worked out row by row: the first entry is the first row, (2, -1), times the column (x_1, x_2), that is \frac{T}{mh}(2x_1 - x_2), which is minus the first equation’s right side divided by m; the second row gives the second equation. Notice that the table is the same read across or down: entry (1, 2) equals entry (2, 1). One stretch of string joins the two beads and feels only the difference of their heights, so moving bead 2 up by one unit pulls bead 1 exactly as hard as moving bead 1 up by one unit pulls bead 2. A table with this property is called symmetric.

The same two beads, closed into a ring. Now join the string’s two ends to each other instead of tying them down. Both of a bead’s neighbors are now the other bead: bead 1 is joined to bead 2 by the string on its left and again by the string on its right. So m\,\ddot x_1 = \frac{T}{h}(x_2 - x_1) + \frac{T}{h}(x_2 - x_1), and the same for bead 2: A_{\text{ring}} = \frac{T}{mh}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . The same beads on the same string, held a different way, give a different operator: how the beads are held is part of the operator.

Any number of beads on a ring. The force on each bead depends on its neighbors, so the rule that turns the shape into the force acts on all the beads at once. Collect the heights into one column u = (u_1, \dots, u_N). The N equations become one, \ddot u = -\frac{T}{mh}\,K\,u , where K is a square table of numbers, a matrix, with 2 down its diagonal (top left to bottom right), -1 just above and below it, and -1 in the two corners, because bead 1 and bead N are neighbors. For N = 5, K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 & -1\\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & -1\\ -1 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Here Ku is the column whose j-th entry is row j of K times u: multiply each entry of row j by the matching entry of u, and add. Row j has 2 in place j and -1 in places j - 1 and j + 1, so (Ku)_j = 2u_j - u_{j-1} - u_{j+1}, which is the bead equation for every bead at once. Each row of K is the row above shifted one place to the right, wrapping around; a matrix with this property is called circulant. The two corner entries are the ring’s boundary condition: they are where the loop closes.

The operator. \ddot u = -A\,u, \qquad A = \frac{T}{mh}\,K , a matrix acting on the list of all the beads’ heights, with the ring closed (bead N next to bead 1). In words: on page 1 the operator was one number; here it is a table of numbers, and A\,u is the list of restoring forces per unit mass, reversed.

3 · Its spectrum

A mode is a shape that keeps its form while it swings. Most starting shapes change as they move: that is the tangle. Start again with the two tied beads.

1. Two beads: a lucky guess. The two beads are mirror images of each other, which suggests adding and subtracting their equations. With the sum S = x_1 + x_2 and the difference D = x_1 - x_2, adding gives \ddot S = -\frac{T}{mh}\big((2x_1 - x_2) + (2x_2 - x_1)\big) and subtracting gives \ddot D = -\frac{T}{mh}\big((2x_1 - x_2) - (2x_2 - x_1)\big), that is, \ddot S = -\frac{T}{mh}\,S, \qquad \ddot D = -\frac{3T}{mh}\,D . The tangle has come apart into two independent springs, each the spring of page 1. In S the beads move up and down together, one arch, and the stretch of string between them stays level. In D they move in opposite directions, two arches, and each bead is also pulled by the middle stretch, which is tilted by twice the bead’s height: three times as stiff in all. Going back, x_1 = (S + D)/2 and x_2 = (S - D)/2, so every motion of the two beads is some S-swing plus some D-swing, at the frequencies \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{T/mh} and \sqrt 3 times that. Their ratio, 1 : 1.73, is already off the whole numbers.

In the plane of the two heights, with x_1 across and x_2 up, a mixed motion traces a tangled path; measured along the tilted directions S and D, the same motion is two separate back-and-forth swings.

2. Why the good directions are eigenvectors. Guessing worked because the two beads are mirror images. In general, look for a motion that keeps its shape and only changes its size: x(t) = v\,q(t), a fixed column v times a number q(t) that changes with time. Newton’s law \ddot x = -Ax becomes \ddot q\,v = -q\,Av. At any moment when q \neq 0, this says Av = (-\ddot q/q)\,v: the matrix only rescales v. Since A and v do not change with time, the rescaling factor -\ddot q/q cannot change either; call it \lambda. So A v = \lambda\, v, \qquad \ddot q = -\lambda\, q . A nonzero column v with Av = \lambda v is called an eigenvector of A, and \lambda its eigenvalue; the list of all the eigenvalues is the matrix’s spectrum. And \ddot q = -\lambda q is page 1’s spring: q = \cos(\omega t + \phi) with \omega^2 = \lambda, so the shape v swings at angular frequency \omega = \sqrt{\lambda}, frequency f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi. The directions in which the beads split into independent springs are exactly the eigenvectors. Since A = \frac{T}{mh}K, the eigenvectors of A are those of the simpler table K: if Kv = s\,v, then Av = \lambda v with \lambda = \frac{T}{mh}\,s.

3. Finding them by hand. For the two tied beads K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Write v = (a, b). The two rows of Kv = s\,v say 2a - b = sa and -a + 2b = sb, that is, b = (2 - s)\,a and a = (2 - s)\,b. Here a \neq 0, since otherwise the first equation gives b = 0 and v would be zero. Putting the first equation into the second, a = (2 - s)^2 a, so (2 - s)^2 = 1: s = 1 or s = 3. For s = 1, b = a: the column (1, 1), the direction S. For s = 3, b = -a: the column (1, -1), the direction D. Check: K(1, 1) = (2 - 1, -1 + 2) = (1, 1) and K(1, -1) = (3, -3) = 3\,(1, -1). The eigenvalues of A are T/mh and 3T/mh, the two springs of step 1.

4. The ring of two beads. For the ring, K_{\text{ring}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Now K_{\text{ring}}(1, 1) = (0, 0): an eigenvector with s = 0. Both beads move up together, nothing stretches, nothing pulls back, and the frequency is 0, so the ring just drifts, with no sound. And K_{\text{ring}}(1, -1) = (4, -4): s = 4, the beads swinging oppositely. Tied ends give eigenvalues proportional to 1 and 3; the ring gives 0 and 4. The way the beads are held changed the spectrum.

Slowed down. Tied ends: S swings at 1×, D at 1.73× (√3). Closed into a ring: S has nothing pulling it back, so it only drifts (here it just stays shifted), and D swings at 2×. In the plane on the right, the path of a mix is a tangle, while S and D each run straight along their own tilted line.

5. For any number of beads on a ring, the special shapes are waves around the ring. On a ring no bead is special, so try waves that fit a whole number of times around: v_j = \cos(2\pi jk/N), a wave with k crests. The identity \cos(a + b) + \cos(a - b) = 2\cos a\cos b, with a = 2\pi jk/N and b = 2\pi k/N, gives v_{j+1} + v_{j-1} = 2\cos(2\pi k/N)\,v_j, so \begin{aligned} (Kv)_j &= 2v_j - v_{j+1} - v_{j-1} \\ &= \big(2 - 2\cos(2\pi k/N)\big)\,v_j \\ &= 4\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi k}{N}\Big)\,v_j , \end{aligned} using 1 - \cos 2x = 2\sin^2 x. The same holds for v_j = \sin(2\pi jk/N). These are the shapes the mode buttons play. At the beads, a wave with N - k crests looks exactly like one with k crests (the cosine is the same and the sine changes sign), so the different eigenvalues come from k = 0, 1, \dots up to N/2, rounded down, and each one from k = 1 up to just below N/2 has two eigenvectors, a cosine and a sine. (At k = 0, and at k = N/2 when N is even, the sine is zero at every bead, so only the cosine is left.) A wave with more crests bends more sharply from bead to bead, so each bead is pulled back harder and the wave swings faster.

The spectrum. Eigenvectors: the waves \cos(2\pi jk/N) and \sin(2\pi jk/N) with k crests. Eigenvalues of A: \lambda_k = \frac{4T}{mh}\,\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi k}{N}\Big), \qquad f_k = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_k}}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{T}{mh}}\,\sin\frac{\pi k}{N} . In words: mode k sounds at \sin(\pi k/N)/\sin(\pi/N) times mode 1.

In the 1730s Daniel Bernoulli studied systems like this, weights strung along a flexible thread and a hanging chain, and came to believe that every vibration is built from such simple motions; in 1753 he put this forward as a general principle. For the beads it is a theorem, and writing a motion as a sum of these waves is the discrete Fourier transform.

ProofEvery motion is a sum of modes uses linear algebra

The matrix A is symmetric, so the hub’s Result applies word for word: its eigenvectors form a perpendicular basis, each amount obeys its own mass-on-a-spring equation, and the starting shape and velocity fix the motion. The one fact specific to the ring is that the eigenvalues are nonnegative: u \cdot Ku = \sum_j u_j(2u_j - u_{j+1} - u_{j-1}) = \sum_j (u_{j+1} - u_j)^2 \ge 0, by regrouping the terms around the ring, and for an eigenvector of A this gives \lambda\,(u\cdot u) = \frac{T}{mh}\,u\cdot Ku \ge 0.

PreciselyThe same waves, written with complex numbers uses complex numbers

With Euler’s formula e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x, the cosine and sine waves combine into v_j = e^{2\pi i jk/N} for k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1 (here i is the imaginary unit and j the bead number). Then (Kv)_j = \big(2 - e^{2\pi ik/N} - e^{-2\pi ik/N}\big)v_j = 4\sin^2(\pi k/N)\,v_j in one line. These N waves are the basis vectors of the discrete Fourier transform, and they are eigenvectors of every circulant matrix, not only this one. Combined with the time factor e^{\mp i\omega t}, the waves with k and N - k crests travel around the ring in opposite directions.

4 · On the spectrum diagram

The stems are the frequencies f_k from the result box, as multiples of mode 1’s, the slowest wave that swings back and forth: mode k stands at \sin(\pi k/N)/\sin(\pi/N). In the demo at the top, each stem’s height is how much of that mode the current motion contains; below, every mode is drawn equally tall. Apart from mode 1 itself, they generally miss the lines. With 8 beads, as the demo starts, the modes sit at 1×, 1.85×, 2.41× and 2.61×. Raise the number of beads and the low stems close in on the lines: with 24 beads, modes 2 and 3 are within 2.5% of 2× and 3×. The high modes stay far off.

Each mode's frequency as a multiple of mode 1's, every mode drawn equally tall: 8 beads (top), 24 beads (bottom). Adding beads pulls the low modes onto the lines; the high ones stay off.

5 · What we hear

The jangle is a mix of pure tones. Pluck one bead and the starting shape is not a wave; but it is a sum of waves, and each wave swings at its own frequency. So the plucked ring sounds all those frequencies at once. They sit off the lines, so they don’t fuse into one note: a jangle. Start the ring in one wave’s shape and only that one frequency sounds: a pure tone. The clean tones were in the jangle all along, as its eigenvectors.

Any motion of the ring is a sum of modes, u(t) = \sum_k a_k\, v^{(k)}\cos(2\pi f_k t + \phi_k), each swinging at its own frequency as if it were alone (the proof is in the fold above). The demo’s sound is exactly this sum: it adds the modes’ frequencies f_k, with mode 1 set to 196 Hz, each as strong as the motion contains it.

Almost whole numbers. A long wave, with few crests, spreads over many beads, and to it the beads look like a smooth string; its frequency grows in step with its number of crests, almost exactly as whole numbers do. A short wave, with crests only a bead or two apart, feels the gaps between the beads, and its frequency falls behind. That is why the low stems sit near the lines and close in as you add beads, while the high ones stay off.

PreciselyHow near to whole numbers uses the sine function

Mode k has frequency proportional to \sin(\pi k/N), which is not proportional to k: the beads are not exactly harmonic. For small k, \sin(\pi k/N) \approx \pi k/N, so the low modes are nearly harmonic, and the ratio \sin(\pi k/N)/\sin(\pi/N) tends to k as N grows. The modes near k = N/2 stay far from the whole-number grid: for an even number of beads the largest ratio is 1/\sin(\pi/N) (slightly less for an odd number), and for any number of beads it is about N/\pi once there are many, not N/2. A mode can land on a line by coincidence: with 6 beads, the top mode sits at exactly 2×.

What we found

With many masses the operator is a matrix, and its eigenvectors are the directions in which the motion splits into independent springs: for two tied beads, together and opposite, at 1 and √3 times; for N beads on a ring, waves with \lambda_k = \frac{4T}{mh}\sin^2(\pi k/N). Any motion, even a tangle, is a mix of these springs, and on the spectrum diagram the low ones sit near the lines but not on them.
