How can a tangled wobble be a few simple motions?

2 · Beads on a ring

Modified

October 27, 2026

← 1 Spring · The Shape of a Sound · Spectrum of an operator · Map · 3 Loop →

Builds on page 1, the spring. Math: up to matrices (explained). About 3 minutes for the summary; about 25 with the mathematics.

Thread beads on a tight string and join the ends into a ring. Pluck one bead and the ring wobbles in a tangle, and sounds like a jangle. Start it in just the right shape, though, and it swings cleanly and sounds one pure tone. Where were the clean tones hiding in the jangle?

Listen first: press “Hear this motion” on the tangle, then press a mode button and hear it again.

Mode k is a wave that fits k times around the ring; in mode 0 all the beads just move together. The beads are drawn moving in and out of the circle; real ones move out of the page.

The recipe on this instrument, in brief.

  1. Newton’s law for one bead: it is pulled toward the average of its two neighbors.
  2. The operator is a matrix, A = \frac{T}{mh}K, acting on the list of all the beads’ heights; the ring closing on itself is its boundary condition.
  3. Its spectrum: the eigenvectors are waves around the ring; the wave with k crests has eigenvalue \frac{4T}{mh}\sin^2(\pi k/N), so its frequency grows more slowly than the number of crests.
  4. On the diagram: the low modes sit near the whole-number lines, the high ones far off.
  5. What we hear: a pluck starts many waves at once, each at its own frequency, so the jangle is a chord, and each clean tone is one of its eigenvectors.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

1 · Newton’s law: the equation of motion

Mathematics used: the small-angle approximation and multiplying a table of numbers by a list (explained).

As on page 1, apply Newton’s law to one piece of the instrument, here one bead, and find the force. It will turn out to depend on the bead’s neighbors as well as on the bead itself.

Let u_j be the height of bead j, with N beads, each of mass m, spaced h apart along a string of tension T. Because the loop closes, bead N is next to bead 1, so bead numbers are counted around the ring. (An idealization: the model keeps only the sideways pulls between neighbors, as for a straight string whose two ends are joined.)

1. The string on one side pulls the bead toward its neighbor. The string between beads j and j+1 pulls bead j along itself with force T. That piece of string rises by u_{j+1} - u_j over a distance h, so its slope is (u_{j+1} - u_j)/h. If the string makes a small angle \theta with the horizontal, the upward part of the pull is T\sin\theta; for a small angle \sin\theta \approx \tan\theta (both are about \theta in radians), and \tan\theta is the slope. So the vertical part of the pull is T\sin\theta \approx T\tan\theta = \frac{T}{h}\,(u_{j+1} - u_j) .

2. The string on the other side does the same. Its vertical part is \frac{T}{h}\,(u_{j-1} - u_j) .

3. The two pulls set the bead’s acceleration. Adding them and setting the total equal to mass times acceleration, m\,\ddot u_j = \frac{T}{h}\left(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}\right). The bracket is twice the gap between the average of the two neighbors and bead j itself: a bead sitting above its neighbors is pulled down, one sitting below is pulled up. So each bead is a mass on a spring, except that where its spring pulls it depends on where its neighbors are.

What we assumed. Small slopes, a tension that does not change as the string flexes, point-like beads on a massless string, no friction, and the idealized closed ring described above.

2 · The operator

The force on each bead depends on its neighbors, so the rule that turns the shape into the force acts on all the beads at once. Collect the heights into one column u = (u_1, \dots, u_N). The N equations become one, \ddot u = -\frac{T}{mh}\,K\,u , where K is a square table of numbers, a matrix, with 2 down its diagonal (top left to bottom right), -1 just above and below it, and -1 in the two corners, because bead 1 and bead N are neighbors. For N = 5, K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 & -1\\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & -1\\ -1 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Here Ku is the column whose j-th entry is row j of K times u: multiply each entry of row j by the matching entry of u, and add. Row j has 2 in place j and -1 in places j - 1 and j + 1, so (Ku)_j = 2u_j - u_{j-1} - u_{j+1}, which is the bead equation for every bead at once. Each row of K is the row above shifted one place to the right, wrapping around; a matrix with this property is called circulant. The two corner entries are the ring’s boundary condition: they are where the loop closes.

The operator. \ddot u = -A\,u, \qquad A = \frac{T}{mh}\,K , a matrix acting on the list of all the beads’ heights, with the ring closed (bead N next to bead 1). In words: on page 1 the operator was one number; here it is a table of numbers, and A\,u is the list of restoring forces per unit mass, reversed.

3 · Its spectrum

A mode is a shape that keeps its form while it swings. Most starting shapes change as they move: that is the tangle. But in a few special shapes, every bead is pulled back in proportion to its own height, by the same factor for every bead. Then every bead is a mass on the same spring, they all swing in step, and the shape only grows and shrinks. In matrix language, a nonzero column v is an eigenvector of A, with eigenvalue \lambda, if A v = \lambda\, v , so the matrix only rescales it, possibly by zero. The list of all the eigenvalues is the matrix’s spectrum. Since A = \frac{T}{mh}K, the eigenvectors of A are those of K: if Kv = s\,v, then Av = \lambda v with \lambda = \frac{T}{mh}\,s. So it is enough to find the eigenvectors of the simpler matrix K.

1. Each eigenvector swings like one mass on a spring. If Av = \lambda v, try u(t) = v\cos(\omega t). Differentiating twice gives \ddot u = -\omega^2 v\cos\omega t, while -Au = -\lambda\, v\cos\omega t. These agree when \omega^2 = \lambda . Every bead swings at the same angular frequency \omega = \sqrt{\lambda} (radians per second; page 1), in the fixed shape v: the mass on a spring again, once for each eigenvector, at frequency f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi.

2. With three beads, find the special shapes by hand. For N = 3, K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1\\ -1 & 2 & -1\\ -1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Row j gives (Kv)_j = 3v_j - (v_1 + v_2 + v_3). The column (1, 1, 1) gives K(1,1,1) = (0,0,0): an eigenvector with s = 0. Every bead moves up together, nothing stretches, nothing pulls back, and the frequency is 0, so the ring just drifts; this is the mode 0 of the demo. Any column whose entries add up to zero gives (Kv)_j = 3v_j, so Kv = 3v. Every such column is a combination a(1, -1, 0) + b(0, 1, -1), so these eigenvectors fill a whole plane: every one is built from those two shapes, and neither of the two is a multiple of the other. The eigenvalues of K are 0, 3, 3, with 3 counted twice: it has multiplicity 2. Those of A are 0 and 3T/mh, twice.

3. For any number of beads, the special shapes are waves around the ring. On a ring no bead is special, so try waves that fit a whole number of times around: v_j = \cos(2\pi jk/N), a wave with k crests. The identity \cos(a + b) + \cos(a - b) = 2\cos a\cos b, with a = 2\pi jk/N and b = 2\pi k/N, gives v_{j+1} + v_{j-1} = 2\cos(2\pi k/N)\,v_j, so \begin{aligned} (Kv)_j &= 2v_j - v_{j+1} - v_{j-1} \\ &= \big(2 - 2\cos(2\pi k/N)\big)\,v_j \\ &= 4\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi k}{N}\Big)\,v_j , \end{aligned} using 1 - \cos 2x = 2\sin^2 x. The same holds for v_j = \sin(2\pi jk/N). These are the shapes the mode buttons play. At the beads, a wave with N - k crests looks exactly like one with k crests (the cosine is the same and the sine changes sign), so the different eigenvalues come from k = 0, 1, \dots up to N/2, rounded down, and each one from k = 1 up to just below N/2 has two eigenvectors, a cosine and a sine. (At k = 0, and at k = N/2 when N is even, the sine is zero at every bead, so only the cosine is left.) A wave with more crests bends more sharply from bead to bead, so each bead is pulled back harder and the wave swings faster.

The spectrum. Eigenvectors: the waves \cos(2\pi jk/N) and \sin(2\pi jk/N) with k crests. Eigenvalues of A: \lambda_k = \frac{4T}{mh}\,\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi k}{N}\Big), \qquad f_k = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_k}}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{T}{mh}}\,\sin\frac{\pi k}{N} . In words: mode k sounds at \sin(\pi k/N)/\sin(\pi/N) times mode 1.

In the 1730s Daniel Bernoulli studied systems like this, weights strung along a flexible thread and a hanging chain, and came to believe that every vibration is built from such simple motions; in 1753 he put this forward as a general principle. For the beads it is a theorem, and writing a motion as a sum of these waves is the discrete Fourier transform.

ProofEvery motion is a sum of modes uses linear algebra

The matrix A is symmetric: the entry in row i, column j equals the entry in row j, column i. By the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices, A has a basis of N mutually orthogonal (perpendicular) eigenvectors v^{(1)}, \dots, v^{(N)} with real eigenvalues \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N. Write the motion as u(t) = \sum_k a_k(t)\,v^{(k)}. Substituting into \ddot u = -Au and taking the dot product with v^{(k)}, which removes every other term because they are perpendicular, gives \ddot a_k = -\lambda_k a_k: each coefficient obeys its own mass-on-a-spring equation, with \omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}. With starting shape u(0) = \sum_k c_k v^{(k)} and starting velocity \dot u(0) = \sum_k d_k v^{(k)}, and by the uniqueness of solutions from page 1, every motion is u(t) = \sum_k \big(c_k\cos\omega_k t + \frac{d_k}{\omega_k}\sin\omega_k t\big)v^{(k)}, read as c_k + d_k t when \omega_k = 0. The eigenvalues are nonnegative because u \cdot Ku = \sum_j u_j(2u_j - u_{j+1} - u_{j-1}) = \sum_j (u_{j+1} - u_j)^2 \ge 0, by regrouping the terms around the ring; for an eigenvector of A this gives \lambda\,(u\cdot u) = \frac{T}{mh}\,u\cdot Ku \ge 0.

PreciselyThe same waves, written with complex numbers uses complex numbers

With Euler’s formula e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x, the cosine and sine waves combine into v_j = e^{2\pi i jk/N} for k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1 (here i is the imaginary unit and j the bead number). Then (Kv)_j = \big(2 - e^{2\pi ik/N} - e^{-2\pi ik/N}\big)v_j = 4\sin^2(\pi k/N)\,v_j in one line. These N waves are the basis vectors of the discrete Fourier transform, and they are eigenvectors of every circulant matrix, not only this one. Combined with the time factor e^{\mp i\omega t}, the waves with k and N - k crests travel around the ring in opposite directions.

4 · On the spectrum diagram

The stems are the frequencies f_k from the result box, as multiples of mode 1’s, the slowest wave that swings back and forth: mode k stands at \sin(\pi k/N)/\sin(\pi/N). In the demo at the top, each stem’s height is how much of that mode the current motion contains; below, every mode is drawn equally tall. Apart from mode 1 itself, they generally miss the lines. With 8 beads, as the demo starts, the modes sit at 1×, 1.85×, 2.41× and 2.61×. Raise the number of beads and the low stems close in on the lines: with 24 beads, modes 2 and 3 are within 2.5% of 2× and 3×. The high modes stay far off.

Each mode's frequency as a multiple of mode 1's, every mode drawn equally tall: 8 beads (top), 24 beads (bottom). Adding beads pulls the low modes onto the lines; the high ones stay off.

5 · What we hear

The jangle is a chord. Pluck one bead and the starting shape is not a wave; but it is a sum of waves, and each wave swings at its own frequency. So the plucked ring sounds all those frequencies at once. They sit off the lines, so the chord is a jangle. Start the ring in one wave’s shape and only that one frequency sounds: a pure tone. The clean tones were in the jangle all along, as its eigenvectors.

Any motion of the ring is a sum of modes, u(t) = \sum_k a_k\, v^{(k)}\cos(2\pi f_k t + \phi_k), each swinging at its own frequency as if it were alone (the proof is in the fold above). The demo’s sound is exactly this sum: it adds the modes’ frequencies f_k, with mode 1 set to 196 Hz, each as strong as the motion contains it.

Almost whole numbers. A long wave, with few crests, spreads over many beads, and to it the beads look like a smooth string; its frequency grows in step with its number of crests, almost exactly as whole numbers do. A short wave, with crests only a bead or two apart, feels the gaps between the beads, and its frequency falls behind. That is why the low stems sit near the lines and close in as you add beads, while the high ones stay off.

PreciselyHow near to whole numbers uses the sine function

Mode k has frequency proportional to \sin(\pi k/N), which is not proportional to k: the beads are not exactly harmonic. For small k, \sin(\pi k/N) \approx \pi k/N, so the low modes are nearly harmonic, and the ratio \sin(\pi k/N)/\sin(\pi/N) tends to k as N grows. The modes near k = N/2 stay far from the whole-number grid: for an even number of beads the largest ratio is 1/\sin(\pi/N) (slightly less for an odd number), and for any number of beads it is about N/\pi once there are many, not N/2. A mode can land on a line by coincidence: with 6 beads, the top mode sits at exactly 2×.

What we found

Newton’s law for each bead gave the equation m\ddot u_j = \frac{T}{h}(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}); written for all the beads at once, it reads \ddot u = -Au with the operator A = \frac{T}{mh}K, a matrix whose corner entries close the ring. Its eigenvectors are waves around the ring, its eigenvalues are \lambda_k = \frac{4T}{mh}\sin^2(\pi k/N), and their square roots, divided by 2\pi, are the modes’ frequencies. On the spectrum diagram the low modes sit near the lines but not on them. Any motion, even a tangle, is a sum of modes: the jangle you hear is that chord, and each clean tone is one eigenvector.
