Why is a clarinet almost an octave deeper than a flute?

5 · The clarinet and the flute

Modified

October 27, 2026

← 4 String · The Shape of a Sound · Spectrum of an operator · Map · 6 Kalimba →

Builds on page 4, the violin string. Math: up to mixed partial derivatives (explained). About 3 minutes for the summary; about 20 with the mathematics.

A clarinet and a flute are about the same length. Yet the clarinet’s lowest note is almost an octave below the flute’s. And when players overblow, making the tube jump to its next mode, the flute jumps up an octave while the clarinet jumps an octave and a fifth. The same equation governs the air in both tubes. What is different?

Listen first: hold the blow button on the clarinet, then switch the same tube to a flute and blow again. The register key (on the flute it stands in for blowing harder) makes either tube jump to its next mode.

Shading: how much the pressure swings, dark where it swings most, pale where it stays at room pressure. The first open hole acts, roughly, as the end of the tube.

The recipe on this instrument, in brief.

  1. Newton’s law on a thin slab of air, with air’s resistance to being squeezed, gives the wave equation p_{tt} = c^2 p_{xx} for the pressure.
  2. The operator is the string’s, A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, now acting on pressure; only the ends differ: room pressure at an open end, no pressure slope at the clarinet’s nearly closed reed.
  3. Its spectrum: the flute’s frequencies are n\,c/2L, the clarinet’s (2n-1)\,c/4L: half as low, and odd multiples only.
  4. On the diagram: both blown sounds sit on the lines; the clarinet’s strong stems are the odd ones.
  5. What we hear: the clarinet sounds almost an octave below the flute and overblows to three times its lowest frequency, an octave and a fifth.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

1 · Newton’s law: the equation of motion

Mathematics used: partial derivatives, including mixed ones (explained).

The air in a tube is a long column of tiny slabs, each pushed back and forth by the pressure on its two faces. We apply Newton’s law, mass times acceleration equals force, to one slab, and combine it with the way air pushes back when it is squeezed.

1. How far the air moves. Let \xi(x, t) be how far the air that normally sits at x has moved along the tube, and let the tube’s cross-section have area S. Let p(x, t) be the air pressure in the tube minus room pressure. Subscripts are partial derivatives: \xi_t is the rate of change in time with x held fixed, \xi_x the rate of change along the tube with t held fixed.

2. A pressure difference pushes the slab. The slab of air between x and x + dx has mass \rho S\,dx, where \rho is the density of air. The only force on it along the tube is the pressure difference between its two faces, S\,[p(x) - p(x + dx)] \approx -S\,p_x\,dx. Mass times acceleration equals force, divided by S\,dx: \rho\,\xi_{tt} = -p_x .

3. Squeezed air pushes back. The slab’s two faces move by \xi(x) and \xi(x + dx), so its length changes by \xi(x + dx) - \xi(x) \approx \xi_x\,dx: it is stretched by the fraction \xi_x, and its pressure drops in proportion, p = -K\,\xi_x , where K is the bulk modulus, air’s resistance to being squeezed (this K has nothing to do with the bead matrix of page 2). In a sound wave the squeezing is too fast for heat to flow, which is called adiabatic; for air this makes K about 1.4 times atmospheric pressure.

4. A pressure slope pushes the air, and moving air changes the pressure: together they make a wave. To combine steps 2 and 3, differentiate both. A mixed subscript means differentiating more than once in turn: \xi_{ttx} means twice in t, then once in x. For a smooth motion the order does not matter, so \xi_{xtt} = \xi_{ttx}. Differentiate step 2 once in x: \rho\,\xi_{ttx} = -p_{xx} . Differentiate step 3 twice in t: p_{tt} = -K\,\xi_{xtt} = -K\,\xi_{ttx}. Put in \xi_{ttx} = -p_{xx}/\rho from the line above, and \xi is gone: p_{tt} = \frac{K}{\rho}\,p_{xx} . It is the string’s wave equation again, with pressure in place of sideways displacement and c = \sqrt{K/\rho}, the speed of sound, about 343 m/s at 20 °C, in place of the string’s wave speed.

What we assumed. The tube is a cylinder, narrow compared with the sound’s wavelength, so the pressure is the same across it; the pressure changes are small; friction with the walls is left out. A real clarinet’s bore is not exactly cylindrical, and its holes and bell matter; they refine the numbers below, not the picture.

2 · The operator

Read the equation as a rule: the pressure’s rate of change, twice over in time, is c^2 times its curvature along the tube. So the operator is the string’s, A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, now acting on pressure shapes, and the equation reads p_{tt} = -A\,p. What differs between a flute and a clarinet is only which shapes the operator may act on: the ends.

The ends. At an open end the air in the tube meets the room, so the pressure there is room pressure: p = 0. This is approximate: the air just outside moves with the air inside, so the tube acts as if it were slightly longer, by about 0.6 of its radius at each open end, an amount called the end correction. At the clarinet’s reed the opening is nearly shut, so the air there cannot move back and forth. Air that never moves has no acceleration, so by step 2 the pressure has no slope there: p_x = 0. The air piles up and thins out against the closed end, and the pressure swings most there. The first condition is the string’s Dirichlet condition; the second, a condition on the slope, is called a Neumann condition.

The tube’s operator. \begin{gathered} p_{tt} = -A\,p, \qquad A = -c^2\,\frac{d^2}{dx^2}, \\ c^2 = \frac{K}{\rho} \approx (343\ \text{m/s})^2, \end{gathered} acting on pressure shapes along a tube of length L. Flute, open at both ends: p(0) = p(L) = 0. Clarinet, closed at the reed (x = 0) and open at the bell: p_x(0) = 0 and p(L) = 0. In words: the same rule as the string, with different ends.

3 · Its spectrum

A mode is a pressure shape X(x) that the operator only rescales, A X = \lambda X; it swings at the frequency f = \sqrt\lambda/2\pi, and the list of all the eigenvalues is the spectrum. The ends decide which shapes are allowed.

1. Which shapes does the operator only rescale? Writing \lambda = c^2k^2, the mode equation -c^2X'' = \lambda X becomes X'' = -k^2X, solved by the waves X = a\cos kx + b\sin kx, \qquad f = \frac{\sqrt\lambda}{2\pi} = \frac{ck}{2\pi} .

2. The flute: room pressure at both ends. X(0) = 0 forces a = 0, and X(L) = 0 needs \sin kL = 0, so kL = n\pi: one half wave, two half waves, and so on. The eigenvalues are \lambda_n = (n\pi c/L)^2 and the frequencies f_n = n\,c/2L.

3. The clarinet: no pressure slope at the reed, room pressure at the bell. X'(0) = bk = 0 at the reed forces b = 0, leaving X = \cos kx, and X(L) = 0 at the open end needs \cos kL = 0, so kL = (2n - 1)\pi/2: one quarter wave, three quarter waves, and so on. The eigenvalues are \lambda_n = \big((2n-1)\pi c/2L\big)^2 and the frequencies f_n = (2n-1)\,c/4L.

The two spectra.

Flute, open at both ends: modes \sin(n\pi x/L), eigenvalues (n\pi c/L)^2, frequencies f_n = n\,c/2L, that is 1, 2, 3, 4, … times the lowest.

Clarinet, closed at the reed and open at the bell: modes \cos\big((2n-1)\pi x/2L\big), eigenvalues \big((2n-1)\pi c/2L\big)^2, frequencies f_n = (2n-1)\,c/4L, that is 1, 3, 5, 7, … times the lowest.

The lowest frequencies are c/2L and c/4L: a ratio of exactly 2 for the same ideal tube.

ProofAnother way to see the odd multiples: the closed tube is a quarter of a loop uses the circle’s eigenfunctions (page 3)

Use the reflections from the string page. Take a pressure shape p on [0, L] with p_x(0) = 0 and p(L) = 0.

  1. Reflect it evenly across the reed end, p(-x) = p(x). A smooth even shape has zero slope at 0, so even reflection is what the Neumann condition p_x(0) = 0 allows, so the result is a shape on [-L, L] that vanishes at both ends.
  2. Reflect that oddly across x = L, p(2L - x) = -p(x), and repeat. The result is a shape on a loop of circumference 4L.
  3. On that loop the eigenfunctions are \cos(k\pi x/2L) and \sin(k\pi x/2L). Step 1 keeps only cosines. Step 2 needs \cos\big(k\pi(2L - x)/2L\big) = (-1)^k\cos(k\pi x/2L) to equal -\cos(k\pi x/2L), which keeps only odd k.

So the clarinet’s modes are the odd multiples of a loop four tube-lengths long, which is where both the factor 4 and the odd numbers come from.

5 · What we hear

Almost an octave deeper. The flute’s lowest mode, \sin(\pi x/L), has room pressure at both ends and swings most in the middle: half a wavelength fits in the tube. The clarinet’s, \cos(\pi x/2L), swings most at the reed and has room pressure at the open end: only a quarter of a wavelength fits. A wave twice as long has half the frequency, so the same ideal tube sounds exactly an octave lower closed at one end. Real flutes and clarinets differ a little in length, the clarinet’s mouthpiece and bell change its effective length, and the open ends act slightly longer than they are, so the real interval comes out a bit less than an octave.

A blown tube does not ring and fade like a plucked string: the reed or the air jet keeps feeding it, and the air settles into one motion that repeats, at a frequency close to the tube’s sounding mode from stage 3. The demo’s sound is made that way: a repeating wave at that mode’s frequency, with the strong odd multiples and faint even ones described below.

Overblowing: an octave or a twelfth. The flute’s spectrum has every whole-number multiple of its lowest frequency; the clarinet’s has only the odd ones, with nothing in between. So when a clarinetist presses the register key, the tube jumps to its next mode, 3 times its lowest frequency, an octave and a fifth, called a twelfth (a fifth is the ratio 3/2). (The demo keeps the low-register tone color when the register key is on.) A flutist overblows by blowing harder, and the flute’s next mode is 2 times its lowest, an octave. That is why clarinet fingerings repeat a twelfth apart and flute fingerings an octave apart.

DetailsHow the register key picks out the 3× mode

The clarinet’s register key opens a small hole near the top of the tube. An open hole lets air in and out, pulling the pressure there toward room pressure, which spoils the fundamental, a mode that needs pressure variation at that point. For one note in the lower part of the low register (the notes played without the key), the hole sits about a third of the way down the sounding tube, right where that note’s 3f_1 mode has no pressure variation anyway, so the open hole does not disturb that mode (Wolfe). For the other notes the hole is off that point, well off for the lowest and highest notes you play with the key (Wolfe), and a second effect helps: the air in a small hole has mass, and the faster it must move back and forth, the harder it is to move. At the fundamental’s lower frequency it moves more easily, so the hole leaks enough to spoil that mode; at the higher frequency of the 3f_1 mode it moves much less, so the hole acts almost as a plug. Either way the tube jumps to the 3f_1 mode.

Why the sound still lands on every line. The reed is a valve: when the pressure in the mouthpiece drops it closes, and when the pressure rises it opens, so the air column drives the reed that feeds it. In normal playing this settles into a periodic oscillation, a sound that repeats, at a playing frequency close to the tube’s lowest mode. A sound that repeats has frequencies only at whole-number multiples of its repetition rate (page 0), but it may have all of them. So the tube’s modes are odd multiples only, while the clarinet’s sound has even multiples too: weak in the low register (the lowest notes), where no mode of the tube supports them, but not absent.

What we found

Newton’s law on a slab of air, together with air’s resistance to being squeezed, gave the wave equation for the pressure. Read as a rule, it is the string’s operator, A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, acting on pressure; only the ends differ. With room pressure at both ends, the flute’s spectrum gives frequencies n\,c/2L: every whole-number multiple. With the air held still at the reed, the clarinet’s gives (2n-1)\,c/4L: half the lowest frequency and only odd multiples. On the diagram both blown sounds sit on the lines, because they repeat; what we hear is the octave between them, and the twelfth a clarinet jumps when overblown.

Same operator, other ends. Stopped organ pipes and pan pipes are closed at one end like the clarinet and share its odd-multiples spectrum. Open organ pipes share the flute’s. Oboes and saxophones have cone-shaped tubes instead of cylinders, a different operator, which this series does not cover.
