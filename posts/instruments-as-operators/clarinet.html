The clarinet

Part 2 of Instruments as Operators

Series · 0 Foundations · 1 Violin · 2 Clarinet · 3 Kalimba · 4 Piano · 5 Drum

The violin’s operator again, now acting on air pressure in a tube. Only the boundary condition at one end changes, and half the spectrum disappears.

Play it

Tap holes or pick a fingering, then hold the button (or the space bar; keys 1–7 change fingering). The shading inside the bore is the pressure of the sounding mode: dark at a pressure antinode, pale at a node. The first open hole acts as the end of the tube.

Deriving the operator

  1. Follow a thin slab of air in a tube of cross-section A. Let \xi(x,t) be how far the air at x has moved along the tube, and p(x,t) the pressure above room pressure.
  2. The slab between x and x+dx has mass \rho A\,dx, and the pressure difference across it is the only force, so Newton’s law reads \rho\,\xi_{tt} = -p_x.
  3. If the slab stretches by the fraction \xi_x, its pressure drops in proportion. Sound compresses air too quickly for heat to flow, so the constant is the adiabatic bulk modulus K = \gamma P_0, and p = -K\,\xi_x.
  4. Differentiate step 2 in x and step 3 twice in t, and eliminate \xi.
TipThe air column’s operator

p_{tt} = c^2 p_{xx}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{K}{\rho} \approx (343\ \text{m/s})^2 .

  1. At an open end the air meets the room, so p = 0. At the reed the opening is nearly shut, so the air cannot move: \xi = 0 there, and by step 2, p_x = 0.

Spectrum

The condition p_x(0) = 0 at the reed leaves X = \cos kx, and p(L) = 0 at the open end requires \cos kL = 0. So k_n = \frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L}, \qquad f_n = \frac{(2n-1)\,c}{4L}.

Only odd multiples survive. A flute is open at both ends: p = 0 at both ends gives \sin(n\pi x/L) and f_n = nc/2L, every multiple. Two consequences, both audible above:

  • An octave lower. The same tube sounds c/4L closed at one end but c/2L open at both. Switch the type and listen.
  • A twelfth, not an octave. The register key makes the tube jump to its next mode. For the clarinet that is 3f_1, an octave plus a fifth; for the flute it is 2f_1. This is why clarinet fingerings repeat a twelfth apart.

Like the bow, the reed is a valve driven by the pressure it creates, so the oscillation is periodic and its partials sit exactly on n f_1. In the low register the even ones are weak, not zero. A real bore is not perfectly cylindrical and the bell matters; those refine the numbers, not the picture.
