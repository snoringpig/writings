A piano string is a violin string that is also a little bit of a kalimba tine. The two restoring forces add, and the overtones creep sharp.

Tap a key, or use the computer keys A W S E D F T G Y H U J K for C4 to C5.

At stiffness 0 every string is ideal and the partials sit on the integers. The default stiffness rises toward the treble, as on a real piano; its size here is illustrative.

Deriving the operator

A piano wire is under heavy tension, like a violin string, and it is thick steel, so it also resists bending, like a kalimba tine. Forces per unit length simply add: the tension term from Part 0 and the bending term from Part 3 give \mu\,u_{tt} = T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \mathcal{L} = -c^2\partial_x^2 + \kappa^2\partial_x^4 .

Treat the ends as pinned: they cannot move and transmit no bending moment, so u = u_{xx} = 0. Real piano strings are held closer to clamped, which shifts the numbers slightly but not the picture.

Spectrum

Pinned ends make \sin(n\pi x/L) an eigenfunction of both terms at once. Substituting it gives \omega^2 = c^2k^2 + \kappa^2 k^4 with k = n\pi/L, so f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, \qquad B = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{T L^2}.

The stiffness constant B is small, but it multiplies n^2, so high partials run noticeably sharp. Since I grows like the fourth power of the wire’s diameter and B falls like 1/L^2, short thick treble strings are the stiffest.

Partials of the last key

Hammer at 1/8 of the length, so the 8th and 16th partials nearly vanish.

Why tuners stretch octaves

A tuner sets an octave by listening for beats between the lower note’s second partial and the upper note’s fundamental. Stiffness pushes that second partial above 2f_0, so a beat-free octave is slightly wider than 2 : 1. Repeated across the keyboard, this is stretch tuning: the top of a piano is tuned sharp of equal temperament, and the bottom flat.

Listen for the slow wobble in the 2 : 1 version. Raise the stiffness above to speed it up.
