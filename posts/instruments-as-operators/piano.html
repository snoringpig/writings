The puzzle. Piano tuners don’t tune octaves to an exact frequency ratio of 2 : 1. They stretch them slightly, so the top of a piano ends up sharp and the bottom flat, compared with equal temperament, the textbook tuning. An octave tuned to exactly 2 : 1 on a piano wobbles. Why would exact sound wrong?

Listen to an octave tuned to exactly 2 : 1, then to a stretched one. The slow wobble in loudness is called a beat.

At first each note keeps only its lowest partials (C4: 1 to 3, C5: 1 and 2), so the one beat a tuner listens for stands out: the exactly 2 : 1 octave wobbles at the rate shown, and the stretched one is steady. The stiffness starts at about 6 times a real middle C wire's so that the wobble is quick enough to hear; at 1 it is a slow swell every few seconds. Every real string is a little stiff. Guitar and harp strings belong here too, faintly. The piano is where stiffness is large enough to hear, and large enough to change how pianos are tuned.

Two restoring forces at once

The operator. Forces per unit length add. Tension contributes T\,u_{xx}, as for the string, and bending contributes -EI\,u_{xxxx}, as for the kalimba, so with \mu the mass per unit length (density times cross-section area, the \rho S of the kalimba page) \mu\,u_{tt} = T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad u_{tt} = -\mathcal{L}u, \quad \mathcal{L} = -c^2\partial_x^2 + \kappa^2\partial_x^4 , with c^2 = T/\mu and \kappa^2 = EI/\mu. Here \partial_x^2 means two derivatives in x, the same as the subscript xx, and \mathcal{L} is this page’s operator, playing the role A played before. Treat the ends as pinned: held in place but free to pivot, so u = 0 and, since a pivot exerts no bending moment and the moment is EI\,u_{xx}, also u_{xx} = 0. Real piano strings are held a little more firmly than that, which shifts the numbers slightly but not the picture.

Step-by-step derivationFrom Newton’s law on a short piece of wire to the operator and its spectrum uses partial derivatives

1. One small piece. Follow the stretch of wire between x and x+dx. Its mass is \mu\,dx and its vertical acceleration is u_{tt}, so Newton’s law says \mu\,dx\,u_{tt} equals the net vertical force on it. Two forces act: the pull of tension, and the wire’s resistance to bending.

2. Tension. The rest of the string pulls on each end of the piece with force T, along the string.

At the right end the pull points along the slope u_x(x+dx), so for small slopes its vertical part is T\sin\theta \approx T\tan\theta = T\,u_x(x+dx). At the left end it is -T\,u_x(x). Together they give T\,[u_x(x+dx) - u_x(x)] \approx T\,u_{xx}\,dx. A curved piece feels a net pull toward its center of curvature; a straight piece feels none.

3. Bending. The wire also resists bending, exactly like the kalimba’s tine. The kalimba page derives the bending force per unit length, -EI\,u_{xxxx}, where E is steel’s Young’s modulus and I = \pi d^4/64 is the second moment of area of a round wire of diameter d.

4. Newton’s law. Adding the two forces gives \mu\,dx\,u_{tt} = T\,u_{xx}\,dx - EI\,u_{xxxx}\,dx. Dividing by \mu\,dx, with c^2 = T/\mu and \kappa^2 = EI/\mu, u_{tt} = -\mathcal{L}u, \qquad \mathcal{L} = -c^2\partial_x^2 + \kappa^2\partial_x^4 .

5. Ends. The bridge at one end and a metal bearing point at the other hold the wire in place, so u = 0. If they let the wire pivot freely, they exert no moment, so M = EI\,u_{xx} = 0. These are the pinned conditions u = u_{xx} = 0.

6. Spectrum. Try u = \sin(kx)\cos(\omega t) with k = n\pi/L, which satisfies both pinned conditions. Then u_{xx} = -k^2u and u_{xxxx} = k^4u, so the equation becomes -\omega^2 = -c^2k^2 - \kappa^2k^4, that is \omega^2 = c^2k^2\Big(1 + \frac{EI}{T}\,k^2\Big) = \Big(\frac{n\pi c}{L}\Big)^2\Big(1 + \frac{\pi^2EI}{TL^2}\,n^2\Big). Taking square roots gives f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, with f_0 = c/2L and B = \pi^2EI/TL^2.

7. How big is B? For a middle C wire, roughly d = 1 mm and L = 0.6 m, with steel’s E = 2\times10^{11} Pa and density 7850 kg/m³; a tension of about 610 N then gives middle C. So I \approx 4.9\times10^{-14} m^4 and B \approx 4.4\times10^{-4}. The 10th partial, at 10f_0\sqrt{1 + 100B}, then sits 1200\log_2\sqrt{(1 + 100B)/(1 + B)} \approx 37 cents sharp of ten times the fundamental f_0\sqrt{1 + B}. These numbers are rough; real strings vary.

8. What was assumed. Small slopes; tension unchanged as the wire flexes; the wire’s rotational inertia neglected (Rayleigh’s correction adds a small term); a uniform wire.

Spectrum. Pinned ends make \sin(n\pi x/L) an eigenfunction of both terms at once, so here the two operators’ eigenvalues simply add; that is special to pinned ends, where the two operators share their eigenfunctions, and with clamped ends the formula needs a small correction. Substituting gives \omega^2 = c^2k^2 + \kappa^2 k^4 with k = n\pi/L, so f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, \qquad B = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{T L^2}, where f_0 = c/2L is the lowest frequency the same string would have without stiffness. The actual fundamental is f_1 = f_0\sqrt{1 + B}.

The stiffness constant B is small, but it multiplies n^2, so high partials run noticeably sharp: the string is slightly inharmonic. For a middle C wire B is about 4.4\times10^{-4}, and the 10th partial sits about 37 cents sharp of ten times the fundamental; the fold above has the arithmetic. Since I grows like the fourth power of the wire’s diameter and B falls like 1/L^2, short treble strings, thick for their length, are the stiffest.

Why exact octaves beat

A tuner sets an octave by listening for beats, a slow wobble in loudness, between the lower note’s second partial and the upper note’s fundamental. Beats happen when two nearly equal frequencies add, at a rate equal to their difference. The lower note’s fundamental is f_0\sqrt{1+B} and its second partial is 2f_0\sqrt{1+4B}. Their ratio is 2\sqrt{(1+4B)/(1+B)} \approx 2\,(1 + 2B)(1 - B/2) \approx 2\,(1 + 3B/2), using \sqrt{1+x} \approx 1 + x/2 and 1/\sqrt{1+x} \approx 1 - x/2 for small x: the second partial is more than twice the fundamental by a factor of about 1 + 3B/2. An upper note tuned to exactly twice the fundamental therefore sits a little below that second partial, and the two beat. To silence the beats the tuner raises the upper note until its fundamental matches the lower note’s second partial, which makes the octave slightly wider than 2 : 1. Tuners also listen to other pairs, such as the lower note’s fourth partial against the upper note’s second, and stiffness makes it impossible to silence all of them at once, so real tuning is a compromise. Repeated across the keyboard, this is stretch tuning: compared with equal temperament, in which every semitone is exactly the ratio 2^{1/12}, the top of a piano is tuned sharp and the bottom flat.

That answers the opening puzzle: on a stiff string, “exactly 2 : 1” lines up the wrong pair of frequencies.

Hammer, dampers and pedals

A second question: what do the pedals actually do to the sound? None of them moves a frequency, apart from tiny shifts explained below.

The operator is only part of the string’s equation. A real string is also pushed by the hammer and loses energy to damping; here f(x,t) is a force, not a frequency: \mu\,u_{tt} + b(x,t)\,u_t = T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx} + f(x,t).

Each term has one job. The operator, T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx}, fixes the frequencies and the mode shapes. The forcing f is the hammer, and it fixes how much of each mode is excited. The damping b is the felt dampers plus small internal losses, and it fixes how fast each mode dies. Every control a pianist touches acts on f or on b. Only the tuner’s wrench, which changes T, touches the operator.

Both effects are read off the mode shapes X_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L). A hammer blow at x_h gives mode n a weight proportional to X_n(x_h), the mode’s own height at the hammer. The drum on the next page uses the same rule. A damper felt resting on a short stretch W of the string makes mode n’s swing shrink like e^{-\gamma_n t}, and for weak damping (to first order in b, that is, ignoring terms in b^2) the decay rate is \gamma_n = \frac{\int_W b\,X_n^2\,dx}{2\mu\int_0^L X_n^2\,dx}. A mode is damped in proportion to how much it moves under the felt, and its frequency shifts only by an amount proportional to \gamma_n^2, negligible for weak damping. A mode with a node under the felt barely notices the damper: in the view below, slide the damper to L/8, where the hammer also sits, and the 8th mode, which has a still point there, is not excited and barely damped.

Step-by-step derivationWhere the hammer weights and the damping rates come from uses integrals of products of modes and a first-order approximation

1. Expand in modes. Write u(x,t) = \sum_n q_n(t)\,X_n(x), with \mathcal{L}X_n = \omega_n^2X_n and the pinned conditions.

2. Project. Substitute into the full equation, multiply by X_m and integrate along the string. The operator is symmetric with these boundary conditions (in fact self-adjoint), by integrating by parts as on the circle page, so the X_n are orthogonal: \int_0^L X_mX_n\,dx = 0 for m \ne n. The acceleration and operator terms collapse to one term each: \mu N_m\,(\ddot q_m + \omega_m^2 q_m) + \sum_n D_{mn}\,\dot q_n = F_m(t), with N_m = \int X_m^2\,dx, D_{mn} = \int b\,X_mX_n\,dx and F_m = \int f\,X_m\,dx.

3. The hammer. The felt touches a short stretch near x_h, so F_m(t) \approx X_m(x_h)\,P(t), where P(t) is the total force. Every mode gets the same push in time, weighted by X_m(x_h): the mode’s own height at the hammer.

4. The damper. If the damping is weak, the cross terms D_{mn} with m \ne n change the answer only at second order. Keeping the diagonal leaves a damped oscillator for each mode, \ddot q_m + 2\gamma_m\,\dot q_m + \omega_m^2 q_m = 0, \qquad \gamma_m = \frac{D_{mm}}{2\mu N_m}, whose solution e^{-\gamma_m t}\cos\big(\sqrt{\omega_m^2 - \gamma_m^2}\,t\big) decays at the rate \gamma_m while its frequency moves only by order \gamma_m^2/\omega_m.

5. Honest limits. A real damper is not weak: it stops a note in a fraction of a second. The formula gives the right picture, that modes are damped by how much they move under the felt, but not exact decay times.

On a grand piano the middle pedal is usually the sostenuto, which holds up only the dampers of keys already down. Most notes have two or three strings, and Weinreich showed that their coupling through the bridge, each string’s motion slightly shaking the others, produces the piano’s two-stage decay: a loud start that fades fast, then a quiet, long aftersound. A grand’s soft pedal strikes fewer of the strings, so the coupled motion starts differently. This model leaves the coupling out.

Where the dampers touch the string matters, and the formula for \gamma_n says why. Mode n is damped in proportion to its motion under the felt. Close to an end every low mode barely moves, so a damper there hardly damps the bass. In most modern uprights the dampers sit below the hammers, nearer the middle of the string; in older “birdcage” uprights they sat above the hammers, near the end. Piano restorers report that the under-damper design stops the strings better, partly because its dampers are closer to the middle of the string (Sykes). Reader questionWhat does a damper do, and what would a piano without dampers sound like?

Without dampers, releasing a key would do nothing: every string would ring until it died on its own, exactly as if the sustain pedal were always down. On a real piano each key lifts a felt damper off its strings; letting go drops the damper back and stops the note within a fraction of a second. The sustain pedal lifts all the dampers at once, so released notes keep ringing. The keyboard above models both. The top 20 keys have no dampers at all, as on a real piano: those short strings die out quickly by themselves.

What we found

Operators add. A piano wire is pulled tight like a string and resists bending like a bar, so its operator is the sum of the two, and its overtones run slightly sharp of the whole numbers. The rest of a real string’s equation, damping and forcing, is where the hammer, the dampers and the pedals act, and none of them moves a frequency, apart from tiny shifts that weak damping causes.
