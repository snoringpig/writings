The puzzle. Below is a note at 220 hertz. Remove its 220 Hz component completely and play it again. The pitch does not change. If a note’s pitch is not one of its frequencies, what is it?

Mix your own

The wave over three repetitions; gray ticks mark each repetition, every 1/220 of a second. Stems (the vertical lines): how strong each component is, at 1, 2, 3, … times 220 Hz.

Watch the wave while you switch between the two. Removing the 220 Hz component changes the wave’s shape, but not how often it repeats: still every 1/220 of a second. The components left are at 440, 660, 880, … Hz, and each of them goes through a whole number of cycles in 1/220 of a second (2, 3, 4, …), so together they still repeat after exactly that time. They do not all fit into 1/440 of a second: 660 Hz would need one and a half cycles. That repetition is what you hear as the pitch. The effect is called the missing fundamental, the fundamental being the component at the repetition rate itself, and it is why a deep voice keeps its pitch over an ordinary telephone line, which cannot carry sounds below about 300 Hz.

A note is a pattern that repeats

Sound is air pressure rising and falling. When the pattern repeats every P seconds, with P the shortest such time, you hear a note, and its pitch is the repetition rate f = 1/P, measured in hertz, repetitions per second. This holds for repetition rates in the range where we hear pitch, from a few dozen to a few thousand times a second; much slower repetition is heard as a rhythm. Middle C, called C4, repeats about 262 times a second.

A pure tone is a single cosine wave, the sound of a tuning fork. Any repeating pattern can be built from pure tones at 1, 2, 3, … times its repetition rate, and from no others; this is Fourier’s theorem, and page 3 shows where it comes from. These tones are its harmonics, and the one at the repetition rate itself is the fundamental. Open “Mix your own” above and try the presets: the wave’s shape changes, and so does the sound’s color, its timbre, but as long as the pattern repeats 220 times a second, the pitch stays put.

In symbols: a pressure signal p(t) is periodic with period P if p(t + P) = p(t) for every t. Any periodic signal that is not too wild (continuous and made of smooth pieces is plenty) is the sum of its Fourier series, p(t) = \sum_{n \ge 0} b_n \cos(2\pi n f t + \phi_n), \qquad f = 1/P . The n = 0 term is a constant, the average pressure, usually zero. The possible frequencies nf are fixed by the period alone, and a given sound may use only some of them: a pure tone uses only f. The amplitudes b_n and the phases \phi_n carry everything else about the sound’s shape, and the ear is mostly insensitive to the phases.

Why whole numbers matter

Turn it around. If an object vibrates at several frequencies at once, its sound repeats only when those frequencies are whole-number multiples of one common frequency. For two tones, that means their frequency ratio is a fraction j/k of whole numbers, in lowest terms; the pattern then repeats every k cycles of the lower tone. Only a small k gives a repetition fast enough to hear as one pitch. Try it:

2.00×

Gray ticks mark cycles of the lower tone. At 2 : 1 the sum repeats every cycle; at 3 : 2 every two; at 1.59 : 1, which is 159/100, it lines up only after 100 cycles of the lower tone, far too slowly to hear as one pitch.

ProofTwo tones repeat together exactly when their frequency ratio is rational

Let s(t) = \cos(2\pi f_1 t) + \cos(2\pi f_2 t) with 0 < f_1 < f_2.

If the ratio is rational, s is periodic. Write f_2/f_1 = j/k in lowest terms and put P = k/f_1. Then f_1P = k and f_2P = j are whole numbers, so both cosines return to their starting values after time P, and s(t+P) = s(t).

If s is periodic, the ratio is rational. Suppose s(t+P) = s(t) for all t, with P > 0. At t = 0 this gives s(P) = s(0) = 2. A sum of two cosines equals 2 only when both cosines equal 1, so \cos(2\pi f_1P) = \cos(2\pi f_2P) = 1, which means f_1P and f_2P are whole numbers. So f_2/f_1 = (f_2P)/(f_1P) is a ratio of whole numbers. Since f_2/f_1 = j/k, we have k\,(f_2P) = j\,(f_1P), so k divides j\,(f_1P); as j and k share no factor, k divides the whole number f_1P. So every period is a multiple of k/f_1, and k/f_1 is the shortest one.

The shortest period k/f_1 grows with the denominator k. A ratio like 1.59 = 159/100 is rational, but its pattern repeats only every 100 cycles of the lower tone, far too slowly for the ear to hear as one pitch. How the ear decides what counts as “simple enough” is a question of perception, not mathematics; see pitch.

Reader questionIs a musical note one frequency?

No. A note is a sound whose pressure repeats every P seconds, and its pitch is the repetition rate f = 1/P. By the Fourier series above, its components can only be at f, 2f, 3f, \dots, and usually several are present at once. The repetition rate sets the pitch, whether or not a component at f itself is present, and the weights b_n set the timbre: a piano C4 and a violin C4 share f and differ in their b_n. So a note corresponds to a period, not to a single frequency.

What we found

A note is a sound that repeats. Its pitch is how often it repeats, and so one note is a sum of tones at whole-number multiples of that rate.

The next puzzle

So a clear note needs frequencies at exact whole-number multiples of one frequency. A guitar string is just a piece of wire, and a drumhead just a sheet of plastic. Why would the wire vibrate at exactly 1, 2, 3, … times one frequency, and the drumhead not? To find out, start with the simplest thing that vibrates at all: one mass on a spring.
