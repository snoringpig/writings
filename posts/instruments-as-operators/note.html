In one sentence. When a sound’s frequencies are all, or very nearly, whole-number multiples of one frequency, the sound repeats, or nearly repeats, at the largest such frequency, and if that rate is in the range we hear as pitch, the ear hears it as the pitch, even when that frequency itself is missing.

The gist. A note is a sound that repeats, and its pitch is how many times a second it repeats. Taking away the 220 Hz part changes the wave’s shape, but it still repeats every 1/220 of a second, so you hear the same pitch. The parts that are left, at 440, 660, 880, … Hz, each fit a whole number of cycles into that time. This page is the last step of the series’ recipe, what we hear; every later page starts from Newton’s law, computes an instrument’s frequencies, and checks whether they sit at whole-number multiples of one frequency.

The pitch ruler

The stems under the wave are the note’s frequencies, drawn as multiples of 220 Hz: 1×, 2×, 3×, …. Those whole-number multiples are the lines of the pitch ruler, and every page of this series puts an instrument on it: stems on the pitch ruler, one per mode, make the spectrum diagram that stage 4 of every instrument page draws. A note’s frequencies sit on the lines. Take away the 1× stem and the rest still sit on lines of the same ruler, so the pitch is still 220 Hz. The component at the repetition rate itself is called the fundamental. Hearing the pitch without it is called the missing fundamental effect, and it is why a deep voice keeps its pitch over an ordinary telephone line, which cannot carry sounds below about 300 Hz.

Open “Mix your own” and try the presets. The stems’ heights change, and so does the sound’s color, its timbre, but the pitch stays put as long as the base frequency does. Keep only 2×, 4×, 6× and 8×, and the sound repeats twice as often: the base becomes 440 Hz, and the pitch jumps up an octave.

How it works

A note is a pattern that repeats. Sound is air pressure rising and falling. When the pattern repeats, you hear a note, and its pitch is the repetition rate, measured in hertz. This holds for rates in the range where we hear pitch, from a few dozen to a few thousand times a second (for a recognizable melody, roughly 30 Hz to 4 or 5 kHz: Pressnitzer and colleagues measured the lower limit; the upper one is the traditional limit, which Oxenham and colleagues revisit). Much slower repetition is heard as a rhythm. Middle C, called C4, repeats about 262 times a second.

Every repeating sound is a chord on the lines. A pure tone is a single sine-shaped wave, the sound of a tuning fork. Any repeating pattern can be built from pure tones at 1, 2, 3, … times its repetition rate, and from no others; this is Fourier’s theorem, and page 3 shows where it comes from. These tones are the sound’s partials, and the one at the repetition rate itself is the fundamental. The repetition rate sets the pitch, whether or not the fundamental is actually there; the partials’ strengths set the timbre.

In symbols: sound is a pressure signal p(t). It is periodic with period P if p(t + P) = p(t) for every t; take P to be the shortest such time. The pitch is the repetition rate f = 1/P, for rates in the range where we hear pitch. Any periodic signal that is not too wild (continuous and made of smooth pieces is plenty) is the sum of its Fourier series: with f = 1/P, p(t) = \sum_{n \ge 0} b_n \cos(2\pi n f t + \phi_n) . The n = 0 term is a constant, the average pressure, usually zero. The possible frequencies nf are fixed by the period alone, and a given sound may use only some of them: a pure tone uses only f. The amplitudes b_n and the phases \phi_n carry everything else about the sound’s shape, and the ear is mostly insensitive to the phases.

A note, in symbols. A sound that repeats every P seconds contains only the frequencies f, 2f, 3f, \dots with f = 1/P: whole-number multiples of its repetition rate, which is its pitch.

Reader questionIs a musical note one frequency?

No. A note is a sound whose pressure repeats every P seconds, and its pitch is the repetition rate f = 1/P. By the Fourier series above, its components can only be at f, 2f, 3f, \dots, and usually several are present at once. The repetition rate sets the pitch, whether or not a component at f itself is present, and the weights b_n set the timbre: a piano C4 and a violin C4 share f and differ in their b_n. So a note corresponds to a period, not to a single frequency.

Why whole numbers matter. Turn it around. If something vibrates at several frequencies at once, its sound repeats only when they are all whole-number multiples of one common frequency. For two tones, the ratio of their frequencies must be a fraction, such as 3/2; the sound then repeats every 2 cycles of the lower tone. The reason is short. Write the ratio in lowest terms, f_2/f_1 = j/k, and wait P = k/f_1 seconds. In that time the lower tone completes f_1P = k whole cycles and the upper one f_2P = j whole cycles, so both are back where they started, and the sum begins again: it repeats every k cycles of the lower tone. A ratio like 1.59, which is 159/100, repeats only every 100 cycles of the lower tone, far too slowly to hear as one pitch. Try it:

Not every instrument is exact. Frequencies very near whole-number multiples still sound like one note: a piano’s are slightly off, as page 7 shows, and it still plays notes. So the rule this series tests every instrument against reads: frequencies at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the range we hear as pitch make a note.

Where the frequencies come from. This page took the frequencies as given. Every later page computes them. Newton’s law, applied to a small piece of the instrument, gives a rule that turns the instrument’s shape into the force pulling it back, the instrument’s operator, and the operator’s special numbers, its eigenvalues, give the frequencies. Page 1 sets this up with the simplest example, one mass on a spring, and page 2 shows why the eigenvalues are the whole story. Whether the frequencies land on the lines of the pitch ruler is the first test of whether the instrument plays a note; pages 6 and 6b show what the frequencies alone do not decide.

ProofThe converse: if two tones repeat together, their ratio is a fraction, and the shortest period uses cosines and divisibility

Let s(t) = \cos(2\pi f_1 t) + \cos(2\pi f_2 t) with 0 < f_1 < f_2.

If the ratio is rational, s is periodic. Write f_2/f_1 = j/k in lowest terms and put P = k/f_1. Then f_1P = k and f_2P = j are whole numbers, so both cosines return to their starting values after time P, and s(t+P) = s(t).

If s is periodic, the ratio is rational. Suppose s(t+P) = s(t) for all t, with P > 0. At t = 0 this gives s(P) = s(0) = 2. A sum of two cosines equals 2 only when both cosines equal 1, so \cos(2\pi f_1P) = \cos(2\pi f_2P) = 1, which means f_1P and f_2P are whole numbers. So f_2/f_1 = (f_2P)/(f_1P) is a ratio of whole numbers. Since f_2/f_1 = j/k, we have k\,(f_2P) = j\,(f_1P), so k divides j\,(f_1P); as j and k share no factor, k divides the whole number f_1P. So every period is a multiple of k/f_1, and k/f_1 is the shortest one.

The shortest period k/f_1 grows with the denominator k. A ratio like 1.59 = 159/100 is rational, but its pattern repeats only every 100 cycles of the lower tone, far too slowly for the ear to hear as one pitch. How the ear decides what counts as “simple enough”, and how near to whole-number multiples is near enough, are questions of perception, not mathematics; see pitch.

What we found

Frequencies at whole-number multiples of one frequency add up to a sound that repeats at that rate, and the ear hears the rate as the pitch, even when that frequency is missing. Every later page computes an instrument’s frequencies and puts them on the pitch ruler: on or very near the lines, a note; off them, a clang or a thud.
