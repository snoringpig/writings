And one question beyond the mathematics: did the instruments people built shape the scales they play? That is the epilogue.

Two ideas answer all six

  1. An instrument’s frequencies come from the spectrum of an operator. The operator is a rule that takes a shape of the instrument, a bent string or a dented drumhead, and gives back the force pulling it back toward rest (per unit mass, and with a minus sign, so that the motion obeys u_{tt} = -Au, where u is the shape, u_{tt} its acceleration and A the operator). The shapes it only rescales are the instrument’s modes; the rescaling factors are its eigenvalues, and the list of them is the spectrum. Each frequency is proportional to the square root of an eigenvalue. The simplest case is a loop of string, where the operator is -c^2\,d^2/d\theta^2, minus the second derivative times a constant c^2, and writing a shape in its modes is the Fourier series.
  2. A clear pitch means the frequencies you hear sit at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency, a frequency in the range we hear as pitch, from a few dozen to a few thousand hertz. Then the sound repeats, or nearly repeats, and the repetition rate is the pitch. Since the frequencies come from the spectrum, whether they line up is decided by the operator and the instrument’s shape. How you play decides how loud each frequency is, with one refinement: a steady bow or breath can lock the motion into exact repetition, close to one of the operator’s frequencies, even when the operator’s overtones are off the grid.

Families

Instruments that are the same up to constants

Instruments in one family share one rule for the restoring force, so they are close to the same mathematical object with different constants. The last column says what still differs inside a family: for air columns the ends, which are part of the operator, and for drums the air around the head, which matters for timpani.
