The Shape of a Sound

The mathematics of musical sound, one instrument at a time

Pluck a string and you get a note you could hum. Strike a drum and you get a thud. Both are a stretched piece of material, set shaking. So why do they sound so different?

Call this the pitch ruler; with one stem per mode, it is the spectrum diagram that stage 4 of every instrument page draws. Its gray lines mark whole-number multiples, 1×, 2×, 3×, …, of one base frequency, here the lowest. Each blue stem is one frequency in the sound; taller means it starts stronger, for an idealized pluck and strike. (The string is plucked a fifth of the way along, so 5× is missing; the drum is struck about a third of the way out from its center.)

The answer in three steps

1. Everything that vibrates has a set of favorite shapes. A string can swing as one arc, as two halves moving in opposite directions, as three thirds, and so on. Each of these shapes, called a mode, swings at its own frequency.

2. A pluck sets many of them going at once. What you hear is a mix of the modes’ frequencies: the lowest, called the fundamental, and the overtones above it. They are the stems on the ruler above.

3. Whether that mix sounds like one note depends on how the frequencies line up. If they are all, or very nearly, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the range we hear as pitch, the sound repeats itself at the largest such frequency, many times a second, and the ear hears that repetition rate as the pitch. A string’s modes do exactly that: 1×, 2×, 3×, …. An ideal drumhead’s land at 1×, 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×, …: a few fall near a line by chance, but no frequency we hear as pitch fits them all. The sound doesn’t repeat at any rate we hear as pitch, and it dies fast: a thud. Drums with a clear note, such as the timpani (kettledrums), are built so that one set of their modes lands near the lines and the rest are weak or fade fast (page 8).

The idea behind the whole series. Apply Newton’s law to a small piece of an instrument and you get a rule for how its shape is pulled back toward rest: its operator. The shapes that rule only scales up or down are the modes; each swings at its own frequency, and left to ring, the instrument moves as a mix of them. The operator’s spectrum fixes those frequencies, so it decides what the instrument can sound like: on the lines, a note; off the lines, a clang or a thud.

The mathematics in one example

The smallest instrument with more than one mode is two beads on a tight string, with both ends tied. Pull one bead aside and let go, and both beads wobble in a tangled way that never quite repeats. But two ways of starting them are special. Lift both beads together, and they swing together forever, at one frequency. Lift one up and push the other down, and they swing in opposite directions forever, at √3 ≈ 1.73 times that frequency: now the stretch of string between the beads pulls too, so the restoring pull is three times as strong, and a frequency goes as the square root of the pull (page 1).

These two shapes are the beads’ modes, and every motion of the two beads, the tangled one included, is a mix of them, each part swinging at its own frequency. The rule that turns the beads’ heights into the pulls on them is their operator; a mode is a shape that the rule only scales; the numbers that fix the modes’ frequencies form its spectrum. A string, a column of air, a steel bar and a drumhead are the same story with more and more small pieces, each pulled back by its own rule. Page 2 works the two beads out in full, and the spectrum of an operator proves that every free motion is a mix of modes and says where that stops being exact: when a bow or a breath keeps driving the motion, when the swings are large, or when friction mixes the modes.

Did instrument makers know?

Not in these terms. Instruments came first, shaped over centuries by trial and ear; the mathematics came later and explained them, from Mersenne’s laws of vibrating strings (1636) and d’Alembert’s wave equation (1747) to Rayleigh’s The Theory of Sound (1877). Designing an instrument by computing its spectrum is recent: a carillon bell with a major third in its overtones, where traditional bells have a minor third, was designed by computer at the Eindhoven University of Technology for the Eijsbouts bell foundry; the map tells the longer story.

The journey

  1. The idea
  2. 0What makes a sound a note?When a sound's frequencies are all, or very nearly, whole-number multiples of one frequency, the sound repeats, or nearly repeats, at the largest such frequency, and if that rate is in the range we hear as pitch, the ear hears it as the pitch, even when that frequency itself is missing.
  3. 1Why does a fading note stay in tune?A mass on a spring vibrates at one frequency, √(k/m)/2π (k its stiffness, m its mass), and the size of the swing doesn't enter (for small swings), which is why a fading note keeps its pitch.
  4. 2How can a tangled wobble be a few simple motions?With many masses the operator is a table of numbers (a matrix); the shapes it only rescales (its eigenvectors) swing as independent springs, and any motion is a mix of them.
  5. 3Why does a loop of string land exactly on the lines?A loop of string vibrates at exactly 1, 2, 3, … times its lowest frequency, because each wave around it has a whole number of crests and all waves travel at the same speed.
  6. ★The spectrum of an operatorLeft to ring, an object vibrates as a mix of its modes; the operator fixes their frequencies, and playing sets only how strongly each sounds.
  7. Instruments
  8. 4Why does a light touch make a string jump an octave?A light touch at the middle drains every mode that moves under the finger and leaves the even modes, which stand still there: a note an octave up.
  9. 5Why is a clarinet almost an octave deeper than a flute?Air in a tube obeys the string's equation with different ends: a tube closed at one end has only odd multiples of its lowest frequency, so a clarinet sounds almost an octave below a flute of the same length and overblows an octave and a fifth higher.
  10. 6Why does a kalimba sound clean?A bar clamped at one end has overtones off whole-number multiples (6.27×, 17.55×), yet a kalimba sounds clean because those overtones die within a fraction of a second (observed, not derived).
  11. 6bWhere did the harmonica reed's overtones go?Blown, the reed swings just below its own lowest frequency and chops the air into identical puffs, so the steady sound has only whole-number multiples and the reed's off-line overtones are absent from it.
  12. 7Why do piano tuners stretch octaves?A piano wire's stiffness puts each overtone slightly above its whole-number multiple, so tuners stretch octaves to reduce the beating, a slow wobble in loudness, most at the ends of the keyboard.
  13. 8Why does a drum thud?An ideal drumhead's frequencies (1, 1.59, 2.14, … times the lowest) fit no pitch, so a plain drum thuds; on a kettledrum, the air the head pushes pulls one set of modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5, a note.
  14. Beyond: two questions the recipe raises
  15. 9Can you hear the shape of a drum?All of a drum's frequencies tell you its area and, for a reasonable rim, its perimeter, but not always its shape: two different drums can have exactly the same frequencies.
  16. 10Did instruments shape our scales?Two notes sound smooth together when their overtones line up, so instruments with different overtones can favour different scales, as Sethares argues (after Plomp and Levelt).
