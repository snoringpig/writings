A piano note has a pitch you could hum and a drum’s thud has none because the piano’s frequencies sit, very nearly, on whole-number multiples of one frequency and the drum’s do not. This series explains where an instrument’s frequencies come from with one piece of mathematics: they are read off the spectrum of an operator, the rule in the instrument’s equation of motion.

The posts

  1. What you hear, with sounds and no differential equations: the four things we hear in a sound, and why a clear pitch needs the partials at or very near whole-number multiples of one frequency.
  2. One string, end to end works the plucked string completely: its equation, from Newton’s law; why its motion is a sum of modes, each swinging at its own frequency (in a basis of eigenfunctions the equation splits into independent springs); and how the motion becomes the sound.
  3. Other instruments, same recipe runs the string’s recipe on nine other instruments, from the piano to the theremin, in short entries. Six deep dives work one instrument each in full: the violin, the clarinet, the kalimba, the harmonica, the piano and the drum.

Beyond. The appendix When does an equation have a spectrum? gives the general theory, with counterexamples. Two further posts take up questions the answers raise: Can you hear the shape of a drum? and Did instruments shape our scales?. A Piano is a playable upright with all 88 keys and its three pedals.

The series in four steps

The series explains a sound from your ear back to the instrument.

  1. What you hear. The air pressure at your ear at time t is a sum of fading pure tones, p(t) = \sum_n c_n e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n), with frequencies f_n, sizes c_n, phases \varphi_n and fading times \tau_n. Pitch, loudness, timbre and duration are read off these numbers (post 1).
  2. Body and room: p = h \ast F. The sound is copies of h, the sound at your ear of one sharp tap on the instrument’s body, delayed and scaled by F(t), the input from the vibrating object (for a string, its force on the bridge; post 2). A pure tone of frequency f passes through at that frequency, scaled by |H(f)|, where H is the Fourier transform of h, once the body’s own ring from the start has died away.
  3. The route out: F from u. The input comes from the object’s motion u where it meets the body, such as a string’s slope at the bridge. Every frequency passes, and mode n is scaled by a route weight (post 3).
  4. The object: u_{tt} = -Au. Here u is the object’s displacement (in a wind instrument, the air’s extra pressure), u_{tt} its acceleration and A the operator. Each eigenvalue \lambda_n of A gives a frequency f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi, for any object of finite size whose motion is linear, apart from special cases. For a plucked or struck note, only this step creates frequencies.

In one line, c_n = |H(f_n)| \cdot (\text{route weight}) \cdot |a_n|, where a_n is how strongly the pluck or strike starts mode n; friction and radiation set the \tau_n. In bowed and blown notes the player’s drive fixes the frequencies instead: it locks the motion into one repeating cycle, so the f_n are exact multiples of the playing frequency.

Operator families and instrument classes

This series groups instruments by operator family: instruments whose operators are the same rule up to constants, possibly with different ends, so that one calculation covers them all. The usual scheme, the Hornbostel–Sachs classification, sorts them instead into five classes by what makes the sound.1 In the table, x is the position along the string, bar or tube, c a wave speed, \kappa a bending constant, \Delta the Laplacian on the drumhead, and L and C the inductance and capacitance of the theremin’s circuit.

DetailsWhat varies inside one operator family
  • Strings and air columns, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2. What moves, the string’s height or the air’s pressure; the constants that set the lowest frequency, such as length, tension and mass per length; the ends, which are part of the operator: tied, open at both ends (flute) or closed at one (clarinet, pan pipes); and the playing: plucked (guitar, harp), bowed (violin) or blown.
  • Bending bars, \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4. Steel tine, brass reed or metal bar; the ends, one clamped or both free, which change the frequency ratios; and the playing: plucked, struck or blown, which decides whether you hear the bar’s own overtones or the whole-number multiples of a steady airflow.
  • Stiff strings, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 + \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4. The stiffness, whose effect is largest at the two ends of a piano’s keyboard (the piano in post 3).
  • Membranes, -c^2\Delta. Tension and size, and what surrounds the head, which matters for timpani.
  • A single number, such as s/m for a mass m on a spring of stiffness s or 1/LC for a coil and a capacitor (Wikipedia, LC circuit: Kirchhoff’s law and \omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}). Nothing varies but the number.
DetailsWhere the classes and the mathematics part ways

Hornbostel–Sachs asks what makes the sound; the operator asks what oscillates and by what rule. Mostly the answers line up. Where they part:

  • The piano is a chordophone, a board zither whose strings are struck by hammers, but its operator adds the bar’s fourth derivative to the string’s, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 + \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4: its steel wire is stiff enough to bend like a bar (the piano in post 3).
  • The harmonica is an aerophone, classed by its interrupted airstream, though its reed is a clamped bar like the kalimba’s tine. That agrees with the harmonica in post 3: the steady sound has only whole-number multiples of the puff rate, set by the chopped airflow, and the reed’s own off-line overtones are absent from it.
  • The glockenspiel and the kalimba are idiophones in different groups, struck and plucked, and in one operator family. They share the rule \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, but their ends differ, both free for the glockenspiel bar and one clamped for the tine, so their operators differ, and so do their frequency ratios, 1 : 2.76 : 5.40 against 1 : 6.27 : 17.55.
  • A violin string and a flute’s air column are in different classes, yet their operators agree up to constants: both are -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 with the value held at zero at both ends, the string’s height at its tied ends and the air’s extra pressure at the flute’s open ends (post 3).
  • An electric guitar is a chordophone with a pickup: its strings make the sound, and the electronics only pick it up and amplify it (Wikipedia, Electric guitar).
  • A theremin is an electrophone: its two radio-frequency oscillators are coil-and-capacitor circuits, whose operator is the single number 1/LC, with the player’s hand as one plate of a capacitor and a mixer producing the audible difference (Wikipedia, Theremin); post 3 derives it.

Did instrument makers know?

Mostly not: makers found what works by trying and listening, and the theory explained it afterwards, often much later. Bell founders tuned the Zutphen carillon of 1644 by ear and lathe,2 a century before d’Alembert wrote down the wave equation in 1747.3

DetailsThe longer story, with sources

Instruments came first. How Antonio Stradivari made his violins is not documented, so saying that he worked by trial and ear is an inference (Wikipedia, Stradivarius). Bells show trial and ear at work more plainly. The blind Utrecht carillonneur Jacob van Eyck picked out the separate partials of a bell by whistling at them, and held that a bell must be tuned after casting by changing its shape. In 1644 the city of Zutphen ordered a carillon from the founders Pieter and François Hemony, with van Eyck as its adviser. The brothers cast the bells slightly too thick, turned each on a lathe and shaved metal from the inside until its five principal partials were in tune. The Zutphen carillon is counted as the first tuned carillon (Wikipedia, Jacob van Eyck, drawing on L. Rombouts, Singing Bronze: A History of Carillon Music, Leuven University Press, 2014, and B. Swager, A History of the Carillon, DMus thesis, Indiana University, 1993; Wikipedia, Zutphen).

Laws from experiment. The Pythagorean tradition tied consonant intervals to simple ratios of string lengths: half the length sounds an octave higher. In the late 1580s Vincenzo Galilei, Galileo’s father, hung weights on strings and found that tension works differently: an octave needs four times the weight and a fifth 9 : 4, so the frequency goes with the square root of the tension (“July 2, 1591: Death of Vincenzo Galilei”, APS News, July 2015, following S. Drake’s dating of the experiments to 1588; W. B. Ashworth Jr., “Vincenzo Galilei”, Scientist of the Day, Linda Hall Library, 2 July 2024). Marin Mersenne’s Harmonie universelle (1636) stated the laws of a string together: its frequency is inversely proportional to its length, proportional to the square root of its tension and inversely proportional to the square root of its mass per length, as in f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu} in post 2 (Wikipedia, Mersenne’s laws). In Oxford in the mid-1670s William Noble and Thomas Pigot found that a string can vibrate in parts with still points between them: small pieces of paper hung on the string stayed at rest at some points and trembled at others. John Wallis reported the discovery in the Philosophical Transactions in 1677 (J. Wallis, “Dr. Wallis’s Letter to the Publisher, Concerning a New Musical Discovery; Written from Oxford, March 14. 1676/7”, Philosophical Transactions 12 (1677), JSTOR; Wikipedia, Thomas Pigot). In 1701 Joseph Sauveur explained such motions to the Paris Academy: a string can vibrate at higher frequencies, which he called harmonics, separated by still points, which he called nodes; both words are still in use (S. Dostrovsky, “Sauveur, Joseph”, Complete Dictionary of Scientific Biography).

The mathematics, after the fact. Brook Taylor (1713) worked out from mechanics the shape and the period of a string’s simplest vibration (MacTutor, Brook Taylor). Jean le Rond d’Alembert (1747) wrote down the wave equation and solved it as two waves running in opposite directions (Wikipedia, Wave equation). Daniel Bernoulli (1753) stated that the general motion of a vibrating system is a superposition of its modes; Euler, d’Alembert and Lagrange did not accept that sums of sines could match an arbitrary starting shape (Wikipedia, Daniel Bernoulli, quoting L. Brillouin, Wave Propagation in Periodic Structures, 1946, for the 1753 statement). Leonhard Euler worked out the vibrations of a drumhead (L. Euler, “De motu vibratorio tympanorum”, Novi Commentarii academiae scientiarum Petropolitanae 10 (1766), 243–260, Euler Archive, E302), and the functions now named after Bessel appear there for every whole-number order; the simplest, J_0, had already turned up in Daniel Bernoulli’s hanging chain in the 1730s (R. C. Archibald, “Mathematicians and music”, American Mathematical Monthly 31 (1924), 1–25, part 3 on MacTutor, which also covers Taylor and d’Alembert; Wikipedia, Bessel function, history; D. D. Nolte, “The ups and downs of the compound double pendulum”, Galileo Unbound, 2020, for the hanging chain). Ernst Chladni (1787) made the nodal lines of vibrating plates visible with sand (Wikipedia, Ernst Chladni). Joseph Fourier, in a memoir of 1807 and a book of 1822, argued that any function can be written as a sum of sines and cosines, and used such sums to solve the equation of heat flow (MacTutor, Joseph Fourier; Wikipedia, Fourier series). Charles Sturm and Joseph Liouville (1836–37) developed expansions of this kind for a whole class of second-order equations, the eigenvalue problems now named after them (MacTutor, Charles Sturm). Karl Weierstrass (1858) corrected a conclusion of Lagrange, repeated by Laplace and others: they had held that two equal frequencies would make a small oscillation grow with time, and Weierstrass showed that for small oscillations about a stable rest they do not (K. Weierstrass, “Über ein die homogenen Functionen zweiten Grades betreffendes Theorem, nebst Anwendung desselben auf die Theorie der kleinen Schwingungen”, Monatsberichte der Akademie der Wissenschaften zu Berlin (1858), 207–220, reprint in Mathematische Werke 1; F. Brechenmacher, “L’identité algébrique d’une pratique portée par la discussion sur l’équation à l’aide de laquelle on détermine les inégalités séculaires des planètes (1766–1874)”, arXiv:0704.2931, which quotes Lagrange (1766) and Weierstrass’s correction). Lord Rayleigh’s The Theory of Sound (1877) gathered the subject into one treatise (MacTutor, Lord Rayleigh). David Hilbert’s work on integral equations in the 1900s introduced the name spectral theory (MacTutor, David Hilbert; Wikipedia, Spectral theory), and Hermann Weyl (1911) proved that the frequencies of a drum determine its area (Wikipedia, Weyl law; Can you hear the shape of a drum?).

Computed design is recent. A marimba bar is thinned by an arch cut under its middle. The arch lowers the lowest frequency more than the overtones, and so moves the first overtone from 2.76 times the lowest, the uniform bar of Did instruments shape our scales?, up to a whole-number multiple, usually 4 (N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 19). Makers such as J. C. Deagan’s company in Chicago, building marimbas from the early 1900s, and later Clair Omar Musser’s, tuned each bar by grinding the arch and checking the result, by ear and with pitch-measuring devices as they appeared, such as Deagan’s own “Deagan-o-meter” (1915) and the strobe tuner (1936); the shape of the arch was found by trial (Percussive Arts Society, Hall of Fame: John Calhoun Deagan; “Chicago shop plays key role in keeping world’s ensembles in tune”, WTTW News, 2016; Wikipedia, Clair Omar Musser and Electronic tuner, for the first strobe tuner, Conn, 1936). Bar profiles computed in advance, before any wood or metal is cut, appear in the acoustics literature around 1990 (F. Orduña-Bustamante, “Nonuniform beams with harmonically related overtones for use in percussion instruments”, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 90 (1991), 2935–2941, doi:10.1121/1.401768). Bells followed the same path. A traditional tuned bell has a minor third among its main partials. In the 1980s engineers at the Eindhoven University of Technology designed a bell with a major third instead, by finite-element computation and structural optimization, and the Eijsbouts bell foundry cast it (A. Schoofs, F. van Asperen, P. Maas and A. Lehr, “Computation of bell profiles using structural optimization”, Music Perception 4 (1987), 245–254, doi:10.2307/40285368; Wikipedia, Bell, section “Major third bell”).
