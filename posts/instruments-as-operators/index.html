A piano note has a pitch you could hum; a drum’s thud has none. Both are a stretched, struck piece of material. This series explains the difference with one piece of mathematics, the spectrum of an operator, and it is organised around four questions:

  1. How do we explain what we hear, for example a drum (no clear pitch) against a piano (a clear pitch)?
  2. How do we explain the four aspects of a sound: pitch, loudness, timbre and duration?
  3. Where do the differential equations of instruments come from?
  4. When is the solution of a differential equation a sum over the spectrum of an operator, what is the deeper theory, and how does it connect back to 1 and 2?

The series was written with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model.

The posts

  1. What you hear answers questions 1 and 2, with sounds and no differential equations. A clear pitch means the partials sit at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the range we hear as pitch, so the sound nearly repeats at that rate; a piano’s partials very nearly do this and a drum’s don’t.
  2. Where the equations come from answers question 3. For small motions, Newton’s law on a small piece, together with a law of the material, gives u_{tt} = -Au, with an operator A for each object: spring, beads, string, air column, bar, stiff string, drumhead, and a coil with a capacitor.
  3. When an equation has a spectrum answers question 4, the mathematical answer. The solutions of U' = BU are exactly the combinations of modes when B has a basis of eigenvectors; for a stable, frictionless object of finite size this always holds (once any rigid drift is split off), and the post shows where it fails.
  4. Instruments, case by case puts the answers to work on one instrument per page, starting from an orchestra: violin, clarinet, kalimba, harmonica, piano, bass drum and kettledrum.

Two further posts take up questions the answers raise: Can you hear the shape of a drum? and Did instruments shape our scales?. A Piano is a playable upright with all 88 keys and its three pedals. Technical words link to the glossary.

The series in one paragraph

A vibrating object of finite size whose motion is linear and unchanging in time moves, left to itself, as a sum of modes, apart from the special cases post 3 lists: shapes that each oscillate at their own frequency f_k and fade with their own time \tau_k. The body, the air and the room pass that motion on to a listener in a way that is also linear and unchanging in time: this keeps every f_k and \tau_k, changes the amounts, and adds the filter’s own ringing (the body’s resonances, the room’s reverberation). So the instrument’s part of the sound is p(t) = \sum_k a_k\, e^{-t/\tau_k} \cos(2\pi f_k t + \varphi_k). The equation of motion (the operator, together with friction and radiation) fixes the f_k and \tau_k; the playing, the radiation and where you listen fix the a_k. The four aspects are heard from these lists, through the ear’s own weighting: pitch mainly from where the f_k sit; loudness from the sizes, weighted by frequency; timbre from all three lists, especially early on, plus noise that is not a mode; duration from the fading and from when the player stops or damps the note. Bowed and blown instruments are different: their motion is a single repeating cycle, so its partials sit at exact whole-number multiples; the resonator’s modes shape which partials are strong, and the player sets loudness and duration.

The series derives the f_k; the \tau_k are mostly measured, and it says so where it uses them.

Here p(t) is the air pressure at the listener; a_k is how strongly the k-th partial sounds, \varphi_k where in its cycle it starts, and e^{-t/\tau_k} says that it shrinks by a factor of e \approx 2.7 every \tau_k seconds.

Operator families and instrument classes

The Hornbostel–Sachs classification, the scheme most used by those who study instruments, sorts them into five classes by what makes the sound. This series groups them by operator family: instruments whose operators agree up to constants and how they are held, so that one calculation covers them all.

DetailsThe decimal codes, and when electrophones were added

The numbers are the scheme’s decimal codes, read from the left. Among the chordophones (3), 321.322 are necked box lutes, such as the violin (bowed, 321.322-71) and the guitar; 322 are harps; 314.122 are board zithers with a resonator box, where the piano sits, as 314.122-4-8, sounded by hammers from a keyboard. Among the aerophones (4), 421.121.12 are open side-blown flutes with fingerholes; 422.211.2 are clarinets with a cylindrical bore and fingerholes; 412.132 are sets of free reeds, inside the interruptive free aerophones (412), where “the air-stream is interrupted periodically”. Among the idiophones (1), 122.1 are plucked idiophones in the form of a comb, with laced-on lamellae (thin tongues), and 111.222 are sets of struck percussion plaques. Among the membranophones (2), 211.11 are single kettle drums and 211.212.1 single cylindrical drums with two heads. Number 531.1 places the theremin in 53, Sachs’s group of radioelectric instruments, those that make their sound with electrically driven oscillators.

Hornbostel and Sachs published the scheme in 1914 with four classes; electrophones came later. Francis Galpin gave them a class of their own in his own scheme (1937), and Sachs added them to Hornbostel–Sachs as a fifth class, with the groups 51 to 53, in The History of Musical Instruments (1940). Anthony Baines and Klaus Wachsmann’s English translation (1961) keeps the four classes of 1914 (Lee 2020).

DetailsWhat varies inside one operator family

Inside one operator family the rule stays and the rest varies:

  • Strings and air columns, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2. What moves, the string’s height or the air’s pressure; the constants that set the lowest frequency, such as length, tension and mass per length; the ends, which are part of the operator: tied, open at both ends (flute) or closed at one (clarinet, pan pipes); and the playing: plucked (guitar, harp), bowed (violin) or blown.
  • Bending bars, \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4. Steel tine, brass reed or metal bar; the ends, one clamped or both free, which change the frequency ratios; and the playing: plucked, struck or blown, which decides whether you hear the bar’s own overtones or the whole-number multiples of a steady airflow.
  • Stiff strings, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 + \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4. The stiffness, whose effect is largest at the two ends of a piano’s keyboard (the piano page).
  • Membranes, -c^2\Delta. Tension and size, and what surrounds the head, which matters for timpani.
  • A single number, such as k/m for a mass on a spring or 1/LC for a coil and a capacitor. Nothing varies but the number.
DetailsWhere the classes and the mathematics part ways

Hornbostel–Sachs asks what makes the sound; the operator asks what oscillates and by what rule. Mostly the answers line up. Where they part, the difference is instructive.

  • The piano is a chordophone, a board zither whose strings are struck by hammers, but its operator adds the bar’s fourth derivative to the string’s, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 + \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4: its steel wire is stiff enough to bend like a bar (the piano page).
  • The harmonica is an aerophone, classed by its interrupted airstream, though its reed is a clamped bar like the kalimba’s tine. That agrees with the harmonica page: the steady sound has only whole-number multiples of the puff rate, set by the chopped airflow, and the reed’s own off-line overtones are absent from it.
  • The glockenspiel and the kalimba are idiophones in different groups, struck and plucked, and in one operator family. They share the rule \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, but their ends differ, both free for the glockenspiel bar and one clamped for the tine, so their operators differ, and so do their frequency ratios, 1 : 2.76 : 5.40 against 1 : 6.27 : 17.55.
  • A violin string and a flute’s air column are in different classes, yet their operators agree up to constants, so they are in one operator family: both are -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 with the value held at zero at both ends, the string’s height at its tied ends and the air’s extra pressure at the flute’s open ends (post 2).
  • An electric guitar is a chordophone with a pickup: its strings make the sound, and the electronics only pick it up and amplify it.
  • A theremin is an electrophone: its oscillators are coil-and-capacitor circuits, whose operator is the single number 1/LC; post 2 derives it and explains how the player’s hand sets the pitch.

Did instrument makers know?

Mostly not. The mathematics in this series explained instruments after they were built: makers found what works by trying and listening, and the theory came later, often much later. Bell founders tuned each bell’s partials by ear and lathe for the Zutphen carillon of 1644; experimenters found the laws of strings (Mersenne, 1636) and the still points of a vibrating string (Noble and Pigot, 1670s; Sauveur, 1701). The wave equation (d’Alembert, 1747), modes and their superposition (Daniel Bernoulli, Euler, Fourier) and the spectral theory behind this series (Sturm and Liouville, Rayleigh, Hilbert, Weyl) followed. Designing an instrument by computing its spectrum before it is made is recent: computed marimba bar profiles appear around 1990, and a bell with a major third was designed by finite-element computation in the 1980s.

DetailsThe longer story, with sources

Instruments came first. How Antonio Stradivari made his violins is not documented, so saying that he worked by trial and ear is an inference. Bells show trial and ear at work more plainly. The blind Utrecht carillonneur Jacob van Eyck picked out the separate partials of a bell by whistling at them, and held that a bell must be tuned after casting by changing its shape. In 1644 the city of Zutphen ordered a carillon from the founders Pieter and François Hemony, with van Eyck as its adviser. The brothers cast the bells slightly too thick, turned each on a lathe and shaved metal from the inside until its five principal partials were in tune. The Zutphen carillon is counted as the first tuned carillon.

Laws from experiment. The Pythagorean tradition tied consonant intervals to simple ratios of string lengths: half the length sounds an octave higher. In the late 1580s Vincenzo Galilei, Galileo’s father, hung weights on strings and found that tension works differently: an octave needs four times the weight and a fifth 9 : 4, so the frequency goes with the square root of the tension. Marin Mersenne’s Harmonie universelle (1636) stated the laws of a string together: its frequency is inversely proportional to its length, proportional to the square root of its tension and inversely proportional to the square root of its mass per length, as in f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu} on post 2. In Oxford in the mid-1670s William Noble and Thomas Pigot found that a string can vibrate in parts with still points between them: small pieces of paper hung on the string stayed at rest at some points and trembled at others. John Wallis reported the discovery in the Philosophical Transactions in 1677. In 1701 Joseph Sauveur explained such motions to the Paris Academy: a string can vibrate at higher frequencies, which he called harmonics, separated by still points, which he called nodes. Both words are still in use.

The mathematics, after the fact. Brook Taylor (1713) worked out from mechanics the shape and the period of a string’s simplest vibration. Jean le Rond d’Alembert (1747) wrote down the wave equation and solved it as two waves running in opposite directions. Daniel Bernoulli (1753) stated that the general motion of a vibrating system is a superposition of its modes; Euler, d’Alembert and Lagrange did not accept that sums of sines could match an arbitrary starting shape. Leonhard Euler worked out the vibrations of a drumhead (published 1766), and the functions now named after Bessel appear there for every whole-number order; the simplest, J_0, had already turned up in Daniel Bernoulli’s hanging chain in the 1730s. Ernst Chladni (1787) made the nodal lines of vibrating plates visible with sand. Joseph Fourier, in a memoir of 1807 and a book of 1822, argued that any function can be written as a sum of sines and cosines, and used such sums to solve the equation of heat flow. Charles Sturm and Joseph Liouville (1836–37) developed expansions of this kind for a whole class of second-order equations, the eigenvalue problems now named after them. Karl Weierstrass (1858) corrected a conclusion of Lagrange, repeated by Laplace and others: they had held that two equal frequencies would make a small oscillation grow with time, and Weierstrass showed that for small oscillations about a stable rest they do not. Lord Rayleigh’s The Theory of Sound (1877) gathered the subject into one treatise. David Hilbert’s work on integral equations in the 1900s introduced the name spectral theory, and Hermann Weyl (1911) proved that the frequencies of a drum determine its area (Can you hear the shape of a drum?).

Computed design is recent. A marimba bar is thinned by an arch cut under its middle. The arch lowers the lowest frequency more than the overtones, and so moves the first overtone from 2.76 times the lowest, the uniform bar of Did instruments shape our scales?, up to a whole-number multiple, usually 4. Makers such as J. C. Deagan’s company in Chicago, building marimbas from the early 1900s, and later Clair Omar Musser’s, tuned each bar by grinding the arch and checking the result, by ear and with pitch-measuring devices as they appeared, such as Deagan’s own “Deagan-o-meter” (1915) and the strobe tuner (1936). The shape of the arch was found by trial. Bar profiles computed in advance, before any wood or metal is cut, appear in the acoustics literature around 1990 (Orduña-Bustamante, 1991). Bells followed the same path. A traditional tuned bell has a minor third among its main partials. In the 1980s engineers at the Eindhoven University of Technology designed a bell with a major third instead, by finite-element computation and structural optimization, and the Eijsbouts bell foundry cast it.
