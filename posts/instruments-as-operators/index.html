The Shape of a Sound

The mathematics of musical sound, one instrument at a time

Pluck a string and you get a note you could hum. Strike a drum and you get a thud. Both are a stretched piece of material, set shaking. So why do they sound so different?

Call this the pitch ruler; with one stem per mode, it is the spectrum diagram that stage 4 of every instrument page draws. Its gray lines mark whole-number multiples, 1×, 2×, 3×, …, of one base frequency, here the lowest. Each blue stem is one frequency in the sound; taller means it starts stronger, for an idealized pluck and strike. (The string is plucked a fifth of the way along, so 5× is missing.)

The answer in three steps

1. Everything that vibrates has a set of favorite shapes. A string can swing as one arc, as two halves moving in opposite directions, as three thirds, and so on. Each of these shapes, called a mode, swings at its own frequency.

2. A pluck sets many of them going at once. What you hear is a chord of the modes’ frequencies: the lowest, called the fundamental, and the overtones above it. They are the stems on the ruler above.

3. Whether that chord sounds like one note depends on how the frequencies line up. If they sit at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the range we hear as pitch, the sound repeats itself many times a second, and the ear hears that repetition rate as the pitch. A string’s modes do exactly that: 1×, 2×, 3×, …. An ideal drumhead’s land at 1×, 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×, …: a few fall near a line by chance, but no frequency we hear as pitch fits them all. The sound doesn’t repeat at any rate we hear as pitch, and it dies fast: a thud.

The idea behind the whole series. Newton’s law turns each instrument into an operator, and the operator’s spectrum gives its frequencies: how the instrument is built, stretched and held decides them. Put the frequencies you hear on the pitch ruler: on the lines, a note; off the lines, a clang or a thud. More exactly, you hear a note when they all sit at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the range we hear as pitch. Each instrument page puts its instrument on the ruler to see which.

The mathematics: one recipe, every instrument

Behind every page is the same recipe, and it is the mathematical heart of the series: Newton’s law gives each instrument an operator, and the operator’s spectrum gives its frequencies. The page The spectrum of an operator lays the idea out in full and runs it on every instrument; here are its two steps.

1. Derive the instrument’s operator from first principles. Take one small piece of the instrument (a bit of string, a slab of air, a slice of steel, a patch of drumhead) and apply Newton’s law: mass times acceleration equals the force on it. The force comes from the material: tension, air pressure, or the stiffness of bent steel. Worked out, the force on the piece turns out to depend only on the shape of the instrument around it, through a fixed rule. Dividing by the mass gives an equation of the same form for every instrument, u_{tt} = -A\,u , where u is the instrument’s shape (how far each point is from rest; for a tube, the air pressure), u_{tt} is its acceleration, and A is the operator: the rule that takes a shape and returns minus the restoring force per unit mass. How the instrument is held (ends tied, clamped, open, free) decides which shapes A may act on, and is part of the operator.

2. Find its spectrum. A mode is a shape that the operator only rescales, A\,X = \lambda\,X , for some number \lambda, an eigenvalue. Every operator here has \lambda \ge 0 (where \lambda = 0 occurs, it is the whole instrument drifting, which makes no sound). For \lambda > 0 such a shape swings in place as X\cos(\sqrt{\lambda}\,t + \phi), so it sounds at the frequency f = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2\pi} . The list of all the eigenvalues is the operator’s spectrum. So the frequencies an instrument rings at on its own, once struck or plucked, are fixed by its operator; how you strike or pluck it only decides how strongly each one sounds. A steady bow or breath can do more: it can lock the sound onto exact whole-number multiples (pages 4, 5 and 6b).

Each instrument page carries out both steps in full, from Newton’s law to the frequencies:

Here n = 1, 2, 3, \dots counts the modes, N is the number of beads, \Delta is the two-dimensional version of d^2/dx^2 (page 8), and \propto means “proportional to”. The constants c, \kappa and B come from tension, density and stiffness; each page derives them. The map has the full comparison.

PreciselyWhat counts as a note uses no extra mathematics

A clear pitch means the frequencies you hear sit at, or very near, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the pitch range, from a few dozen to a few thousand hertz. That frequency need not be present itself (page 0). Then the sound repeats, or nearly repeats, and the repetition rate is the pitch. What you hear can differ from the spectrum in two ways: some frequencies die almost at once, leaving the rest (the kalimba, page 6), and a steady bow or breath can lock the motion into exact repetition near one of the operator’s frequencies, even when the operator’s overtones are off the lines (the harmonica, page 6b).
