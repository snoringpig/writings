The Shape of a Sound · Spectrum of an operator · Map · Glossary

Builds on pages 1, 2 and 3, but stands on its own. Math: up to matrices (page 2); the folds use integration by parts and some analysis. About 5 minutes for the flowchart, the idea in brief and the table in section 6; about 35 with the mathematics.

Pluck a string, blow into a tube, tap a steel tine, strike a drum. Four different objects, four different sounds, and one recipe predicts all four. This page lays out the recipe, shows why it works, and says where it stops working.

In one sentence. Left to ring, an object vibrates as a mix of its modes; the operator fixes their frequencies, and playing sets only how strongly each sounds.

The idea in brief: left to ring, an object vibrates as a mix of its modes, and each mode is an independent spring. Physics gives the operator: Newton’s law, applied to one small piece of the instrument, comes out as u_{tt} = -A\,u, where the operator A turns a shape of the instrument into minus the acceleration it causes. Mathematics splits the motion: measured along the eigenvectors of A (the shapes it only rescales, AX = \lambda X), the object becomes a set of independent springs, one per eigenvector, and the spring with eigenvalue \lambda swings at f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi. So the list of eigenvalues, the spectrum, fixes the frequencies, and how you pluck or strike sets only how strongly each mode sounds. This is exact for the small, linearized free vibrations (only the part of each force proportional to the displacement is kept) of a stable, frictionless object of finite size that doesn’t change; section 6 says where it stops, including the steady bow or breath of pages 4, 5 and 6b.

1 · What an operator is

Mathematics used on this page: derivatives, partial derivatives and matrices (explained on page 2). The folds use integration by parts and some analysis.

Two beads on a string, ends tied (page 2). Newton’s law for each bead gives two equations, tangled together because each bead is pulled toward the other. With the two heights in one column x = (x_1, x_2) they read \ddot x = -A\,x, \qquad A = \frac{T}{mh}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} , where T is the tension, m each bead’s mass and h the spacing. Here A acts on a list of two heights. One mass on a spring (page 1) is the smallest case of all: there A is the single number k/m.

A string (pages 3 and 4). With many beads, the pull on each bit of string becomes tension times curvature, and Newton’s law becomes the wave equation u_{tt} = c^2\,u_{xx}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad u_{tt} = -A\,u, \quad A = -c^2\,\frac{d^2}{dx^2}, \quad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu} . Here u(x, t) is the height of the string at position x and time t, subscripts are partial derivatives, and A acts on a whole shape, a function of x.

Definition. For a vibrating object, the operator A is the rule that takes a shape of the object (how far each point is from rest; for a tube, the air pressure) and returns minus the acceleration it causes at every point (for a string or a bar, minus the restoring force per unit mass). It acts only on the shapes allowed by how the object is held. Newton’s law then reads u_{tt} = -A\,u . In words: the acceleration of every point is the restoring force on it, per unit mass.

Why does every instrument come out this way? For small motions we keep only the part of each restoring force that is proportional to the displacement: tension pulls a curved string back in proportion to its curvature, squeezed air pushes back in proportion to the squeeze, bent steel pushes back through the change of its bending. Double the shape and the force doubles; add two shapes and their forces add. So A is linear: A(au + bv) = a\,Au + b\,Av for numbers a, b. (Larger motions break this, and with it the recipe; see section 6.)

2 · Why a mode swings at √λ / 2π

A mode is a shape that the operator only rescales, A\,X = \lambda\,X , for some number \lambda, an eigenvalue of A; the shape X is an eigenfunction (for the beads, an eigenvector). The list of all the eigenvalues, each counted as many times as it has modes (its multiplicity), is the operator’s spectrum. Here is why each eigenvalue is a frequency.

  1. Start the object in the shape of a mode and let go. Try a motion that keeps that shape and only changes its size: u(x, t) = X(x)\,q(t), with AX = \lambda X.
  2. Newton’s law for this motion. The left side is u_{tt} = X\,q''. The right side is -Au = -q\,AX = -\lambda\,q\,X, since A is linear and q(t) is just a number at each moment. So X\,q'' = -\lambda\,q\,X, and dividing by X, q'' = -\lambda\,q .
  3. This is page 1’s spring. For \lambda > 0 its solutions are q(t) = \cos(\sqrt{\lambda}\,t + \phi), times any number: the shape swings with angular frequency \omega = \sqrt{\lambda}. For \lambda = 0 the solutions are q = a + bt: the whole object drifting, with no sound. A negative \lambda would give exponential growth; section 3 shows it never happens.

The converse holds too, and it is why we look for such shapes at all. Suppose a motion keeps one shape, u(x, t) = X(x)\,q(t), with X and q not zero. Newton’s law gives X\,q'' = -q\,AX, so wherever q \neq 0, AX = (-q''/q)\,X; since A and X don’t change with time, the factor -q''/q can’t either. So a motion that keeps its shape exists exactly when the shape is an eigenvector. Looking for such motions is called separation of variables. Notice which object has the eigenvalues: A, which acts on shapes. The motion u itself is then built by adding up these pieces, as the next section shows.

Eigenvalues are frequencies. A mode with eigenvalue \lambda > 0 swings in place at f = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2\pi} . In words: \lambda is restoring force per unit mass per unit displacement, a stiffness over a mass, and the frequency goes as its square root, as for a mass on a spring.

3 · Why every motion is a mix of modes

Modes alone would be a curiosity if the object could also move in other ways. It cannot: measured along its modes, the object splits into independent springs. Here is the reason for any number of beads; strings, tubes, bars and drums follow the same way.

Two beads again. Page 2 found the eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, -1) of the two tied beads, with eigenvalues T/mh and 3T/mh. Scaled to length 1 they are X_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt 2 and X_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt 2. They are perpendicular, X_1\cdot X_2 = (1\cdot 1 + 1\cdot(-1))/2 = 0, and each has length 1, X_1\cdot X_1 = X_2\cdot X_2 = 1. Every column can be built from them: x = c_1X_1 + c_2X_2 with c_1 = X_1\cdot x = S/\sqrt 2 and c_2 = X_2\cdot x = D/\sqrt 2, page 2’s sum and difference rescaled. A set of directions like this, perpendicular, of length 1, and enough to build every column, is called an orthonormal basis.

Result (any number of beads). Let A be a real symmetric n\times n matrix (entry (i, j) equals entry (j, i)) whose eigenvalues are \ge 0.

  1. A has eigenvectors X_1, \dots, X_n forming an orthonormal basis, with eigenvalues \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n \ge 0. This is the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices.
  2. Every solution of \ddot x = -Ax is x(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big(a_i\cos\omega_i t + \frac{b_i}{\omega_i}\sin\omega_i t\Big), \qquad \omega_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}, with a_i = X_i\cdot x(0) and b_i = X_i\cdot\dot x(0); for \lambda_i = 0 the term is X_i\,(a_i + b_i t), a drift.
  3. The starting position and velocity fix the motion: there is exactly one.

Proof. By part 1, write x(t) = \sum_j c_j(t)\,X_j, where c_j(t) = X_j\cdot x(t) is the amount of X_j in x at time t. Take the dot product of Newton’s law \ddot x = -Ax with X_i. On the left, X_i\cdot\ddot x = \ddot c_i. On the right, X_i\cdot Ax = (AX_i)\cdot x, because both sides equal \sum_{j,k} (X_i)_j A_{jk}\, x_k once A is symmetric, A_{jk} = A_{kj}; so X_i\cdot Ax = \lambda_i\,X_i\cdot x = \lambda_i c_i. (Expanding x in the basis gives the same, \sum_j c_j\lambda_j\,X_i\cdot X_j = \lambda_i c_i, since X_i\cdot X_j is 1 when j = i and 0 otherwise; the symmetric route is the one that carries over to strings.) So \ddot c_i = -\lambda_i\,c_i \qquad\text{for each } i \text{ separately}: n independent springs (page 1), or, where \lambda_i = 0, a drift. Each of these one-variable equations, with starting value c_i(0) = a_i and starting speed \dot c_i(0) = b_i, has exactly one solution, a_i\cos\omega_i t + (b_i/\omega_i)\sin\omega_i t (or a_i + b_i t). That gives parts 2 and 3. Symmetry did the work twice: it guarantees perpendicular eigenvectors that fill out a basis, and it moves A from x onto X_i.

Why A is symmetric, and its eigenvalues are \ge 0. One stretch of string joins two beads and feels only the difference of their heights, so moving bead j up by one unit pulls bead i exactly as hard as moving bead i up by one unit pulls bead j: entry (i, j) equals entry (j, i). And pushing the object away from rest stores energy, so every eigenvalue is \ge 0: in the mode X the stored energy per unit mass is \tfrac12\lambda\,X\cdot X, which can’t be negative, with \lambda = 0 only for a drift of the whole object that stores none, like the two-bead ring’s (page 2).

PreciselyEnergy, unequal masses, and the space of all motions uses multivariable calculus

Symmetry from energy. Near rest the stored energy is V(x) \approx \tfrac12\,x\cdot Kx, where K, the stiffness matrix, is the matrix of second derivatives of V (its Hessian). It is symmetric because mixed partial derivatives don’t depend on the order of differentiation. The restoring force is -\nabla V \approx -Kx, keeping only the first-order term; that is where linearity comes from. A stable rest means x\cdot Kx > 0 for every x except rigid drifts, so the eigenvalues are \ge 0, and the drifts (\lambda = 0) are split off.

Unequal masses. Newton’s law reads M\ddot x = -Kx with M the diagonal matrix of masses, and A = M^{-1}K is not symmetric in the plain dot product. It is symmetric in the mass-weighted one, \langle x, y\rangle = \sum_i m_i x_i y_i; equivalently, v = M^{1/2}x obeys \ddot v = -M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}\,v with a symmetric matrix. Every density in this series is uniform, apart from page 8’s air-loaded model and the tabla, so the plain dot product suffices on the other pages.

The space of all motions. The solutions form a space of dimension 2n: the sum, over the distinct eigenvalues \lambda, of the motions X\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\,t + Y\sin\sqrt{\lambda}\,t with X and Y in the eigenspace of \lambda (all eigenvectors with that eigenvalue), read as X + tY when \lambda = 0. Sending a motion to its starting position and velocity is one-to-one and onto \mathbb{R}^n\times\mathbb{R}^n. So the space of motions is built from the eigenspaces of A, not an eigenspace of A.

Other equations the same modes split. Any equation built from time derivatives and the fixed A alone splits the same way, each eigenvector’s amount obeying its own one-variable equation: for example \ddot x + (\alpha + \beta A)\dot x + Ax = 0, friction proportional to mass and stiffness (Rayleigh damping), or the heat equation \dot x = -Ax.

Strings, tubes, bars and drums. For a string, sums become integrals: the dot product becomes the overlap \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^L f(x)\,g(x)\,dx, and the amount of mode X_n in a shape f is \langle f, X_n\rangle/\langle X_n, X_n\rangle. The proof above goes through, with sums becoming integrals, once two facts hold (the fold below gives the fine print): A is symmetric, \langle Af, g\rangle = \langle f, Ag\rangle, and its modes are complete, meaning every allowed shape is a sum of modes. For the string with tied ends, one integration by parts gives \langle Af, g\rangle = c^2\int_0^L f'g'\,dx, which reads the same with f and g swapped, so A is symmetric, and it is \ge 0 when g = f. Completeness, for every object in this series, is a theorem of analysis; on the loop it is Fourier’s theorem (page 3).

ProofSymmetry, perpendicular modes and energy, for strings, bars and drums uses integration by parts

The identity: the operator is symmetric. Measure how much two shapes f and g overlap by \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^L f(x)\,g(x)\,dx (for the beads, \sum_j f_j g_j; for a drum, an integral over the head). For the string, integrate by parts once: \langle Af, g\rangle = -c^2\int_0^L f''\,g\,dx = -c^2\big[f'\,g\big]_0^L + c^2\int_0^L f'\,g'\,dx . If the ends are tied, g(0) = g(L) = 0 and the bracket vanishes. (It also vanishes on a loop, where the two ends are the same point, and at a closed tube end, where f' = 0.) What is left, c^2\int f'g'\,dx, does not change when f and g swap places. So \langle Af, g\rangle = \langle f, Ag\rangle . This is what it means for A to be symmetric. For a kalimba tine, integrating by parts twice gives \kappa^2\int f''g''\,dx, with every boundary term killed by the clamped and free ends; for a drum, the two-dimensional version gives c^2\iint \nabla f\cdot\nabla g\,dA (here \nabla f = (f_x, f_y), the slope in each direction, and \nabla f\cdot\nabla g = f_x g_x + f_y g_y), with the rim term killed by the fixed rim. The same holds for every instrument in the series: each way of holding an instrument that appears here makes the boundary terms vanish.

Fact 1: modes with different frequencies are perpendicular. If AX = \lambda X and AY = \mu Y, then \lambda\langle X, Y\rangle = \langle AX, Y\rangle = \langle X, AY\rangle = \mu\langle X, Y\rangle. If \lambda \neq \mu, this forces \langle X, Y\rangle = 0: the two shapes do not overlap at all, in the sense above. (Modes that share an eigenvalue, like a loop’s cosine and sine, can be chosen perpendicular too.)

Fact 2: no eigenvalue is negative. Put g = f in the identity: \langle Af, f\rangle = c^2\int (f')^2\,dx \ge 0. For a mode, \lambda\langle X, X\rangle = \langle AX, X\rangle \ge 0, so \lambda \ge 0. The physics is energy. Let E(t) = \tfrac12\langle u_t, u_t\rangle + \tfrac12\langle Au, u\rangle . Times the mass per unit length, the first term is the kinetic energy and the second the energy stored in the stretched string (\tfrac12 T\int u_x^2\,dx). Using symmetry, E' = \langle u_t, u_{tt}\rangle + \langle Au, u_t\rangle = \langle u_t, u_{tt} + Au\rangle = 0 by Newton’s law: energy is conserved, and since the stored part can never be negative, no mode can grow exponentially (a mode with \lambda = 0 can only drift). For the string, zero occurs only for a shape with no slope anywhere, the whole string moving at once, which a tied end forbids. (For a bar with both ends free, the zero modes are the bar sliding and tilting as a whole.)

Fact 3: the modes are complete. Every allowed shape f is a sum of modes, f = \sum_n a_n X_n, and Fact 1 gives the amounts: taking the overlap of both sides with X_m kills every term but one, so a_n = \frac{\langle f, X_n\rangle}{\langle X_n, X_n\rangle} . For the beads this is the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices, used on page 2. For strings, tubes, bars and drums it is a theorem of analysis (fold below); on the loop it is Fourier’s theorem, page 3.

PreciselyCompleteness for differential operators uses functional analysis

Each operator in the series is the operator of its energy form: the string’s c^2\int u'v'\,dx, on shapes with u = 0 at tied ends; the clamped–free bar’s \kappa^2\int u''v''\,dx, on shapes with u(0) = u'(0) = 0; the stiff string’s sum of the two, on shapes vanishing at the pinned ends; the membrane’s c^2\iint\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dA, on shapes vanishing on the rim. Each way of holding either restricts the allowed shapes (it is imposed: u = 0, and u' = 0 at a clamp) or falls out of the energy when the shape is varied (it is natural: a free bar end’s u'' = u''' = 0, the clarinet’s p' = 0 at the reed, a free string end’s u' = 0). A pinned end is half of each: u = 0 is imposed and u'' = 0 is natural. A way of holding that does work, like a bridge that yields and absorbs energy, breaks the symmetry.

On the space L^2 of shapes with finite \int f^2 (mass-weighted where the density varies), each operator is self-adjoint and \ge 0, and (A + 1)^{-1} is a compact operator: for one-dimensional objects it is an integral operator with a continuous kernel, the Green’s function; for a drum on a bounded region with a reasonable edge this is Rellich’s theorem. (Boundedness alone is not enough: with free edges, very irregular regions can fail.) The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators then gives an orthonormal basis of L^2 made of eigenfunctions, with eigenvalues \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \to \infty, each of finite multiplicity. For second-order one-dimensional problems this is Sturm–Liouville theory; the fourth-order bars are not classical Sturm–Liouville problems but satisfy the same theorem. Courant and Hilbert, volume 1, chapters V and VI, treat all the cases in this series.

For a starting shape f with finite energy and a starting velocity g with finite \int g^2, the one motion with finite energy is u(t) = \cos(t\sqrt{A})\,f + \frac{\sin(t\sqrt{A})}{\sqrt{A}}\,g (read \sin(t\sqrt A)/\sqrt A as t on drifts), which is exactly the mode sum below, converging in the energy sense and conserving energy. A plucked string, with its kink, is a motion in this sense rather than a twice-differentiable one.

Now put it together. Start the object in any shape u(x, 0) = f(x) with any velocity u_t(x, 0) = g(x), and expand both in modes, f = \sum a_n X_n and g = \sum b_n X_n. The motion is u(x, t) = \sum_n X_n(x)\Big(a_n\cos\omega_n t + \frac{b_n}{\omega_n}\sin\omega_n t\Big), \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}, with a_n + b_n t for a mode with \lambda_n = 0 (drifting, no sound).

Every free motion is a mix of modes. Left to ring, the object moves as a sum of its modes, each swinging at its own frequency f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi. The spectrum fixes the frequencies; the start (how and where you pluck or strike) fixes only the amounts a_n and b_n.

4 · How the instrument is held is part of the operator

The rule alone is not the operator: the allowed shapes are part of it. Take the same rule, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, on a piece of length L, and hold the ends in three ways. Writing X as a sine or cosine of kx, so that AX = c^2k^2X (here k is the wavenumber, 2\pi divided by the wavelength, not the spring stiffness of section 1), each way of holding picks out different values of k:

Held Modes Eigenvalues \lambda_n Frequencies f_n
as a loop: X(x + L) = X(x) \cos\frac{2\pi n x}{L}, \sin\frac{2\pi n x}{L} \big(\frac{2\pi n c}{L}\big)^2 \frac{nc}{L}: 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots
both ends tied (string; flute, on pressure): X(0) = X(L) = 0 \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} \big(\frac{n\pi c}{L}\big)^2 \frac{nc}{2L}: 1, 2, 3, \dots

(On the loop each n \ge 1 has two modes, the cosine and the sine; page 3 uses a loop of length 2\pi, where f_n = nc/2\pi.) The same happens for a bar, where the rule is \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4: clamped at one end and free at the other (kalimba, harmonica reed), the frequencies stand in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55; pinned at both ends, 1 : 4 : 9; free at both ends (glockenspiel), 1 : 2.76 : 5.40. Change how the instrument is held and you change its spectrum, without touching the physics of the material.

PreciselyOne principle behind the shifts: stiffness raises frequencies, added mass lowers them uses integrals

For a shape X, the Rayleigh quotient is its stored energy divided by its kinetic weight, for the string R(X) = c^2\int (X')^2\,dx \big/ \int X^2\,dx; at a mode it equals the eigenvalue \lambda. The min–max principle (Courant–Fischer) says more: the n-th eigenvalue \lambda_n is the least, over all n-dimensional families of allowed shapes, of the largest Rayleigh quotient in the family. So making the object stiffer (a larger energy) or allowing fewer shapes never lowers any \lambda_n; the piano’s stiff wire has both (page 7). Adding mass never raises any \lambda_n; the air a drumhead pushes adds mass (page 8). A term \tfrac12\eta\,(\int X)^2 added to the energy changes nothing for a shape with \int X = 0: indeed (A + (\eta/\sigma)\,\mathbf 1\langle\mathbf 1, \cdot\rangle)X = AX whenever \langle\mathbf 1, X\rangle = 0, where \mathbf 1 is the constant shape and \sigma the mass per unit area. That is the nearly sealed air in a kettledrum’s bowl, in page 8’s model: every mode with lines through the center keeps its frequency exactly, and only the round modes move.

5 · From the spectrum to the sound

The spectrum gives the frequencies. Three more things decide what reaches your ear.

How you play sets the heights. The amounts a_n, b_n come from the starting shape and velocity, so where and how you pluck or strike decides which modes start and how strongly. A string plucked a fifth of the way along starts no 5th mode, because that mode stands still at the pluck point (page 4); a drum struck at its center starts only the round modes (page 8). On the spectrum diagram, the stems stand at the frequencies the spectrum gives, and their heights are set by the playing.

Each mode fades at its own rate. Real instruments lose energy to friction, to the air and to their supports. For light losses each mode still swings at very nearly its own frequency, and its size fades away (page 1, and the piano’s dampers on page 7). So the sound is p(t) \approx \sum_n c_n\, e^{-t/\tau_n}\cos(2\pi f_n t + \phi_n), with the frequencies f_n from the spectrum and amounts and fading times c_n, \tau_n from the playing, the losses and the way the instrument passes its motion to the air. That passing on is linear too (a violin’s body, for instance), so it changes the heights of the stems, never their positions (page 4). Every plucked or struck sound in the instrument pages’ demos is made exactly this way: a sum of sine waves at the frequencies from the spectrum, each fading at its own rate. The bowed and blown sounds (pages 4, 5 and 6b) are made differently: a wave repeating at the playing frequency, so their frequencies are its whole-number multiples (apart from the violin’s gentle vibrato and the chromatic harmonica’s breath noise). The frequencies come from the operator; the amounts and fading times are modelling choices, rough values chosen to illustrate. The front page’s orchestra labels its exceptions: its timpani uses measured frequencies, and its theremin is a single steady tone.

Then the ear. If the frequencies you hear are all, or very nearly, whole-number multiples of one frequency in the range we hear as pitch, you hear a note, at the largest such frequency (page 0).

What the operator does not decide, the series also shows. A kalimba’s off-line overtones fade within a fraction of a second, so what you hear is mostly one frequency (page 6). And a steady bow or breath is not a free vibration at all: it keeps feeding energy in, and the motion locks into one exactly repeating pattern near one of the modes’ frequencies, usually the lowest (observed, not derived in this series), so the sound sits exactly on the lines even when the operator’s other frequencies do not (the violin, page 4; the clarinet’s sound, page 5; the harmonica, page 6b). Section 6 places this among the other ways the recipe stops being exact.

6 · When the recipe applies, and when it doesn’t

The recipe is exact for one class of motions: small, linearized free vibrations (keeping only the part of each force proportional to the displacement, section 1) of a stable, frictionless object of finite size whose properties don’t change in time. This is Lagrange’s theory of small oscillations, whose solutions are the normal modes (the fold gives it in symbols). Every operator in this series is in it. Real instruments leave it in a few ways.

PreciselyThe table in equations uses differential equations

The class in symbols. M\,u_{tt} = -K\,u with the mass M and the stiffness K symmetric, M positive definite and K positive definite apart from rigid drifts (so x\cdot Mx > 0 for every x \ne 0, and x\cdot Kx > 0 unless x is a drift), on an object of finite size.

Stability. An unstable rest, \ddot x = +x, runs away exponentially; the class needs K \ge 0.

A steady push. Rayleigh’s equation (1883), \ddot x - \varepsilon(\dot x - \dot x^3/3) + x = 0, is the simplest model: near rest the middle term acts as negative friction, so small motions grow; the cubic term caps them, and every motion except rest itself settles into one repeating cycle, a limit cycle, at angular frequency 1 - \varepsilon^2/16 + \cdots, near but not at the linear value 1. A bow’s stick and slip and a reed’s valve are nonlinear exciters of this kind. A harmonica’s bent note is the player’s mouth, together with the hole’s other reed, pulling the pitch (Bahnson, Antaki and Beery 1998). By contrast, a linear periodic force is solved by the same modes, and without friction the response at an exact resonance grows in proportion to time.

Friction. With a damping matrix C, M\ddot x + C\dot x + Kx = 0 has solutions X e^{st} with (s^2M + sC + K)X = 0, a quadratic eigenvalue problem. If CM^{-1}K = KM^{-1}C, for example when C = \alpha M + \beta K, the real modes survive and each just fades (Caughey and O’Kelly 1965); otherwise the modes are generally complex. For light friction, mode i fades at the rate \gamma_i \approx X_i\cdot CX_i \big/ (2\,X_i\cdot MX_i), to first order, when the friction is small compared with the gaps between frequencies; within a repeated frequency, choose the modes that the friction leaves unmixed. Coupled piano unisons decay first fast, then slowly (Weinreich 1977).

The surrounding air. Treated as not squeezable near the head (the kettle’s uniform pressure aside), the air adds a fixed, symmetric, positive added mass: \sigma AX = \omega^2(\sigma + M_{\text{air}})X, a generalized eigenvalue problem inside the class, which is page 8’s toy model. Exactly, the added mass depends on the frequency and is complex, because sound radiates away into unbounded air: a nonlinear eigenvalue problem whose “modes” are resonances that decay at the rate the head radiates energy.

A pumped swing. Mathieu’s equation \ddot x + (1 + \varepsilon\cos 2t)\,x = 0 has solutions e^{\mu t}p(t) with p periodic (Floquet’s theorem); for every small \varepsilon \neq 0 one \mu is positive, about \varepsilon/4: parametric resonance.

Spinning objects. Gyroscopic forces G\dot x, with G antisymmetric, do no work and need no friction, yet the equation is not of the form M\ddot x = -Kx. The class is that of “natural” mechanical systems, whose energy is kinetic plus stored, with no such terms.

No edges. On an infinite string, -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 has the continuous spectrum [0, \infty) and no eigenfunctions with finite energy; the Fourier transform replaces the Fourier series.

What we found

Newton’s law on a small piece gives u_{tt} = -A\,u. Measured along the eigenvectors of A, the object splits into independent springs, so left to ring it moves as a mix of modes at the frequencies f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi, and playing sets only how strongly each sounds. This is exact for small, linearized free vibrations of a stable, frictionless object of finite size; section 6 lists where it stops. The recipe run card by card on every instrument is on the map.
