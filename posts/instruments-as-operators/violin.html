Why does a light touch make a string jump an octave?

4 · The violin string

Builds on page 3, the loop of string. About 10 minutes, plus the folds.

Touch a vibrating string lightly at its exact middle, without pressing it down, and the note jumps up an octave. Touch it a third of the way along and it jumps to three times the frequency. How can a feather-light finger, which does not even shorten the string, change the note so much?

Pluck the string, then pick a point to touch and pluck again. String players call these touched notes harmonics.

Touch lightly at

Left end: the nut, near the tuning pegs; right end: the bridge. The motion is slowed for the eye. Stems: how far each mode still swings a quarter of a second after the pluck.

The gist. A string tied at both ends can swing as one arch, two, three, …, at 1, 2, 3, … times its lowest frequency, and a pluck starts many of these at once. A light touch makes no new end: it soaks up the energy of every shape that moves under the finger. Touch at the middle and only the shapes that already stand still there survive: two arches, four, six, …. They sit at 2, 4, 6, … times the old lowest frequency, which is a note an octave up.

On the pitch ruler

In this ideal model, an open string’s frequencies sit exactly on the lines: 1×, 2×, 3×, …. Each stem’s height is how far that mode still swings a quarter of a second after the pluck. Touch at the middle and the odd stems die almost at once; what is left sits on every second line, the lines of a note twice as high. Touch at a third and only every third stem survives. Move the pluck point too: near the bridge the stems shrink slowly as they go up, and at the middle they shrink fast, with every second stem missing even without a finger.

How it works

Tied ends keep half of the loop’s waves. A string tied at both ends can only take shapes that stay still at the ends: one arch, two arches, three, …. Each is exactly half of a wave on a loop twice as long, so the reason from page 3 still holds (a shorter wave bends more sharply, and the string pulls back in proportion to how sharply it bends): a shape with n arches is n times shorter, is pulled back n² times harder, and swings n times faster. The frequencies are exactly 1, 2, 3, … times the lowest, which itself rises with the tension and falls with the string’s length and its weight per length. One thing does change: on a loop each frequency had two waves, one shifted along the loop; on a string a wave cannot run around, it reflects at the ends, and each frequency has a single shape, a standing wave.

Step-by-step derivationThe string’s operator and its frequencies uses derivatives and integration by parts

The operator and its boundary condition. A string of length L, tension T and mass \mu per unit length obeys the wave equation, derived on the circle page: u_{tt} = -c^2 A u, \qquad A = -\frac{d^2}{dx^2}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}, \qquad u(0) = u(L) = 0 . The same rule -d^2/dx^2 now acts on shapes on the interval [0, L], and only shapes that vanish at both ends are allowed. That restriction, called a Dirichlet condition, changes which shapes are eigenfunctions, and so changes the spectrum. An operator is the rule together with the shapes it is allowed to act on. As on the circle page, the operator proper is c^2A, and we work with A.

Spectrum. The solutions of -X'' = k^2X are a\cos kx + b\sin kx. The condition X(0) = 0 forces a = 0, and X(L) = 0 then needs \sin kL = 0, so k = n\pi/L. The eigenfunctions are \sin(n\pi x/L) for n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, with eigenvalues (n\pi/L)^2, and the frequencies are f_n = \frac{c\,k_n}{2\pi} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} . Exactly whole-number multiples of f_1, as on the circle. (There are no other eigenvalues. Multiply -X'' = \lambda X by X and integrate by parts, using X = 0 at both ends: \lambda\int X^2\,dx = \int (X')^2\,dx. So a zero or negative \lambda would need \int (X')^2\,dx \le 0, which only X = 0 satisfies.) Each eigenvalue now has multiplicity 1, not 2: a wave running along the string reflects at the ends instead of going around, and each reflected pair forms one standing wave.

ProofThe string is half of a circle: the sine series as a Fourier series uses Fourier series and the uniqueness of solutions of the wave equation

Take a shape u on [0, L] with u(0) = u(L) = 0. Extend it to [-L, 0] by u(-x) = -u(x), an odd reflection, and then repeat with period 2L. The result is a shape on a loop of circumference 2L.

  1. Odd shapes stay odd. If u(x, t) solves the wave equation on the loop, so does -u(-x, t), since two derivatives in x do not change sign under x \mapsto -x. Starting from an odd shape at rest, both are the same solution by uniqueness, so the motion stays odd.
  2. Odd shapes on the loop are exactly string shapes. An odd periodic shape vanishes at 0, because u(0) = -u(0), and at L, because u(L) = u(-L) = -u(L). Conversely every string shape extends this way.
  3. So the string’s eigenfunctions are the loop’s odd eigenfunctions. On a loop of circumference 2L the eigenfunctions are \cos(n\pi x/L) and \sin(n\pi x/L) with eigenvalues (n\pi/L)^2. The odd ones are the sines. Each eigenvalue keeps one of its two eigenfunctions, which is why the multiplicity drops from 2 to 1.

The sine series of a string shape, u = \sum_n b_n\sin(n\pi x/L), is therefore the Fourier series of its odd extension, and everything proved on the circle page carries over. The same trick with an even reflection, u(-x) = u(x), gives the cosines and the condition u'(0) = u'(L) = 0; the next page combines both reflections for air in a tube.

Step-by-step derivationThe same result from beads with the ends tied uses matrices, as on page 2

Put N beads of mass m, spaced h apart, on a thread of tension T, with both ends tied, so u_0 = u_{N+1} = 0. The force on bead j is the one derived on the ring page, so m\ddot u_j = \frac{T}{h}(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}). The matrix is now the ring’s matrix without its two corner entries: K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ -1 & 2 & \ddots & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & -1\\ & & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Its eigenvectors are v_j = \sin\big(n\pi j/(N+1)\big), the sampled sines, with eigenvalues 4\sin^2\big(n\pi/(2(N+1))\big) for n = 1, \dots, N. To check: \sin(\alpha(j+1)) + \sin(\alpha(j-1)) = 2\cos\alpha\,\sin(\alpha j), so (Kv)_j = (2 - 2\cos\alpha)\,v_j = 4\sin^2(\alpha/2)\,v_j with \alpha = n\pi/(N+1), and the tied ends hold because \sin 0 = \sin n\pi = 0. As on the ring, letting N \to \infty with L = (N+1)h and \mu = m/h fixed gives the wave equation and the frequencies f_n = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}.

A light touch keeps only the shapes that are still under the finger. Where a shape has a still point, a node, the finger has nothing to grab, and that shape rings on. Every other shape moves under the finger and loses its energy to it within a moment. At the middle, the shapes with a node there are the ones with an even number of arches: 2, 4, 6, …, at 2, 4, 6, … times the open string’s frequency, a note an octave up. At a third, the survivors have 3, 6, 9, … arches: three times the frequency, an octave and a fifth up (a factor 2 times a factor 3/2). (In this simple model the finger drains each shape in proportion to how much that shape moves under it, the rule the piano page derives for felt dampers.)

PreciselyWhich modes survive a light touch uses the sine function

Let the finger touch the string at the distance s from the nut, and write X_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L) for the shape of mode n. In the model, the finger drains mode n at a rate proportional to X_n(s)^2, how much that mode moves under it. The modes that survive are the ones with a node right under the finger: \sin(n\pi s/L) = 0, which means ns/L is a whole number. At s = L/2 these are the even n, at 2f_1, 4f_1, 6f_1, \dots: a note with fundamental 2f_1. At s = L/3 they are n = 3, 6, 9, \dots, with fundamental 3f_1: an octave (a factor 2) and a fifth (a factor 3/2) up, since 2 \times 3/2 = 3.

Where you pluck sets the brightness. A pluck starts each shape according to how much of it is in the plucked triangle. What reaches your ear is the string’s push on the bridge, and for the same height a shape with more arches has steeper ends, so it pushes harder. Pluck near the bridge, and the first several shapes push the bridge about equally hard (near the end, a shape with n arches starts about 1/n as large but is n times as steep, so the pushes come out about equal; fold below): the sound is bright. Pluck at the middle, and every shape with an even number of arches is missing, since those stand still exactly where you pulled; the rest push the bridge less and less, the more arches they have: a mellow, hollow sound.

Step-by-step derivationHow much of each shape a pluck starts uses integration by parts

By the circle page, every shape of the string is a sum \sum_n b_n\sin(n\pi x/L) of its modes, and b_n says how much of mode n the shape contains. A string pulled aside a distance d at a point x_p from one end, and let go, starts as a triangle. With x_p measured from the nut, where x = 0, its sine coefficients are b_n = \frac{2dL^2}{\pi^2 x_p(L - x_p)}\cdot\frac{\sin(n\pi x_p/L)}{n^2}. Measuring x_p from the bridge instead only flips the sign of every second b_n, since \sin(n\pi(L - x_p)/L) = \pm\sin(n\pi x_p/L), and signs do not change how loud a mode is; from here on x_p is the distance from the bridge. What reaches your ear is driven by the force on the bridge, which is proportional to the string’s slope there and so to n\,b_n for mode n. Near the bridge, x_p small, \sin(n\pi x_p/L) \approx n\pi x_p/L for the first several modes, so n b_n hardly falls off at all: the high modes are strong and the sound is bright. At the middle, \sin(n\pi/2) is zero for every even n, and the rest have n b_n falling like 1/n: a mellower, hollow sound.

Where the formula comes from. Take L = 1 and write q = x_p. The starting shape is u_0(x) = dx/q for 0 \le x \le q and u_0(x) = d(1 - x)/(1 - q) for q \le x \le 1. Its sine coefficients are b_n = 2\int_0^1 u_0(x)\sin(n\pi x)\,dx, by the perpendicularity of the sines, which the string inherits from the circle page (second fold above). Integrate by parts twice on each straight piece. Since u_0 vanishes at both ends and u_0'' = 0 on each piece, the only term left comes from the jump in slope at the corner, from d/q just left of it to -d/(1-q) just right of it: b_n = \frac{2}{n^2\pi^2}\big(\tfrac{d}{q} + \tfrac{d}{1-q}\big)\sin(n\pi q) = \frac{2d\sin(n\pi q)}{n^2\pi^2 q(1-q)}. The 1/n^2 comes from that single corner.

Pressing down makes a new end. Pressing a finger firmly onto the fingerboard is different: it ties the string down there, and only the piece between the finger and the bridge sounds. A shorter string sounds higher: two-thirds of the length sounds a fifth up, half the length an octave up, and the semitone positions bunch closer together toward the bridge. Play a whole violin: press and hold a string to bow it, slide sideways to finger it, or switch to plucking.

DetailsWhere the finger goes for each semitone uses powers

The nut is the end of the string near the tuning pegs, and an open string is one not pressed down by a finger. With the finger at distance s from the nut, the vibrating length shrinks to L - s, so the pitch rises by the factor L/(L - s). In equal temperament the twelve semitones of an octave are equal ratios of frequency, so each is 2^{1/12}, and k semitones are 2^{k/12}. Solving L/(L - s) = 2^{k/12} gives s_k = (1 - 2^{-k/12})L: the semitone positions bunch together toward the bridge. These are the ticks on the violin’s fingerboard above.

The bow locks the note. A bow grips the string, drags it along until the string’s pull tears it free, lets it slip back, and grips it again, once per period. By 1860 Hermann von Helmholtz had watched this through a vibration microscope: the string forms two straight pieces joined by one sharp corner, which races between the bridge and the finger. This is Helmholtz motion. It repeats exactly, so its partials sit exactly on the lines. That matters because a real string is slightly stiff, and its own frequencies run a little sharp of the lines (page 7): plucked, each mode rings at its own slightly sharp frequency and fades; bowed, the stick and slip lock the whole motion into exact repetition. The locking comes from the bow’s friction, which is not linear; this page shows it but does not derive it.

Step-by-step derivationHelmholtz motion as a sum of modes uses the plucked-string formula above

A vibration microscope’s lens rides on a vibrating tuning fork, so that a bright speck on the string traces a pattern revealing its motion. Helmholtz reported what he saw in 1860 and in his 1863 book. The expansion of Helmholtz motion in the string’s eigenfunctions is, for some overall size a, u(x,t) = \sum_{n\ge1} \frac{a}{n^2}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\sin(2\pi n f_1 t). Where this comes from: at each moment the string is two straight pieces, like a plucked string, with the corner at some point x_c. Helmholtz showed that the corner’s height is proportional to x_c(L - x_c), so it traces two arcs of a parabola, the lens in the demo, and that the corner moves at the wave speed c = 2Lf_1. Put that height in place of d in the pluck formula above: the factor x_c(L - x_c) cancels and b_n \propto \sin(n\pi x_c/L)/n^2. With x_c = 2Lf_1t this is \sin(2\pi nf_1t)/n^2; on the way back the corner runs along the lower arc, and the same formula covers it: for x_c between L and 2L, \sin(n\pi x_c/L) describes a corner at 2L - x_c with negative height. The motion repeats exactly every 1/f_1 seconds, so its partials sit exactly on nf_1. The force on the bridge is proportional to the string’s slope there, and the slope of \sin(n\pi x/L) carries an extra factor n\pi/L, so partial n pushes the bridge with strength proportional to 1/n. On an ideal string a pluck also gives exact multiples of f_1; the difference between plucking and bowing shows only on a real, slightly stiff string, as described above.

The body changes loudness, not pitch. The string itself moves very little air. It rocks the bridge, the bridge shakes the wooden body, and the body moves the air. Each step is linear: shaken at one frequency, it responds at that same frequency, only louder or quieter and a little delayed. So the body can change each partial’s height but can never move it to another frequency. Toggle the wooden body on the violin above: the stems change height and stay where they are.

What we found

Tying a string’s ends down keeps half of the loop’s waves, so its frequencies still land exactly on the lines. A light touch drains every wave that moves under the finger, so only those already still there survive: at the middle, every second one, which is a note an octave up. And the operator lesson: the rule for what the ends may do, a boundary condition, is part of the operator. It decides which shapes are allowed, and so which frequencies.

Same operator, different constants. Guitar, cello and harp strings obey the same equation with the same tied ends. They differ in length, tension and mass per length, which set the lowest frequency, and in how they are played: plucked (guitar, harp) or bowed (violin, cello).
