Builds on page 3: on a loop, the operator -d^2/d\theta^2 has eigenfunctions \cos n\theta and \sin n\theta with eigenvalues n^2. About 10 minutes, plus 5 for the folds.

The puzzle. Touch a vibrating string lightly at its exact middle, without pressing it down, and the note jumps up an octave. Touch it a third of the way along and it jumps to three times the frequency, an octave and a fifth up. Pluck it near the bridge and it sounds bright; pluck it near the middle and it sounds mellow. What are the finger and the pluck doing?

Try it. Pluck the string, then pick a point to touch and pluck again. String players call these notes harmonics.

Touch lightly at

Left end: the nut; right end: the bridge. The motion is slowed down for the eye, while modes fade at their real rates. Stems (the vertical lines): how strong each mode is a quarter of a second after the pluck, at 1, 2, 3, … times the open (untouched) string's 196 Hz. The touching finger is modeled as extra damping, energy loss, on every mode that moves under it, the same model as the piano's dampers on page 7.

And here is a whole violin to play: press and hold a string, slide to finger it, and choose the bow or plucking.

Press and hold a string. Slide left and right to finger it.

Left edge is the nut, right edge the bridge. The ticks mark equal-tempered semitones, at fractions 1 − 2−k/12 of the length. The stems are the partials you hear. The body passes each partial on at its own frequency, only louder or quieter (it is linear, as explained below), so switching it off or on changes their heights, never their positions. Its resonances, the frequencies at which it vibrates most easily, here (about 280, 470, 550 and 2700 Hz) are rough values chosen to illustrate; see the references.

Same operator, different constants. Guitar, cello and harp strings obey the same equation with the same tied ends. They differ in length, tension and mass per length, which set the lowest frequency, and in how they are played: plucked (guitar, harp) or bowed (violin, cello).

To explain the harmonics, we need to know what a string’s ends do to the circle’s operator.

Tie the ends down

The operator and its boundary condition. A string of length L, tension T and mass \mu per unit length obeys the wave equation, derived on the circle page: u_{tt} = -c^2 A u, \qquad A = -\frac{d^2}{dx^2}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}, \qquad u(0) = u(L) = 0 . The same rule -d^2/dx^2 now acts on shapes on the interval [0, L], and only shapes that vanish at both ends are allowed. That restriction, called a Dirichlet condition, changes which shapes are eigenfunctions, and so changes the spectrum. An operator is the rule together with the shapes it is allowed to act on. As on the circle page, the operator proper is c^2A, and we work with A.

Spectrum. The solutions of -X'' = k^2X are a\cos kx + b\sin kx. The condition X(0) = 0 forces a = 0, and X(L) = 0 then needs \sin kL = 0, so k = n\pi/L. The eigenfunctions are \sin(n\pi x/L) for n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, with eigenvalues (n\pi/L)^2, and the frequencies are f_n = \frac{c\,k_n}{2\pi} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} . Exactly whole-number multiples of f_1, as on the circle. (There are no other eigenvalues. Multiply -X'' = \lambda X by X and integrate by parts, using X = 0 at both ends: \lambda\int X^2\,dx = \int (X')^2\,dx. So a zero or negative \lambda would need \int (X')^2\,dx \le 0, which only X = 0 satisfies.) Each eigenvalue now has multiplicity 1, not 2: a wave running along the string reflects at the ends instead of going around, and each reflected pair forms one standing wave.

ProofThe string is half of a circle: the sine series as a Fourier series uses Fourier series and the uniqueness of solutions of the wave equation

Take a shape u on [0, L] with u(0) = u(L) = 0. Extend it to [-L, 0] by u(-x) = -u(x), an odd reflection, and then repeat with period 2L. The result is a shape on a loop of circumference 2L.

  1. Odd shapes stay odd. If u(x, t) solves the wave equation on the loop, so does -u(-x, t), since two derivatives in x do not change sign under x \mapsto -x. Starting from an odd shape at rest, both are the same solution by uniqueness, so the motion stays odd.
  2. Odd shapes on the loop are exactly string shapes. An odd periodic shape vanishes at 0, because u(0) = -u(0), and at L, because u(L) = u(-L) = -u(L). Conversely every string shape extends this way.
  3. So the string’s eigenfunctions are the loop’s odd eigenfunctions. On a loop of circumference 2L the eigenfunctions are \cos(n\pi x/L) and \sin(n\pi x/L) with eigenvalues (n\pi/L)^2. The odd ones are the sines. Each eigenvalue keeps one of its two eigenfunctions, which is why the multiplicity drops from 2 to 1.

The sine series of a string shape, u = \sum_n b_n\sin(n\pi x/L), is therefore the Fourier series of its odd extension, and everything proved on the circle page carries over. The same trick with an even reflection, u(-x) = u(x), gives the cosines and the condition u'(0) = u'(L) = 0; the next page combines both reflections for air in a tube.

Step-by-step derivationThe same result from beads with the ends tied uses matrices, as on page 2

Put N beads of mass m, spaced h apart, on a thread of tension T, with both ends tied, so u_0 = u_{N+1} = 0. The force on bead j is the one derived on the ring page, so m\ddot u_j = \frac{T}{h}(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}). The matrix is now the ring’s matrix without its two corner entries: K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ -1 & 2 & \ddots & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & -1\\ & & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Its eigenvectors are v_j = \sin\big(n\pi j/(N+1)\big), the sampled sines, with eigenvalues 4\sin^2\big(n\pi/(2(N+1))\big) for n = 1, \dots, N. To check: \sin(\alpha(j+1)) + \sin(\alpha(j-1)) = 2\cos\alpha\,\sin(\alpha j), so (Kv)_j = (2 - 2\cos\alpha)\,v_j = 4\sin^2(\alpha/2)\,v_j with \alpha = n\pi/(N+1), and the tied ends hold because \sin 0 = \sin n\pi = 0. As on the ring, letting N \to \infty with L = (N+1)h and \mu = m/h fixed gives the wave equation and the frequencies f_n = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}.

The finger and the still points

Let the finger touch the string at the distance s from the nut, and write X_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L) for the shape of mode n. A light touch is a soft contact that soaks up energy: it barely changes the modes, but it drains each one in proportion to how much that mode moves under the finger, roughly X_n(s)^2. The piano page derives this rule for the piano’s felt dampers; for a finger it is a rough guide, not an exact law. The modes that survive are the ones with a node, a point that never moves, right under the finger: \sin(n\pi s/L) = 0, which means ns/L is a whole number. At the middle, s = L/2, the surviving modes are the even ones, at 2f_1, 4f_1, 6f_1, \dots: a note with fundamental 2f_1, an octave up. At s = L/3 they are n = 3, 6, 9, \dots, with fundamental 3f_1: an octave (a factor 2) and a fifth (a factor 3/2) up, since 2 \times 3/2 = 3. Pressing the string down firmly is different, as described under Fingers below.

Where you pluck

By the circle page, every shape of the string is a sum \sum_n b_n\sin(n\pi x/L) of its modes, and b_n says how much of mode n the shape contains. A string pulled aside a distance d at a point x_p from one end, and let go, starts as a triangle. With x_p measured from the nut, where x = 0, its sine coefficients are b_n = \frac{2dL^2}{\pi^2 x_p(L - x_p)}\cdot\frac{\sin(n\pi x_p/L)}{n^2}. Measuring x_p from the bridge instead only flips the sign of every second b_n, since \sin(n\pi(L - x_p)/L) = \pm\sin(n\pi x_p/L), and signs do not change how loud a mode is; from here on x_p is the distance from the bridge. What reaches your ear is driven by the force on the bridge, which is proportional to the string’s slope there and so to n\,b_n for mode n. Near the bridge, x_p small, \sin(n\pi x_p/L) \approx n\pi x_p/L for the first several modes, so n b_n hardly falls off at all: the high modes are strong and the sound is bright. At the middle, \sin(n\pi/2) is zero for every even n, and the rest have n b_n falling like 1/n: a mellower, hollow sound.

Step-by-step derivationThe sine coefficients of a plucked string uses integration by parts

Take L = 1 and write q = x_p. The starting shape is u_0(x) = dx/q for 0 \le x \le q and u_0(x) = d(1 - x)/(1 - q) for q \le x \le 1. Its sine coefficients are b_n = 2\int_0^1 u_0(x)\sin(n\pi x)\,dx, by the perpendicularity of the sines, which the first fold above inherits from the circle page. Integrate by parts twice on each straight piece. Since u_0 vanishes at both ends and u_0'' = 0 on each piece, the only term left comes from the jump in slope at the corner, from d/q just left of it to -d/(1-q) just right of it: b_n = \frac{2}{n^2\pi^2}\big(\tfrac{d}{q} + \tfrac{d}{1-q}\big)\sin(n\pi q) = \frac{2d\sin(n\pi q)}{n^2\pi^2 q(1-q)}. The 1/n^2 comes from that single corner.

Fingers. The nut is the end of the string near the tuning pegs, and an open string is one not pressed down by a finger. A finger pressed firmly onto the fingerboard makes a new tied end, and the piece between the finger and the bridge sounds on its own. With the finger at distance s from the nut, the vibrating length shrinks to L - s, so the pitch rises by the factor L/(L - s). In equal temperament the twelve semitones of an octave are equal ratios of frequency, so each is 2^{1/12}, and k semitones are 2^{k/12}. Solving L/(L - s) = 2^{k/12} gives s_k = (1 - 2^{-k/12})L: the semitone positions bunch together toward the bridge.

The bow. The bow grips the string and drags it along until the string’s pull tears it free; it slips back, and is gripped again. Hermann von Helmholtz watched it with a vibration microscope, whose lens rides on a vibrating tuning fork, so that a bright speck on the string traces a pattern revealing its motion, and reported it in 1860 and in his 1863 book: the string forms two straight pieces joined by one sharp corner, and the corner races back and forth between the bridge and the finger, once per period. This is called Helmholtz motion. Its expansion in the string’s eigenfunctions is, for some overall size a, u(x,t) = \sum_{n\ge1} \frac{a}{n^2}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\sin(2\pi n f_1 t). Where this comes from: at each moment the string is two straight pieces, like a plucked string, with the corner at some point x_c. Helmholtz showed that the corner’s height is proportional to x_c(L - x_c), so it traces two arcs of a parabola, the lens in the demo, and that the corner moves at the wave speed c = 2Lf_1. Put that height in place of d in the pluck formula above: the factor x_c(L - x_c) cancels and b_n \propto \sin(n\pi x_c/L)/n^2. With x_c = 2Lf_1t this is \sin(2\pi nf_1t)/n^2; on the way back the corner runs along the lower arc, and the same formula covers it: for x_c between L and 2L, \sin(n\pi x_c/L) describes a corner at 2L - x_c with negative height. The motion repeats exactly every 1/f_1 seconds, so its partials sit exactly on nf_1. The force on the bridge is proportional to the string’s slope there, and the slope of \sin(n\pi x/L) carries an extra factor n\pi/L, so partial n pushes the bridge with strength proportional to 1/n. On an ideal string a pluck also gives exact multiples of f_1. The difference shows on a real string, which is slightly stiff, so its own frequencies run a little sharp of the whole numbers (page 7): plucked, each mode rings at its own slightly sharp frequency and fades; bowed, the stick and slip lock the whole motion into exact repetition. That locking is a property of the bow’s friction, which is not linear, and is observed rather than derived here.

Slowed about 500 times. The faint lens-shaped outline is the path the corner traces. Stems: partials of the bridge force.

The body. The string itself moves very little air. It rocks the bridge, the bridge shakes the wooden body, and the body moves the air. Each step is linear, and a linear system shaken at one frequency responds at that same frequency, only scaled and delayed. So the body can make each partial louder or quieter but can never move it to another frequency. Toggle the wooden body in the first demo: the stems change height and stay where they are.

What we found

A boundary condition, the rule for what the ends of the string may do, is part of the operator. Tying the ends down keeps the circle’s operator but keeps only half of its eigenfunctions, the sines, so the frequencies are still exactly whole-number multiples. A light touch drains every mode that moves under the finger, so only the modes that already have a still point there survive.

The next puzzle

A flute and a clarinet are both tubes of air, and about the same length. Yet the clarinet’s lowest note is almost an octave below the flute’s, and when the players overblow, making the tube jump to its next mode, the flute jumps up an octave while the clarinet jumps an octave and a fifth. The same operator governs the air in both. What is different?
