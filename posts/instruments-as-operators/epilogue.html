Did instruments shape our scales?

Play two notes an octave apart, then two notes a semitone apart, the smallest step on a piano. The octave sounds smooth and the semitone sounds rough. Why? And why does a Javanese gamelan, an orchestra of tuned metal bars and gongs, use scales that fit no piano?

Pick a sound, then slide the interval between two notes of that sound and listen. The curve shows how rough each interval sounds.

Solid: roughness of two notes of the chosen sound against the ratio between them, lower note at 262 Hz; faint: the other sound. Dots: the dips. Each curve is scaled to its own peak, so compare where the dips are, not the heights.

The gist. Two notes sound rough when their partials sit close together but not on top of each other, so they beat. A string-like sound has its partials on the lines of the pitch ruler, and then the partials of two notes land exactly on each other at simple whole-number ratios, such as 3 : 2 and 2 : 1: those intervals sound smooth. A metal bar’s partials are off the lines, so its smooth intervals move, and the exact octave is no longer one of them. The engineer and musician William Sethares argues that this is one reason scales fit the instruments they are played on.

On the roughness curve

The curve shows how rough two notes of the chosen sound are, against the ratio between their frequencies, from 1 (the same note) to 3. The dips are the smoothest intervals. With the string-like sound, up to the octave, the dips sit at 6 : 5, 5 : 4, 4 : 3, 3 : 2, 5 : 3 and 2 : 1: the intervals of Western harmony. Switch to the metal bar and the dips move. The octave, 2 : 1, is no longer a dip; a slightly narrower interval, about 1.96, is smoother.

How it works

Close partials beat. Two pure tones close in frequency produce beats: their sum swells and fades at the difference of their frequencies. Slow beats sound like a wobble, faster ones like roughness, and once the tones are far enough apart the roughness fades away and you hear two separate, smooth tones. In 1965 Reinier Plomp and Willem Levelt asked listeners how dissonant pairs of pure tones sounded, as a function of their separation; for two pure tones, the answers tracked this roughness. In Sethares’ fit to their results, the roughest separation near middle C is about 22 Hz. A note from a real instrument is several pure tones at once, its partials, so for two notes Sethares adds up the roughness of every pair of partials. That sum is the curve in the demo.

PreciselyThe roughness of two notes, in symbols uses the exponential function

Sethares’ fit to Plomp and Levelt’s data, for two pure tones of equal loudness, the lower at frequency f and the other x hertz above it, is \delta(f, x) = e^{-3.5\,s\,x} - e^{-5.75\,s\,x}, \qquad s = \frac{0.24}{0.021\,f + 19}, with frequencies in hertz. It is zero at x = 0, rises to a peak at x = \ln(5.75/3.5)/(2.25\,s), about 22 Hz near middle C, and then decays. For tones of amplitudes a_1 and a_2, Sethares multiplies by a_1 a_2, as in D(\alpha) below. The factor s makes the roughest separation grow with the frequency, as Plomp and Levelt found: tones high up must be further apart, in hertz, to sound equally smooth. The formula describes average listeners’ judgments of roughness for pure tones; it is a fitted curve, not derived from first principles.

For two notes, Sethares adds up the roughness of every pair of partials, from either note, each weighted by the product of the two partials’ amplitudes: D(\alpha) = \sum_{\text{pairs } i < j} a_i a_j\; \delta\big(\min(f_i, f_j),\ |f_i - f_j|\big). Here the list f_1, f_2, \dots holds the partials of both notes: those of the lower note, and those of the upper note, whose frequencies are \alpha times the lower note’s; a_i is the amplitude of partial f_i. The roughness \delta of two pure tones depends on how far apart they are and, a little, on how high they are, hence its two inputs. The curve in the demo is D(\alpha). In the demo, the string-like sound has six partials with strengths falling by a factor 0.88 each, and the metal bar has the first four modes of a uniform bar free at both ends, with strengths 1, 0.7, 0.5 and 0.35. Both sets of strengths are chosen for illustration.

Partials that coincide don’t beat. In a string-like sound the partials of the lower note sit at 1, 2, 3, … times its frequency, and those of the upper note at the same multiples of the upper note’s frequency. When the ratio between the notes is a ratio of small whole numbers, say 3 : 2, many partials of the two notes land exactly on each other: the lower note’s third partial and the upper note’s second both sit at three times the lower note’s frequency. Partials that coincide do not beat. Move off 3 : 2 and those pairs pull apart, by a few hertz and then by tens of hertz, into the range where beating sounds roughest. So each simple ratio sits at the bottom of a dip. This extends the idea behind the whole series: frequencies on the lines give a clear pitch, and they also make whole-number intervals smooth.

A metal bar moves the dips. A uniform bar free at both ends, such as a glockenspiel bar, has the kalimba’s operator with different ends, and its partials are at 1, 2.756, 5.404 and 8.933 times the lowest: off the lines. Now the coincidences happen elsewhere. At a ratio of 2.756, the upper note’s lowest partial lands on the lower note’s second. At about 1.961, the upper note’s second partial lands on the lower note’s third (1.961 × 2.756 ≈ 5.404), and that is the dip just below the octave. At exactly 2, nothing lines up. (Xylophone and marimba bars are thinned underneath, in the middle, to move the first overtone onto a whole-number multiple, 3 on a xylophone and 4 on a marimba; gamelan bars are shaped too.)

Step-by-step derivationThe modes of a bar free at both ends uses a 2×2 determinant and hyperbolic functions

The operator is the bar’s \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4 from the kalimba page. A free end carries no bending moment and no shear force, so at both x = 0 and x = L the conditions are X'' = 0 and X''' = 0.

  1. As on the kalimba page, write X = A_1\cos\beta x + A_2\sin\beta x + A_3\cosh\beta x + A_4\sinh\beta x.
  2. At x = 0: X''(0) = \beta^2(-A_1 + A_3) = 0 and X'''(0) = \beta^3(-A_2 + A_4) = 0, so A_3 = A_1 and A_4 = A_2. Then X = A_1(\cos\beta x + \cosh\beta x) + A_2(\sin\beta x + \sinh\beta x).
  3. At x = L, dividing by \beta^2 and \beta^3 and writing C_1 = \cos\beta L, S_1 = \sin\beta L, C_2 = \cosh\beta L, S_2 = \sinh\beta L, as on the kalimba page: (C_2 - C_1)\,A_1 + (S_2 - S_1)\,A_2 = 0, \qquad (S_2 + S_1)\,A_1 + (C_2 - C_1)\,A_2 = 0 .
  4. These have a solution other than A_1 = A_2 = 0 exactly when the determinant is zero: (C_2 - C_1)^2 - (S_2 - S_1)(S_2 + S_1) = (C_2^2 - S_2^2) + (C_1^2 + S_1^2) - 2C_1C_2 = 2 - 2C_1C_2, using C_2^2 - S_2^2 = 1 and C_1^2 + S_1^2 = 1. So \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = 1 . Besides \beta = 0, which belongs to the bar moving or rotating rigidly without bending, the roots are \beta L = 4.7300,\ 7.8532,\ 10.9956,\ 14.1372, \dots, computed on this page. Since the frequency is proportional to \beta^2, the partials are at (\beta_n/\beta_1)^2 = 1,\ 2.756,\ 5.404,\ 8.933 times the lowest.

The gamelan. Sethares measured the spectra of gamelan metallophones, instruments of tuned metal bars, and reports that “the dissonance curve for these metallophones have minima that occur at or near the scale steps used by the gamelans”. The demo’s bars are idealized, not measured gamelan bars, so it shows the mechanism, not his result; real gamelan bars are shaped by their makers, and tunings vary from one gamelan to another.

Roughness is not the whole story. The curve measures sensory roughness, which is one ingredient of what sounds pleasant, not all of it. In a study of more than 250 American college students, Josh McDermott, Andriana Lehr and Andrew Oxenham found in 2010 that liking for consonant chords followed harmonicity, whether the frequencies fit one harmonic series, more than it followed beating, and grew with musical training. In 2016 McDermott and colleagues found that the Tsimane’, a society in the Bolivian Amazon with little exposure to Western music, rated consonant and dissonant chords as equally pleasant, although they disliked roughness just as other groups did.

What we found

An instrument’s spectrum does not only decide whether it has a pitch. It decides which pairs of notes sound smooth together on it: for instruments with their partials on the lines, the simple whole-number ratios of Western harmony; for metal bars, with their partials off the lines, other intervals. That much is a fact about sound and the ear. What a culture finds beautiful is also learned. Sethares argues that instruments and the scales played on them tend to fit each other in the way the demo shows; how much that shaped the world’s music is an open, argued question.
