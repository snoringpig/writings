Can you hear the shape of a drum?

In 1966 the mathematician Mark Kac asked: if you could hear every frequency a drum makes, perfectly, could you work out the drum’s shape? The two drums below have different shapes. Listen to them.

Play each drum with every mode equally loud. Then click a drum to strike it at a point, and compare.

Color shows the selected mode: one color where the drumhead is up, the other where it is down. Both drums show the mode with the same frequency, in different shapes. Click a drum to strike it there; the stems then show how strongly each of the first 12 modes starts, heard at the struck point. The lowest frequency is set to 150 Hz.

The gist. No. These two drums have different shapes but exactly the same frequencies: every mode of one has a partner on the other at the same frequency, so played with every mode equally loud, they sound identical. The list of frequencies still knows a lot. It fixes a drum’s area and, for a smooth or straight-sided rim, the length of its rim, and that is enough to recognize a round drum. Click to strike the drums, though, and they sound different: their modes have different shapes, so a strike starts them with different strengths.

On the pitch ruler

Here are both drums’ first twelve frequencies, as multiples of the lowest: 1×, 1.20×, 1.43×, 1.60×, 1.69×, 1.90×, 2.04×, …. Like the drum on page 8, they sit off the gray whole-number lines, so neither drum has a clear pitch. And the two lists are the same to every digit computed: the stems under drum A and drum B stand in the same places. Each stem’s height is how strongly a strike at the clicked point starts that mode: all equal until you click, then different on the two drums, because it depends on the mode’s shape at the struck point.

How it works

A list of frequencies comes from a list of eigenvalues. In the series’ language: on page 8 the drumhead became an operator, a rule that turns a shape of the drumhead into the force pulling it back toward rest. Its eigenvalues give the drum’s frequencies, and its eigenfunctions are the mode shapes. Kac’s question asks whether the frequencies alone, with nothing about the mode shapes, fix the shape of the drumhead.

PreciselyKac’s question in symbols uses the Laplacian (page 8)

A drum’s operator, from the drum page, is -c^2\Delta on the drumhead \Omega, a bounded region of the plane in one piece, with u = 0 on the rim. Here \Delta is the Laplacian. Write \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \lambda_3 \le \cdots for the eigenvalues of -\Delta, each repeated as many times as it occurs (its multiplicity). The drum’s frequencies are then f_k = \frac{c}{2\pi}\sqrt{\lambda_k}. Kac’s question, in these terms, is whether the list \lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots determines the shape of \Omega. Kac wrote that he had first heard the question posed this way about ten years earlier, from Salomon Bochner.

The list knows the area and the length of the rim. Count how many of a drum’s frequencies lie below some limit. As the limit rises, the count grows in proportion to the drum’s area: twice the area, about twice as many modes below any high limit. This is Weyl’s law, from 1911, so the area can be heard. The count also falls short of the area’s share, by an amount in proportion to the length of the rim: holding the rim still costs the drum some modes. (Proved, in a slightly smoothed form, for every rim that is smooth or made of straight pieces; the fold has the details.) The demo counts the frequencies of three drums with the same area: once the area’s share, the same for all three, is taken away, the drum with the longest rim sits lowest.

Three drums of equal area: a disk, a square, a long rectangle. Steps: the true counts. Dashed: Weyl's formula, area and rim together. Gray: the area's share alone, the same for all three.

PreciselyWeyl’s law and the rim term uses limits

Count the eigenvalues up to \lambda: N(\lambda) = \#\{k : \lambda_k \le \lambda\}, where \# means “the number of”. For large \lambda, N(\lambda) \approx \frac{A}{4\pi}\,\lambda \;-\; \frac{P}{4\pi}\sqrt{\lambda}, where A is the area of the drum and P the length of its rim, and “\approx” means that the error, divided by \sqrt\lambda, shrinks to zero as \lambda grows. The first term is Weyl’s law. Hermann Weyl proved it in 1911, and later work extended it to every drum. So the area can be heard: it is 4\pi times the rate at which N grows. In the demo all three drums have area \pi (a disk of radius 1, a square, and a rectangle 4 times as long as it is wide), so the area’s share is \lambda/4, and “minus the area’s share” plots N(\lambda) - \lambda/4, roughly -P\sqrt\lambda/4\pi: the longer the rim, the lower the curve. The demo’s frequency limit is shown as a multiple of the disk’s lowest frequency, \sqrt{\lambda/\lambda_1}.

The second term is subtler. Weyl conjectured it, and Victor Ivrii proved it in 1980 for drums with smooth rims, provided that closed billiard paths are rare. A billiard path is the path of a ball rolling inside the drum and bouncing off the rim, angle in equal to angle out; it is closed if it comes back to where it started, heading the same way. “Rare” means that if you pick a starting point and a direction at random, the chance of landing on a closed path is zero. Ivrii conjectured that this always holds; it has not been proved. The square and the rectangle in the demo have corners, which his theorem does not cover, but for them the formula can be checked by counting, as in the next fold. The perimeter can be heard in any case, for every drum whose rim is smooth or a polygon, by a smoother way of counting; that is the fold after it.

Proof sketchWeyl’s law for a square, by counting dots uses partial derivatives

Take the square of side s. Its eigenfunctions are \sin(m\pi x/s)\sin(n\pi y/s) for whole numbers m, n \ge 1, with eigenvalues (\pi/s)^2(m^2 + n^2); substituting them into the equation checks this, as for the circle on page 3. So N(\lambda) is the number of dots (m, n), with m, n \ge 1, inside the quarter circle of radius R = s\sqrt\lambda/\pi.

Give each dot the unit square centered on it. These squares nearly fill the quarter disk, which has area \pi R^2/4. What they miss is a strip of width \tfrac12 along each axis, of area about R/2 each, shaded in the figure; the squares sticking out past the arc and the gaps inside it roughly cancel. So N(\lambda) \approx \frac{\pi R^2}{4} - R = \frac{s^2}{4\pi}\lambda - \frac{4s}{4\pi}\sqrt\lambda = \frac{A}{4\pi}\lambda - \frac{P}{4\pi}\sqrt\lambda . In the figure, R = 5.3: there are 17 dots, and the formula gives 16.8. The strips are missing because m = 0 and n = 0 give the zero function, not an eigenfunction: the rim’s condition u = 0 is what costs the perimeter term. Making “roughly cancel” precise is a classical problem about counting lattice points in a circle.

Proof sketchHearing the perimeter of any drum: a smoother count uses the heat equation and the reflection trick of page 4

N(\lambda) jumps by one at every eigenvalue, and those jumps can bunch up, which is what Ivrii’s condition controls. A smoother count avoids them. For t > 0 let Z(t) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}. Each eigenvalue adds nearly 1 if \lambda_k is much less than 1/t and nearly 0 if it is much more, so Z(t) is roughly N(1/t), with the jumps smoothed out. Like N, Z is determined by the list of eigenvalues.

Z has a physical meaning. Heat spreading on the drumhead, with the rim held at temperature 0, obeys v_t = \Delta v, the drum’s equation with one time derivative instead of two, so each mode now decays like e^{-\lambda_k t} instead of oscillating. Start a unit burst of heat at a point x and let it spread for time t. The temperature it leaves at x itself, added up over all points x of the drum, is Z(t). (Write the burst as a sum of modes, each scaled so that \iint X_k^2\,dA = 1. A burst at x contains mode k in proportion to X_k(x), the same rule as a strike on the drum page; after time t that part has shrunk by e^{-\lambda_k t}, and its temperature at x is again proportional to X_k(x). So the temperature left at x is \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}X_k(x)^2, and adding it up over the drum gives \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}.) In a short time t heat travels a distance of about \sqrt t, so a burst only feels the rim if it starts within about that distance of it. Kac called this “not feeling the boundary”. Then:

  1. Away from the rim, the burst spreads as on an endless plane, and the temperature it leaves at its starting point is 1/(4\pi t). Adding this up over the drum gives A/(4\pi t).
  2. Near a straight piece of rim, at distance r from it, the rim’s condition v = 0 is met by adding a mirror-image burst of opposite sign on the far side of the rim, the odd reflection of the string page. The image is a distance 2r away, and lowers the temperature at x by e^{-r^2/t}/(4\pi t). Adding this up over a strip along the rim, using \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/t}\,dr = \tfrac12\sqrt{\pi t} for each unit of rim length, gives -P/(8\sqrt{\pi t}).

So, as t \to 0, Z(t) = \frac{A}{4\pi t} - \frac{P}{8\sqrt{\pi t}} + (\text{terms that stay bounded}). Knowing Z, one reads off A from the first term and then P from the second. Kac gave this argument in his 1966 paper. Åke Pleijel had already proved the formula for smooth rims in 1954, by another method; Henry McKean and Isadore Singer proved in 1967 the next, constant term, which Kac had conjectured, from an argument that was not a full proof: (1 - h)/6 for a drum with h holes and a smooth rim; and Michiel van den Berg and Sivakolundu Srisatkunarajah proved the formula for polygons in 1988. Unlike N, Z needs no condition on billiard paths, because averaging over a range of eigenvalues smooths out the bunching.

So a round drum can be heard. Of all shapes with the same area, the disk has the shortest rim. Both the area and the rim’s length can be heard, so a drum, with a smooth or straight-sided rim, that has exactly a disk’s frequencies has the disk’s area and the disk’s rim length, and so it must be a disk. Kac pointed this out in his 1966 paper: “By pitch alone one can thus determine whether a drum is circular or not!”

PreciselyThe disk argument in symbols uses the isoperimetric inequality

The isoperimetric inequality says that every drum satisfies P^2 \ge 4\pi A, with equality only for the disk. Both sides can be heard, by the folds above. So a drum, with a smooth or polygonal rim, that has exactly the frequencies of a disk has the disk’s area and perimeter, so P^2 = 4\pi A, so it is a disk. Kac makes this remark in section 10 of his 1966 paper.

But in general, no. Two years before Kac’s paper, John Milnor had found two different shapes with the same frequencies, and Kac said so; but they were 16-dimensional and had no rim. (They are flat tori: 16-dimensional versions of a video-game screen whose edges wrap around, so that leaving on the right brings you back on the left.) For drums in the plane the question stayed open for 26 years. In 1992 Carolyn Gordon, David Webb and Scott Wolpert answered it: no. They found two different polygons with exactly the same frequencies, repeats included: the two drums at the top of this page. Such drums are called isospectral. Since area and rim length can be heard, they must share them, and they do: each is made of seven copies of the same right triangle, with two short sides of length 1, so each has area 3.5 and rim length 6 + 3\sqrt2.

Why do they share every frequency? Cut each drum into its seven triangles. Any mode of drum A can be rebuilt on drum B: fill each of B’s triangles with three triangle-shaped pieces of the mode, taken from A’s triangles, turned or flipped to fit, some with their signs reversed. With the right recipe, the pieces join smoothly across every seam and stay still on B’s rim, so the result is a mode of B, at the same frequency. The recipe runs backwards too, so the two drums have exactly the same frequencies, with the same number of modes at each.

DetailsTransplantation: carrying a mode from one drum to the other uses the Laplacian (page 8)

Cut each drum along its seams into its seven triangles. Take any eigenfunction X of drum A, with eigenvalue \lambda. On each triangle of drum B, place a sum of three pieces of X, each taken from one of A’s triangles, turned or reflected to fit, and some with a minus sign. Each piece satisfies -\Delta X = \lambda X, so the sum does too, inside each triangle. With the right choice of pieces and signs, the result is zero on B’s rim, and across every seam of B both its values and its slopes match. A function that satisfies the equation on both sides of a line, with values and slopes matching on the line, satisfies it across the line too, a standard fact about this equation. At the points where several seams meet, one more standard fact closes the gap: a solution that stays bounded around a single point, and satisfies the equation everywhere near it except possibly at the point itself, satisfies it there too. So the result is an eigenfunction of B with the same \lambda. The construction can be reversed, so each eigenvalue has the same multiplicity on both drums.

This argument is called transplantation. It grew out of work of Peter Buser on surfaces in the 1980s and of Pierre Bérard around 1992; the version for drums like these is in a 1994 paper of Buser, John Conway, Peter Doyle and Klaus-Dieter Semmler. The seven formulas, with pictures, are in Cleve Moler’s blog post on transplantation, and in S. J. Chapman’s article, which does the same by folding paper. Gordon, Webb and Wolpert’s own proof used a method of Toshikazu Sunada from 1985 for building isospectral spaces from group theory; Wikipedia has an overview.

DetailsHow the numbers on this page were computed uses matrices, as on page 2

Each drum is covered by a square grid of spacing h, and the Laplacian is replaced by its five-point version: at each grid point, the sum of the four neighbors minus four times the point, divided by h^2. This is the bead model of page 2 in two dimensions. Grid points on the rim are held at zero. The drums’ corners sit on grid points, so whether each point is inside, on the rim, or outside is decided with exact whole-number arithmetic.

At h = 1/40 and h = 1/80 the two drums give the same first twelve eigenvalues to a relative difference of 3\times10^{-14}, which is rounding error. This is expected, as far as I can tell, because the grid lines up with every triangle’s edges, so transplantation works for the grid model too; Moler’s post transplants grid eigenvectors in the same way. Combining the two grids (Richardson extrapolation) gives values within 0.03% of the high-accuracy values in Table 3.1 of Driscoll’s paper, after multiplying his by 4: his drums are twice the size of these. That is a check, not a precise computation: Richardson’s trick assumes the grid error shrinks like h^2, and the drums’ inward-pointing corners slow that down. The page uses Driscoll’s values.

One eigenvalue is known exactly, the ninth. Its mode is, on each triangle, that triangle’s own lowest mode, \sin\pi x\,\sin 2\pi y - \sin 2\pi x\,\sin\pi y in coordinates where the triangle has corners (0,0), (1,0) and (1,1), with its sign flipping across each seam. Substituting gives the eigenvalue \pi^2(1 + 4) = 5\pi^2 = 49.348\ldots, and Driscoll’s value agrees to every digit he gives. On the grid, the ninth eigenvalue equals the grid version of this mode’s eigenvalue to rounding error, a second check.

The scripts are gww.py for the eigenvalues and gww_modes.py for the mode pictures. The corner coordinates are those in Moler’s first post. The mode pictures use the coarser grid h = 1/40, sampled every 0.1.

Same frequencies, not the same sound. A struck drum’s sound is a sum of its modes, each at its own frequency and with a strength that depends on where it is struck and where it is heard. The two drums share the frequencies. The mode shapes differ, so the strengths from a strike at a given point differ, and so does the timbre, the color of the sound. What the drums share is the answer to “which frequencies can this drum make”, which is exactly what the spectrum records.

What we found

A drum’s list of frequencies knows its area and the length of its rim, and for a round drum that pins down the shape. In general it does not: two differently shaped drums can share every frequency. On the pitch ruler, both drums are off the lines, like the ideal drumhead of page 8, and in exactly the same places. Every earlier page went from an instrument to its spectrum; this one went backwards, asking what the spectrum says about the instrument. The study of such questions is called spectral geometry.

The next puzzle

One question is left, and it goes beyond mathematics: did the instruments people built shape the music they made?
