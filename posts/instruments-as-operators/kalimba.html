The kalimba

Part 3 of Instruments as Operators

Series · 0 Foundations · 1 Violin · 2 Clarinet · 3 Kalimba · 4 Piano · 5 Drum

Take away the tension and keep only stiffness. A steel tine clamped at one end obeys a fourth-order equation, its overtones scatter far from the integers, and it still sounds pitched.

Play it

Tap a tine, or drag across several.

Tine lengths are drawn from the operator: frequency goes as 1/L², so each tine's length is the C4 tine's divided by the square root of its frequency ratio. Tick the box and pluck again to hear the same spectrum as a bell.

Deriving the operator

  1. Bend a thin bar. Fibers on the outside of the curve stretch and fibers on the inside compress. By Hooke’s law their stresses add up to a bending moment proportional to the curvature, M = EI\,u_{xx}, where E is Young’s modulus of the steel and I is the second moment of area of the cross-section.
  2. Cut out a slice of length dx. Its two faces carry moments and vertical shear forces. A thin slice has negligible rotational inertia, so its torques balance, which forces the shear to be the slope of the moment: V = -M_x.
  3. Vertical forces do not balance; their difference accelerates the slice. With density \rho and area A, Newton’s law reads \rho A\,u_{tt} = V_x = -M_{xx}. Substituting step 1 gives the operator below.
  4. The clamp forbids moving and tilting: u = u_x = 0 at the base. Nothing grips the tip, so it carries no moment and no shear: u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 there.
TipThe tine’s operator

u_{tt} = -\kappa^2 u_{xxxx}, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\rho A}.

One slice of the tine. Moments (warm) bend it; shear forces (cool) push it up and down. Torque balance links them; force balance moves the slice.

Spectrum

A standing wave X(x)\cos\omega t needs X'''' = \beta^4 X with \beta^4 = \omega^2/\kappa^2. The solutions are combinations of \cos\beta x, \sin\beta x, \cosh\beta x and \sinh\beta x. The four boundary conditions are four linear equations for the four coefficients, and they have a nonzero solution exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1 .

The roots are \beta L = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548,\ 10.9955, \dots, computed live on this page. Since \omega = \kappa\beta^2, the frequency ratios are (\beta_n/\beta_1)^2 = 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39. Bending makes frequency grow like the square of the wavenumber, so the overtones fly apart.

The same formula gives f_1 \propto 1/L^2. Kalimba makers tune a tine by sliding it over the bridge to change its free length.

The tine sounds pitched anyway because its first overtone is weak, sits more than two octaves up, and dies within a few hundredths of a second. The ear hears the fundamental with a short metallic click on top.
