Why does a kalimba sound clean?

6 · The kalimba

Modified

October 29, 2026

← 5 Clarinet · The Shape of a Sound · Spectrum of an operator · Map · 6b Harmonica →

Builds on page 4, the violin string. Math: up to cosh, sinh and a 2×2 determinant (explained). About 3 minutes for the summary; about 30 with the mathematics.

A kalimba tine is a steel bar clamped at one end. Its first overtone sits at about 6.3 times its fundamental, off the whole numbers, and overtones off the whole numbers are what make a bell clang. So why does a kalimba sound like a clean note?

Listen first: tap a tine. Then tick the box that keeps the overtones ringing, and tap it again.

Tap a tine, or drag across several.

The tines are drawn to scale: a tine half as long sounds two octaves higher, as this page explains.

In one sentence. A bar clamped at one end has overtones off whole-number multiples (6.27×, 17.55×), yet a kalimba sounds clean because those overtones die within a fraction of a second (observed, not derived).

The recipe on this instrument, in brief.

  1. Newton’s law on a thin slice of bent steel gives the beam equation u_{tt} = -\kappa^2 u_{xxxx}, with four derivatives where a string had two.
  2. The operator is \kappa^2 times the fourth derivative, A = \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, on a tine clamped at one end and free at the other.
  3. Its spectrum: the end conditions allow a mode only when \cos\beta L\cosh\beta L = -1, which puts the frequencies in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39, off the whole numbers.
  4. On the diagram: the overtones sit off the whole-number lines.
  5. What we hear: a click of off-line overtones at the start, which die within a fraction of a second (observed on real kalimbas, not derived here), and then the fundamental alone: a clean note.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

1 · Newton’s law: the equation of motion

Mathematics used: partial derivatives, an integral over a cross-section, cosh and sinh, and a 2×2 determinant (all explained).

A string is held straight by tension. A tine is under no tension at all: it springs back because steel resists being bent. To find the force behind that, we apply Newton’s law, mass times acceleration equals force, to one thin slice of the tine.

Let u(x, t) be the tine’s height at distance x from the clamp at time t; subscripts are partial derivatives, so u_{xx} is the curvature, how sharply the tine bends. The tine is steel of Young’s modulus E (how stiff the steel is) and density \rho, with cross-section area S.

1. Bending stretches the outside and squeezes the inside. Bend the bar. Fibers on the outside of the curve stretch and fibers on the inside compress, while a middle line, the neutral axis, keeps its length.

For a gentle bend, a fiber at height y above the neutral axis is stretched by the fraction -y\,u_{xx}: its distance from the axis times how fast the bar turns per unit length. (The minus sign: where u_{xx} < 0 the bar bends down, and its top side, y > 0, is on the outside of the curve and stretched.) By Hooke’s law the fiber then carries the stress (force per area) -E\,y\,u_{xx}. Summed over the cross-section, these forces cancel, which is what places the neutral axis through the middle (the centroid) of the cross-section; but their turning effects do not cancel. Add them up over the small bits of area dA of the cross-section S, each pulling with stress times dA at lever arm y; the total is the bending moment \begin{aligned} M &= -\int_S y\,(-E\,y\,u_{xx})\,dA = E\,u_{xx}\int_S y^2\,dA \\ &= EI\,u_{xx} . \end{aligned} The number I = \int_S y^2\,dA, the second moment of area, says how the cross-section’s shape resists bending. For a rectangle of width w and thickness h, the strip at height y has area w\,dy, so I = \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} y^2\,w\,dy = wh^3/12; for a round wire of diameter d, I = \pi d^4/64.

2. A change in bending moment along the tine is a sideways force. A moment turns a slice but cannot lift it. Each face of a slice also carries a vertical force, the shear force V. The conventions: M is the moment on a face that bends the slice into an upward curve, counterclockwise on the right face and clockwise on the left; V is the upward force on the right face, and the left face feels the same force downward.

We neglect the slice’s resistance to being spun, its rotational inertia, which is accurate when the bar is thin compared with the length of its bending waves; then the turning effects on the slice must balance. Taking them about the left face, the shear on the right face times the lever arm dx cancels the change in moment: V\,dx + dM = 0, so V = -M_x = -EI\,u_{xxx} .

3. Newton’s law on the slice. The vertical forces on the two faces do not balance: their difference, V(x + dx) - V(x) = V_x\,dx, accelerates the slice’s mass \rho S\,dx. So \rho S\,u_{tt} = V_x = -EI\,u_{xxxx} . Dividing by the mass per length \rho S, this is the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation, u_{tt} = -\kappa^2 u_{xxxx} with \kappa^2 = EI/\rho S.

What we assumed. Small slopes; a uniform bar; no rotational inertia of the slices (Rayleigh’s correction for it adds a small term that matters only for thick bars and high modes); no shear deformation of the slices (Timoshenko’s correction); a tine clamped at exactly one point; and no damping or sound radiation: in this model every mode rings forever, and how fast the overtone dies is taken from measurement (stage 5).

2 · The operator

Read the beam equation as a rule: take the tine’s shape, differentiate it four times along the tine, and multiply by \kappa^2. That is minus the restoring force per unit mass, the tine’s operator A = \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, and the equation reads u_{tt} = -Au.

The ends. At the base the tine is held down, so it can neither move nor tilt: u = 0 and u_x = 0. A bar held this way at one end and free at the other is called a cantilever. At the free tip nothing pushes or bends the bar, so both the moment and the shear force vanish: u_{xx} = 0 and u_{xxx} = 0. That is two conditions at each end, four in all, as a fourth-order operator needs; they are part of the operator, as the tied ends were for the string.

The tine’s operator. \begin{gathered} u_{tt} = -A\,u, \qquad A = \kappa^2\,\frac{d^4}{dx^4}, \\ \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\rho S}, \end{gathered} with u = u_x = 0 at the clamp and u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 at the tip. In words: the force per unit mass pulling each point back is the fourth derivative of the shape, times a constant set by the steel and the cross-section. The string’s operator was -c^2\,d^2/dx^2: two derivatives instead of four.

What four derivatives do. Squeeze a shape into half the length, and every rate of change along it doubles. A string’s pull depends on its curvature, how sharply it bends, a rate of change of a rate of change: 2 × 2 = 4 times harder. A tine’s push depends on how that curvature itself changes along the tine, twice over: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 times harder. Both of the tine’s surprises in stage 5 come from this.

3 · Its spectrum

A mode is a shape X(x) that the operator only rescales, A X = \lambda X. Such a shape swings as X(x)\cos\omega t with \omega = \sqrt\lambda, at the frequency f = \sqrt\lambda/2\pi. The list of all the eigenvalues \lambda is the operator’s spectrum. For the tine, the mode equation is \kappa^2 X'''' = \lambda X .

1. Which shapes does the operator only rescale? Write \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, so that X'''' = \beta^4 X: four derivatives give back the shape, times \beta^4. The number \beta plays the role of the string’s wavenumber. Four functions do this: \cos\beta x, \sin\beta x, \cosh\beta x and \sinh\beta x, where \cosh y = (e^y + e^{-y})/2 and \sinh y = (e^y - e^{-y})/2 are the hyperbolic functions. Differentiating the definitions, (\cosh y)' = \sinh y and (\sinh y)' = \cosh y, with no sign changes, so the hyperbolic pair gives back \beta^4 times itself after four derivatives, just as \cos and \sin do after their sign flips cancel; multiplying out the definitions also gives \cosh^2 y - \sinh^2 y = 1, and \cosh 0 = 1, \sinh 0 = 0. So X is a combination of the four, with four unknown constants, and the four end conditions are four equations for them.

2. Hold the clamped end still. X(0) = 0 and X'(0) = 0 leave two constants: \begin{aligned} X = {}&a\,(\cos\beta x - \cosh\beta x) \\ &+ b\,(\sin\beta x - \sinh\beta x) . \end{aligned}

3. Leave the free tip unbent and unpushed. Differentiating, and dropping the common factors \beta^2 and \beta^3, the conditions X''(L) = 0 and X'''(L) = 0 read, with the shorthand c_1 = \cos\beta L, s_1 = \sin\beta L, c_2 = \cosh\beta L, s_2 = \sinh\beta L: \begin{gathered} -(c_1 + c_2)\,a - (s_1 + s_2)\,b = 0, \\ (s_1 - s_2)\,a - (c_1 + c_2)\,b = 0 . \end{gathered}

4. When is there a mode? Two equations pa + qb = 0 and ra + sb = 0 have a solution other than a = b = 0 exactly when ps - qr = 0, the determinant (solve the first for a and substitute into the second). Here \begin{aligned} &(c_1 + c_2)^2 + (s_1 + s_2)(s_1 - s_2) \\ &\quad = c_1^2 + s_1^2 + 2c_1c_2 + c_2^2 - s_2^2 \\ &\quad = 2 + 2c_1c_2 = 0, \end{aligned} using c_1^2 + s_1^2 = 1 and c_2^2 - s_2^2 = 1. So the tine has a mode exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1 .

5. Find the roots. Since \cosh\beta L grows fast, \cos\beta L must be close to 0, so \beta L sits near (n - \tfrac12)\pi, closer and closer as n grows. Solved numerically, the roots are \beta L = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548,\ 10.9955, \dots; the first is the furthest from its estimate, \pi/2 \approx 1.571, because the clamp and the free tip shift it most.

The tine’s spectrum. The modes are X_n = \cosh\beta_n x - \cos\beta_n x - \sigma_n(\sinh\beta_n x - \sin\beta_n x), which is step 2 with a = -1 and b = \sigma_n = (\sinh\beta_nL - \sin\beta_nL)/(\cosh\beta_nL + \cos\beta_nL), from the second equation of step 3 (the first gives (c_1 + c_2)/(s_1 + s_2), the same number at every root, by the mode condition). Their eigenvalues are \lambda_n = \kappa^2\beta_n^4, and their frequencies are f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{\kappa\,\beta_n^2}{2\pi} = \frac{\kappa\,(\beta_nL)^2}{2\pi L^2} . Since f_n \propto (\beta_nL)^2, the frequency ratios are 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39 : \cdots, growing like {(2n-1)^2}: off the whole numbers.

Compare pinned ends. A bar held in place at both ends but free to pivot has u = u_{xx} = 0 at each end. Then the shapes \sin(n\pi x/L) satisfy all four conditions, four derivatives multiply them by (n\pi/L)^4, and the ratios are 1 : 4 : 9 : 16: whole numbers. If every root of the clamped–free tine sat exactly at (n - \tfrac12)\pi, its ratios would be the whole numbers 1 : 9 : 25 too; the shift of the first root is what puts them off the lines.

ProofWhy the eigenvalues are real and positive, and the modes perpendicular uses integration by parts

The operator d^4/dx^4 with these four conditions is symmetric, by integrating by parts four times (the boundary terms all vanish), so its eigenvalues are real and its eigenfunctions perpendicular, as on the circle; with the right domain it is also self-adjoint. Integrating by parts twice gives \int_0^L X\,X''''\,dx = \int_0^L (X'')^2\,dx, so no eigenvalue is negative, and zero is ruled out because X'' = 0 with a clamped base forces X = 0. That is why \beta is a real, positive number.

5 · What we hear

Why the note still sounds clean. You might guess the overtone is too quiet to hear. It isn’t. In how far the tine swings, the overtone is tiny; but the body hears the push on the bridge, not the swing, and in that push the overtone starts strong (fold below). That strong start is the click at the beginning of each note. What keeps the note clean is that the overtone dies within a fraction of a second, while the fundamental rings on, and the fundamental left on its own is a clean note. That fast decay is observed, not derived: the model of stages 1 to 3 has no damping, and the page takes the decay from measurement. The acoustician D. M. F. Chapman found exactly this pattern on a real kalimba: one dominant overtone, at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, which dies away so fast that it is heard only at the instant the note starts. With the box ticked, the off-line overtones keep ringing, and the same tine clangs like a bell.

Step-by-step derivationHow strongly a pluck at the tip excites each mode uses integration by parts

Chapman measured the tines of one kalimba, a Hugh Tracey pentatonic, and found one dominant overtone at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, near the ideal 6.27, dying away quickly after the attack. A real tine is clamped a little behind the bridge and rests on the bridge partway along, rather than being perfectly clamped at one point, and he found that the measured ratios depend on how the bridge divides the tine’s length, which is one reason they spread. The steps below compute, for the ideal tine, how much of each mode a pluck starts. In short: in displacement, the second mode starts at only 2.5% of the first; but at the clamp, where in this ideal model the tine presses on the bridge, it starts at about 16% of the first in bending moment and 55% in shear force. Those are two different measures of the push on the bridge, not a range of uncertainty.

  1. The pluck shape. Before release, a push F at the tip holds the tine still in a bent shape u_0, so the bar’s restoring force balances the push.
  2. Split it into modes. Write u_0 = \sum_n a_n X_n, with each mode scaled so that \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L. Inside the tine nothing pushes, so EI\,u_0'''' = 0 there; the push enters only at the tip, as the shear force there. Now compute \int_0^L EI\,u_0\,X_n''''\,dx in two ways. First, EI\,X_n'''' = \rho S\,\omega_n^2X_n (the mode equation), and the modes are perpendicular, so only the a_n term survives: the integral is \rho S L\,\omega_n^2\,a_n. Second, integrate by parts four times to move the derivatives onto u_0, as in the proof fold above. Inside, EI\,u_0'''' = 0; at the clamp both u_0 and X_n vanish with their slopes; at the tip X_n'' = X_n''' = 0 and u_0'' = 0. The one term left is the tip’s shear force times X_n(L), which is F\,X_n(L). So \rho S L\,\omega_n^2\,a_n = F\,X_n(L). This is page 1’s spring again: mode n is a spring of stiffness proportional to \omega_n^2, pushed with a force proportional to its value at the tip, and it gives way by force over stiffness.
  3. Tip values. Use the modes in the standard form of the spectrum box above, with \sigma_n about 0.734 and 1.018 for the first two modes. Written this way, each mode already has \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L, and its tip value is \pm2 for every n (a known property of clamped–free modes, easily confirmed numerically). So a_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2, and a_2/a_1 = 1/6.27^2 \approx 2.5\%.
  4. Forces. In this model the tine is clamped right at the bridge, so the forces at the clamp are the forces on the bridge. The bending moment at the clamp is EI\,u_{xx} there, and the shear force EI\,u_{xxx} there (up to sign). From the standard form, X_n''(0) = 2\beta_n^2 and X_n'''(0) = -2\sigma_n\beta_n^3. Multiplying by a_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2 \propto 1/\beta_n^4, the second mode’s moment at the clamp is (\beta_1/\beta_2)^2 = (1.875/4.694)^2 \approx 16\% of the first’s, and its shear force (\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(\beta_1/\beta_2) \approx 1.39 \times 0.40 \approx 55\%.

A plucked tine moves as the sum of its modes, u = \sum_n a_n X_n(x)\cos(2\pi f_n t + \phi_n), each mode fading at its own rate. The demo’s sound is made exactly this way: it adds up the first five modes at the frequencies of stage 3, each starting with its shear-force strength from the fold above (55% for the second mode), as in stage 4. The fundamental fades over a couple of seconds and the overtones within a small fraction of that; those fading rates are an illustrative choice consistent with Chapman’s observation, not computed. Tick the box and every mode fades as slowly as the fundamental.

Overtones off the lines. Higher modes bend in shorter humps, and for a tine shorter humps push back much harder, so the frequencies grow like the square of the mode number instead of the number itself. Where exactly they land is set by the four end conditions: pinned ends would give the whole numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, but a tine is clamped at one end and free at the other, and its spectrum is 1, 6.27, 17.55, …, off the lines.

Half the length, two octaves up. The result box says f_n = \kappa(\beta_nL)^2/(2\pi L^2), and the numbers \beta_nL do not depend on the length: so a tine’s frequency goes as one over its length squared. Half the length gives 4 times the frequency, two octaves, where a string, with f \propto 1/L, rises only an octave. That is why the low tines in the demo are not twice as long as the high ones an octave up. Kalimba makers tune a tine by sliding it over the bridge to change its free length (Kalimba Magic), and the 1/L^2 makes a small slide go a long way.

What we found

A tine is a bar clamped at one end, with the operator A = \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, and its spectrum, from \cos\beta L\cosh\beta L = -1, puts the overtones at 6.27 and 17.55 times the lowest, off the whole-number multiples: four derivatives spread the frequencies like the squares, and the clamped and free ends keep them off the whole numbers. A kalimba still sounds clean because those overtones die within a fraction of a second, which is observed on real kalimbas, not derived here. The next page blows the same kind of bar instead of plucking it.

The tine’s operator family. Harmonica reeds and the teeth of a music box’s steel comb are also bars clamped at one end and free at the other: the same operator with the same ends, so the series puts them in the kalimba’s operator family.
