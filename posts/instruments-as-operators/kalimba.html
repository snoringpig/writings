The puzzle. Halve the length of a string and its pitch rises an octave. Halve the length of a kalimba tine and its pitch rises two octaves. And a tine’s first overtone is at 6.27 times its fundamental frequency, nowhere near a whole number, yet a kalimba sounds like a clean note. Why?

They are drawn to scale: each tine’s length follows from its pitch by the rule this page derives, and the long low tines are not twice as long as the high ones an octave up. Then tick the box that lets the overtones ring as long as the fundamental, and listen to how the sound changes.

Tine lengths are drawn from the operator: frequency goes as 1/L², so each tine's length is the C4 (middle C) tine's divided by the square root of its frequency ratio. Tick the box and pluck again: with the off-grid overtones ringing as long as the fundamental, the same frequencies sound clangorous, like a bell.

Same operator, different constants. Harmonica reeds and the steel comb of a music box are also bars held at one end and free at the other. The harmonica, next, has the same operator and the same ends, but it is blown instead of plucked, and that decides what you hear.

A bar resists bending

The operator. A bar of Young’s modulus E (how stiff the steel is), cross-section area S, second moment of area I (how the cross-section’s shape resists bending) and density \rho obeys u_{tt} = -\kappa^2\,u_{xxxx}, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\rho S}. This is the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation. The operator is \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, with four derivatives. Two of them measure how sharply the bar is bent, its curvature u_{xx}; the bar resists bending with a turning effect, the bending moment, proportional to it. Two more turn that into a net force: one derivative turns the change of bending moment along the bar into the sideways force across each cut, the shear force, and another turns the change of shear force along the bar into the net force on each piece.

One slice of the tine. Bending moments (warm), the turning effects that bend it; shear forces (cool), the sideways forces across each cut, push it up and down. Torque balance links them; force balance moves the slice.

Step-by-step derivationFrom Newton’s law on a thin slice to four derivatives uses integrals over an area

1. Bending stresses. Bend the bar. Fibers on the outside of the curve stretch and fibers on the inside compress, while a middle line, the neutral axis, keeps its length.

A fiber at height y above the neutral axis is stretched by the fraction -y\,u_{xx} (for a gentle bend, the fraction by which a fiber stretches is its distance from the neutral axis times how fast the bar turns per unit length, which is the curvature u_{xx}), and by Hooke’s law it carries the stress (force per area) \sigma = -E\,y\,u_{xx}. Summed over the cross-section these forces cancel, which is what places the neutral axis through the middle (the centroid) of the cross-section, but their torques do not. They add up to the bending moment M = -\int_S y\,\sigma\,dA = E\,u_{xx}\int_S y^2\,dA = EI\,u_{xx}, \qquad I = \int_S y^2\,dA , the integrals running over the cross-section. For a rectangle of width w and thickness h, I = wh^3/12; for a round wire of diameter d, I = \pi d^4/64.

2. Shear. A moment rotates a slice but cannot lift it. Each face of a slice also carries a vertical force, the shear force. The conventions: M is the moment on a face that bends the slice into an upward curve, counterclockwise on the right face and clockwise on the left; V is the upward force on the right face, and the left face feels the same force downward.

We neglect the slice’s resistance to being spun, its rotational inertia, which is accurate when the bar is thin compared with the length of its bending waves; then the torques on the slice must balance. Taking torques about the left face, the shear on the right face times the lever arm dx cancels the change in moment: V\,dx + dM = 0, so V = -M_x.

3. Newton’s law. The vertical forces on the slice do not balance: V(x + dx) - V(x) = V_x\,dx accelerates its mass \rho S\,dx. So \rho S\,u_{tt} = V_x = -M_{xx} = -EI\,u_{xxxx}, the equation above.

What was assumed. Small slopes, a uniform bar, no rotational inertia of the slices (Rayleigh’s correction for it adds a small term that matters only for thick bars and high modes), and no shear deformation of the slices (Timoshenko’s correction).

Boundary conditions. The bending moment is EI\,u_{xx} and the shear force is -EI\,u_{xxx} (the fold derives both). At the base the tine is held down, so it can neither move nor tilt: u = 0 and u_x = 0. A bar held this way at one end and free at the other is called a cantilever. At the free tip nothing pushes or bends the bar, so both the moment and the shear force vanish: u_{xx} = 0 and u_{xxx} = 0. That is two conditions at each end, four in all.

Spectrum. Substituting a mode X(x)\cos\omega t into the equation gives \kappa^2 X'''' = \omega^2 X. Write \omega/\kappa = \beta^2, so that X'''' = \beta^4 X; the number \beta plays the role of the string’s wavenumber. Four functions solve this equation: \cos\beta x, \sin\beta x, \cosh\beta x and \sinh\beta x, where \cosh y = (e^y + e^{-y})/2 and \sinh y = (e^y - e^{-y})/2 are the hyperbolic functions. So X is a combination of the four with four unknown constants. The four boundary conditions are four equations for those constants, and they have a solution other than all zeros exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1 . The roots are \beta L = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548,\ 10.9955, \dots, close to (n - \tfrac12)\pi from the second one on. Since \omega \propto \beta^2, the frequency ratios are 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39 : \cdots, \qquad \text{growing like } (2n-1)^2 . For a string, frequency is proportional to the wavenumber; for a bar, to its square. That change of order spreads the overtones apart, roughly like n^2 instead of n. It does not by itself move them off the whole numbers: with pinned ends, held in place but free to pivot, so u = u_{xx} = 0 at both ends, the shapes \sin(n\pi x/L) satisfy the conditions, four derivatives multiply them by (n\pi/L)^4, and the frequencies are in the ratios 1 : 4 : 9 : 16, all whole numbers. What moves them off is the clamped base and the free tip, which shift the low roots: the first is 1.8751 instead of \pi/2 \approx 1.571, and if every root were exactly (n - \tfrac12)\pi the ratios would be the whole numbers 1 : 9 : 25.

Step-by-step derivationThe four boundary conditions give cos βL cosh βL = −1 uses a 2×2 determinant and integration by parts

The solutions of X'''' = \beta^4X are combinations of \cos\beta x, \sin\beta x, \cosh\beta x and \sinh\beta x, where \cosh and \sinh are the hyperbolic functions, whose fourth derivatives also return them times \beta^4.

  1. Clamped base. X(0) = 0 and X'(0) = 0 leave X = a\,(\cos\beta x - \cosh\beta x) + b\,(\sin\beta x - \sinh\beta x).
  2. Free tip. Differentiating, and dropping the common factors \beta^2 and \beta^3, the conditions X''(L) = 0 and X'''(L) = 0 read, with the shorthand C_1 = \cos\beta L, S_1 = \sin\beta L, C_2 = \cosh\beta L, S_2 = \sinh\beta L: -(C_1 + C_2)\,a - (S_1 + S_2)\,b = 0, \qquad (S_1 - S_2)\,a - (C_1 + C_2)\,b = 0 .
  3. Nonzero solution. Two equations pa + qb = 0 and ra + sb = 0 have a solution other than a = b = 0 exactly when ps - qr = 0, the determinant: solve the first for a and substitute into the second. Here (C_1 + C_2)^2 + (S_1 + S_2)(S_1 - S_2) = C_1^2 + S_1^2 + 2C_1C_2 + C_2^2 - S_2^2 = 2 + 2C_1C_2 = 0, using C_1^2 + S_1^2 = 1 and C_2^2 - S_2^2 = 1. So \cos\beta L\cosh\beta L = -1.
  4. Roots. Since \cosh\beta L grows fast, \cos\beta L must be close to 0, so \beta L \approx (n - \tfrac12)\pi, and the approximation improves quickly with n. The first root, 1.8751, is the furthest from it.

The operator d^4/dx^4 with these four conditions is symmetric, by integrating by parts four times (the boundary terms all vanish), so its eigenvalues are real and its eigenfunctions perpendicular, as on the circle; with the right domain it is also self-adjoint. Integrating by parts twice gives \int_0^L X\,X''''\,dx = \int_0^L (X'')^2\,dx, so no eigenvalue is negative, and zero is ruled out because X'' = 0 with a clamped base forces X = 0. That is why \beta is a real, positive number.

Tuning. Since \beta_1 = 1.8751/L and \omega = \kappa\beta^2, the lowest frequency is f_1 \propto \kappa/L^2 (\propto means “proportional to”), so halving a tine’s free length raises its pitch by a factor of 4, two octaves. Kalimba makers tune a tine by sliding it over the bridge to change its free length.

Why it still sounds pitched. Plucked at the tip, a tine starts in the bent shape that a steady push at the tip produces. In that shape the second mode moves only about 1/6.27^2 \approx 2.5\% as far as the first (fold below). But what you hear is driven by the force the tine puts on the bridge, as on the string page, and for the same displacement a higher mode pushes harder. At the clamp, which in this model is where the tine presses on the bridge, the second mode’s bending moment is about 16% of the first’s and its shear force about 55%. The bridge feels both, so either way the overtone is far stronger in the sound than 2.5% suggests: it is the brief metallic clink of the attack. What keeps the note clean is that the overtone dies within a fraction of a second, while the fundamental rings on. What remains is close to a single frequency, and a sound made of essentially one frequency repeats, so it has a clear pitch.

Step-by-step derivationHow strongly a pluck at the tip excites each mode uses integration by parts
  1. The pluck shape. Before release, a push F at the tip holds the tine still in a bent shape u_0, so the bar’s restoring force balances the push.
  2. Split it into modes. Write u_0 = \sum_n c_n X_n, with each mode scaled so that \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L. Inside the tine nothing pushes, so EI\,u_0'''' = 0 there; the push enters only at the tip, as the shear force there. Now compute \int_0^L EI\,u_0\,X_n''''\,dx in two ways. First, EI\,X_n'''' = \rho S\,\omega_n^2X_n (the mode equation), and the modes are perpendicular, so only the c_n term survives: the integral is \rho S L\,\omega_n^2\,c_n. Second, integrate by parts four times to move the derivatives onto u_0, as in the fold above. Inside, EI\,u_0'''' = 0; at the clamp both u_0 and X_n vanish with their slopes; at the tip X_n'' = X_n''' = 0 and u_0'' = 0. The one term left is the tip’s shear force times X_n(L), which is F\,X_n(L). So \rho S L\,\omega_n^2\,c_n = F\,X_n(L). This is page 1’s spring again: mode n is a spring of stiffness proportional to \omega_n^2, pushed with a force proportional to its value at the tip, and it gives way by force over stiffness.
  3. Tip values. Taking a = -1 and b = \sigma_n in the fold above gives the standard form X_n = \cosh\beta_nx - \cos\beta_nx - \sigma_n(\sinh\beta_nx - \sin\beta_nx), with \sigma_n = (\sinh\beta_nL - \sin\beta_nL)/(\cosh\beta_nL + \cos\beta_nL), about 0.734 and 1.018 for the first two modes. Written this way, each mode already has \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L, and its tip value is \pm2 for every n (a known property of clamped–free modes, easily confirmed numerically). So c_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2, and c_2/c_1 = 1/6.27^2 \approx 2.5\%.
  4. Forces. The bending moment at the clamp is EI\,u_{xx} there, and the shear force EI\,u_{xxx} there (up to sign). From the standard form, X_n''(0) = 2\beta_n^2 and X_n'''(0) = -2\sigma_n\beta_n^3. Multiplying by c_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2 \propto 1/\beta_n^4, the second mode’s moment at the clamp is (\beta_1/\beta_2)^2 = (1.875/4.694)^2 \approx 16\% of the first’s, and its shear force (\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(\beta_1/\beta_2) \approx 1.39 \times 0.40 \approx 55\%.

Chapman measured the tines of one kalimba, a Hugh Tracey pentatonic, and found one dominant overtone at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, near the ideal 6.27, dying away quickly after the attack. A real tine is not perfectly clamped: Chapman models it as clamped at one end, resting on the bridge partway along, and free at the tip, and finds that the measured overtone ratios depend on how the tine’s length is divided by the bridge, which is one reason they spread.

What we found

A bar under no tension springs back because it resists bending. Its operator has four derivatives instead of two, so the frequencies spread apart, growing roughly like the square of the mode number. The clamped base and free tip then move them off the whole numbers: 1, 6.27, 17.55, … times the lowest. With pinned ends the same operator would give 1, 4, 9, 16.

The next puzzle

A harmonica reed is the same kind of bar, fixed at one end and free at the other, with the same overtones at 6.27 and 17.55 times its fundamental. Pluck one and you hear them. Yet a harmonica, blown, plays notes whose overtones are exact whole-number multiples. Where did the bar’s own overtones go?
