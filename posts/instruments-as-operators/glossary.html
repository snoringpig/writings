Technical words in the series link here, usually the first time they appear on a page. Each entry gives a plain definition and a link to a fuller explanation.

Sound

Hertz (Hz)
One cycle per second, the unit of frequency. The A above middle C is tuned to 440 Hz. Wikipedia
Periodic
A signal p(t) is periodic with period P > 0 if it repeats exactly every P seconds: p(t + P) = p(t) for all t. Its shortest such P is usually what is meant by the period. Wikipedia
Pitch
How high or low a note sounds. For a periodic sound with shortest period P, the pitch is heard at the repetition rate 1/P, even when no component at that frequency is present, as long as that rate is between a few dozen and a few thousand per second. Wikipedia, and page 0
Partial, overtone
A sound can be split into components of single frequencies; each one is a partial. An overtone is any partial above the lowest. Wikipedia
Fundamental
For a periodic sound, the frequency f = 1/P at which it repeats; its harmonics are the whole-number multiples of f. It is usually the lowest and strongest partial, but it can be weak or even missing, and the pitch is still heard at f (page 0). For a sound that is not periodic, such as a drum’s, “fundamental” just means the lowest partial. Wikipedia
Harmonic
A partial at a whole-number multiple nf of the fundamental. A periodic sound contains only harmonics. Wikipedia
Harmonic series
The whole-number multiples f, 2f, 3f, \dots of one frequency f. All the partials of a periodic sound belong to one harmonic series. Wikipedia
Inharmonic
Having partials that are not whole-number multiples of one frequency. A bell, a drum and a kalimba tine are strongly inharmonic; a piano string is slightly inharmonic. Wikipedia
Timbre
What tells two notes of the same pitch and loudness apart, such as a violin and a flute both playing A. It depends mostly on the relative strengths of the partials and how they change over the course of the note. Wikipedia
Note names: C4, A4, middle C
Notes are named by a letter from A to G and an octave number. C4 is middle C, about 262 Hz, near the middle of the piano keyboard; C5 is the C an octave above it; A4 is the A at 440 Hz. Wikipedia
Semitone
The smallest step on a piano, from one key to the next, white or black. Twelve semitones make an octave. Wikipedia
Equal temperament
The usual way of tuning a piano: every semitone is the same frequency ratio, 2^{1/12} \approx 1.0595, so twelve of them make an exact octave. The price is that the other intervals miss the simple ratios slightly: an equal-tempered fifth is 2^{7/12} \approx 1.4983, not 3/2. Wikipedia
Cent
A unit for small differences in pitch: one hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone, so an octave is 1200 cents. Two frequencies in the ratio r are 1200\log_2 r cents apart. Trained listeners notice a few cents. Wikipedia
Octave, fifth, twelfth
Musical intervals, named by frequency ratio. An octave is a ratio of 2. A fifth is the ratio 3/2 when tuned to the simple ratio, and 2^{7/12} \approx 1.498 in equal temperament. A twelfth, an octave plus a fifth, is about 3. Two notes an octave apart sound like “the same note, higher”. Wikipedia
Beats
When two pure tones of nearly equal frequency sound together, their sum swells and fades in loudness at a rate equal to the difference of their frequencies. Tuners listen for beats; fast beats are heard as roughness. Wikipedia
Consonance, roughness
Consonance is how smooth or stable two notes sound together. Roughness is one ingredient of it: the harsh quality of fast beats between partials that are close but not equal. Wikipedia, and the epilogue

Instrument parts and playing

Nut, bridge
On a string instrument, the two points that end a string’s vibrating length: the nut at the top of the neck, the bridge on the body. The bridge also carries the string’s vibration to the body, or on a piano to the soundboard. On a kalimba, the bridge is the bar the tines are pressed against. Wikipedia
Open string
A string played with no finger pressing it down, so its whole length between the nut and the bridge vibrates. Wikipedia
Harmonics, on a string
Notes a string player gets by touching the string lightly at a fraction of its length, such as the middle, instead of pressing it down. Only the modes with a still point under the finger keep sounding. Wikipedia, and page 4
Overblowing, register key
Making a wind instrument’s air column jump to a higher mode. A flutist overblows by blowing faster; a clarinetist presses the register key, which opens a small hole that spoils the lowest mode. Wikipedia, and page 5
Blow, draw
On a harmonica, a blow note sounds when you breathe out through a hole, and a draw note when you breathe in. Each hole has one reed for each (on a chromatic harmonica, one for each in each of its two sets). Wikipedia
Free reed
A thin metal tongue fixed at one end that swings through a closely fitting slot, opening and shutting it like a valve. Harmonicas and accordions use free reeds. Wikipedia
Damper
A felt pad that rests on a piano string and stops it when the key is released. Each key lifts its own damper while held; the right pedal lifts them all. The highest strings, roughly the top 20 keys, have no dampers. Wikipedia: Sustain pedal
Soft pedal, una corda
The left pedal of a piano. On a grand it shifts the hammers sideways so they hit fewer strings; this is the una corda, Italian for “one string”. On an upright it instead moves the hammers closer to the strings, so they strike more gently. Wikipedia
Practice pedal, mute
The middle pedal on many uprights. It lowers a strip of felt between the hammers and the strings, making the sound much quieter and duller. Wikipedia: Piano pedals

Vibration

Partial derivative, u_t, u_{xx}
For a function of several variables, such as the height u(x, t) of a string at position x and time t, the rate of change in one variable with the others held fixed. Subscripts name the variable: u_t is the velocity of the point at x, u_{tt} its acceleration, u_x the slope of the string, and u_{xx} how fast that slope changes along it, which for small slopes is its curvature. Wikipedia
Proportional, \propto
a \propto b means that a is a fixed number times b. So f \propto 1/L^2 means that halving L multiplies f by four. Wikipedia
Angular frequency
\omega = 2\pi f: the frequency counted in radians per second instead of cycles per second, one cycle being 2\pi radians. Formulas such as \omega^2 = k/m are shortest in \omega. Wikipedia
Wavenumber
The counterpart of angular frequency in space: a wave \sin kx has wavenumber k = 2\pi/\text{wavelength}. For the wave equation, \omega = ck. Wikipedia
Linear
An equation with no outside force is linear if sums and multiples of solutions are again solutions. An equation with an outside force is linear when it reads (left side) = (the force), where the force does not involve u, and the left side is built from u and its derivatives only by multiplying each by a number, or by a given function of position and time, and adding: no u^2, no u\,u_x, no \sin u. The piano page’s \mu u_{tt} + b\,u_t - Tu_{xx} + EI\,u_{xxxx} = f(x, t) is an example. Then the difference of any two solutions solves the same equation without the force. The operators in this series are linear, a good model for the small vibrations of instruments. What keeps a bowed string, a clarinet reed or a harmonica reed going is not linear: the bow’s grip and slip and a reed opening and closing. Wikipedia
Superposition
Adding solutions of a linear equation to get another solution. The free motion of an instrument, once nothing is driving it, is a superposition, a sum, of its modes. Wikipedia
Mode, normal mode
A motion of an object, with no friction and nothing driving it, in which every part swings at the same single frequency and the overall shape stays the same, only growing and shrinking. Every small free vibration is a sum of modes. Wikipedia
Standing wave
A wave whose shape stays in place, such as a mode of a string. It can be seen as two equal waves traveling in opposite directions. Wikipedia
Node, antinode, nodal line
A node is a point of a standing wave that does not move; an antinode is a point that moves the most. Touching a string lightly at a node of a mode does not disturb that mode. On a drumhead or a plate, a mode stays still along whole curves, its nodal lines; Chladni’s sand collects on them. Wikipedia
Wave equation
The equation u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx} for the height u(x, t) of a stretched string, and its relatives for air in a pipe and for a drumhead. The constant c is the speed at which a bump travels. Wikipedia, and page 3
Boundary condition
A rule for what happens at the edge of a vibrating object, set by how it is held. A string tied down at an end has u = 0 there. A bar clamped at an end can neither move nor tilt there: u = 0 and u_x = 0. At the open end of a pipe the pressure equals room pressure. Wikipedia, and page 4
Dirichlet and Neumann conditions
Two kinds of boundary condition. A Dirichlet condition fixes the value at the edge, such as u = 0 at a string’s tied end or p = 0 at a pipe’s open end. A Neumann condition fixes the slope, such as p_x = 0 at a clarinet’s reed end. Wikipedia: Dirichlet, Neumann
Damping
Friction that drains energy from a vibration, so that it dies away. A term b\,u_t in the equation, proportional to velocity, is the simplest model. Wikipedia
Self-sustained oscillation, limit cycle
An oscillation kept going at a steady size by a steady supply of energy, such as a bow drawn at constant speed or a steady breath. In normal playing the motion settles into one repeating cycle, called a limit cycle; under some conditions it does not, as in the unsteady, warbling wolf note of some cellos. This needs a mechanism that is not linear, such as the bow’s grip and slip or a reed opening and closing. Wikipedia
Coupling
Two vibrating parts are coupled when the motion of one pushes on the other, as when piano strings share a bridge. Coupled parts trade energy, and their modes mix. Wikipedia
Helmholtz motion
The way a bowed string moves. The string sticks to the bow, slips back, and sticks again; seen from the side, a single sharp corner races back and forth along the string, one round trip per period. J. Wolfe, UNSW: The bowed string
Cantilever, clamped bar
A bar fixed rigidly at one end, so that end can neither move nor tilt, and free at the other, like a diving board. A kalimba tine is close to a cantilever: it is pressed against a bridge near one end and its tip is free. Wikipedia
Euler–Bernoulli beam
The standard model of a bending bar under no tension: \mu u_{tt} = -EI\,u_{xxxx}, where \mu is the mass per unit length, E the Young’s modulus and I the second moment of area. The bar resists being bent, and bending is curvature, two derivatives; balancing forces along the bar adds two more, giving four. Wikipedia
Bending moment, shear force
Imagine cutting a bent bar at some point. The bending moment is the turning effect that one side exerts on the other, which tries to bend the bar; the shear force is the sideways force across the cut. A free end carries neither. Wikipedia: Bending moment, Shear force
Young’s modulus
How stiff a material is when stretched: the force per unit area needed, divided by the fraction by which it lengthens. Steel is about 200 GPa; nylon, used for classical guitar strings, is a few GPa. Wikipedia
Second moment of area
How the shape of a bar’s cross-section resists bending, written I. For a rectangle of width w and thickness h, I = wh^3/12: doubling the thickness makes the bar eight times harder to bend. Wikipedia
Hyperbolic functions, cosh and sinh
\cosh x = (e^x + e^{-x})/2 and \sinh x = (e^x - e^{-x})/2. They satisfy \cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1, much as \cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1. In a bar they describe the part of the bending that grows or fades along the bar instead of wiggling. Wikipedia
Bulk modulus, adiabatic
How hard a material is to compress: the pressure needed, divided by the fraction by which the volume shrinks. In a sound wave the squeezes are too quick for heat to flow in or out, which is called adiabatic, and for air this gives about 1.4 \times atmospheric pressure. Wikipedia
First-order approximation
Keeping only the terms proportional to a small change and dropping those proportional to its square and higher powers. For a small \varepsilon, (1 + \varepsilon)^3 = 1 + 3\varepsilon + 3\varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon^3 \approx 1 + 3\varepsilon to first order. A second-order approximation keeps the \varepsilon^2 terms too. Wikipedia

Matrices and operators

Matrix times vector
For a square matrix K and a list of numbers v, the product Kv is the list whose j-th entry is \sum_k K_{jk}v_k: row j of K, multiplied entry by entry with v and added up. Wikipedia
Eigenvector, eigenvalue
A nonzero vector v that a matrix K only rescales, multiplying it by a number \lambda: Kv = \lambda v. The number \lambda is the eigenvalue. On the bead ring of page 2, v lists how far each bead is displaced and Kv the forces pulling them back, up to a constant; an eigenvector is a shape in which each bead is pulled back in proportion to its own displacement, so all the beads swing at one frequency. Eigenvalues are squared angular frequencies, up to a constant. Wikipedia
Eigenvalue
See eigenvector.
Multiplicity
The number of independent eigenvectors that share one eigenvalue. Vectors are independent when none of them is a combination of the others. On a ring, the cosine and sine shapes of the same wave are two independent modes with the same frequency, so that frequency has multiplicity 2. Wikipedia
Perpendicular, orthogonal
Two vectors are perpendicular, or orthogonal, when their dot product, the sum of the products of matching entries, is zero. For functions the dot product is replaced by the inner product. Wikipedia
Symmetric matrix
A square matrix of real numbers equal to its own mirror image across the diagonal: the entry in row i, column j equals the entry in row j, column i. The spectral theorem says its eigenvalues are real and it has a full set of perpendicular eigenvectors. Wikipedia
Determinant
A number computed from a square matrix that is zero exactly when the matrix sends some nonzero vector to zero. So linear equations with all right-hand sides zero have a solution other than all zeros exactly when the determinant of their coefficients is zero. For a 2\times2 matrix with rows (p, q) and (r, s) it is ps - qr. Wikipedia
Circulant matrix
A square matrix in which each row is the row above shifted one place to the right, wrapping around. Whatever its entries, the complex waves e^{2\pi ijk/N} of the discrete Fourier transform, the next entry, are eigenvectors of it; when the matrix is also symmetric, as on page 2, so are the real cosine and sine waves. Wikipedia
Discrete Fourier transform (DFT)
Writing a list of N numbers, indexed by j = 0, \dots, N-1, as a sum of the waves \cos(2\pi jk/N) and \sin(2\pi jk/N) for k = 0, \dots, N-1; with Euler’s formula these are combined into e^{2\pi ijk/N}. Wikipedia, and page 2
Euler’s formula
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta, where i^2 = -1. It packs a cosine and a sine into one expression that is easier to multiply and differentiate. Wikipedia
Operator
A rule that takes a function and returns another function. In this series the operator A takes a shape u of the instrument and returns minus the restoring force on it, per unit mass, so that the motion obeys u_{tt} = -Au. It comes with its allowed shapes, those that obey the boundary conditions. All of these operators are linear. Example: A = -c^2\,d^2/d\theta^2 on shapes of a loop. Pages 3 and 4 drop the constant c^2 and work with -d^2/d\theta^2, whose eigenvalues \lambda give frequencies c\sqrt\lambda/2\pi. Wikipedia
Eigenfunction
A nonzero function f that an operator only rescales: Af = \lambda f. Example: -\frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}\cos 3\theta = 9\cos 3\theta. Wikipedia
Spectrum
For every instrument in this series, the list of its operator’s eigenvalues; the frequencies are their square roots divided by 2\pi (when the constant c^2 is dropped from the operator, multiply by c too). (In general, for an operator on an infinite-dimensional space, the spectrum is the set of numbers \lambda for which A - \lambda has no well-behaved inverse, which can contain more than eigenvalues.) Wikipedia
Inner product
A way to multiply two vectors or two functions to get a number, generalizing the dot product. For functions on the circle, \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f\,\bar g\,d\theta, where \bar g is the complex conjugate of g, which is just g when g is real. Two functions are perpendicular if it is zero. Wikipedia
L^2(S^1)
The space of functions on the circle S^1 with \int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta finite, with the inner product above. (For readers who know measure theory: two functions that differ only on a set of measure zero count as the same.) It is where the shapes of a vibrating loop live. Wikipedia
Symmetric and self-adjoint operators
An operator A is symmetric if \langle Af, g\rangle = \langle f, Ag\rangle for all allowed f and g, the analogue of a symmetric matrix. That alone makes its eigenvalues real and its eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues perpendicular. Self-adjoint is a stronger condition on the set of allowed shapes, roughly that it is neither too small nor too large. Together with a further property that every instrument here has, it is what the spectral theorem needs to give enough eigenfunctions to build every shape (page 3). (For readers who know functional analysis: self-adjoint means A = A^*, domains included; for the loop the domain is H^2(S^1), and the further property is that (A + 1)^{-1} is compact.) Wikipedia
Fourier series
Writing a periodic function as a sum of sines and cosines, or of e^{in\theta}, of whole-number frequencies. On page 3 this is the expansion in eigenfunctions of -d^2/d\theta^2. Wikipedia
Laplacian
The operator \Delta u = u_{xx} + u_{yy} in the plane. At each point it is proportional to how far u sits below the average of its values on a small circle around the point. A drumhead’s operator is -c^2\Delta. Wikipedia
Separation of variables
Looking for solutions that are a product of a function of one variable and a function of another, such as u(x, t) = X(x)\,q(t). It splits one equation in several variables into simpler equations in one variable each. Wikipedia
Bessel function
The functions J_m(r) that describe how the modes of a round drum vary from the center to the rim, as sines do along a string. J_0 starts at 1 and swings like a slowly fading cosine, crossing zero at r \approx 2.405,\ 5.520,\ 8.654, \dots; for m \ge 1, J_m starts at 0. Their zeros, which are not whole-number multiples of the first one, set the drum’s frequencies. Wikipedia, and page 8
Divergence theorem
The total flow out through the boundary of a region equals the sum, over the region, of how much flow is created at each point. It turns forces along the edge of a patch of drumhead into an integral over the patch. Wikipedia
Rayleigh quotient
The ratio of a shape’s stored energy to its mass-weighted size, for example R(f) = \int EI\,(f'')^2\,dx \big/ \int \rho S\,f^2\,dx for a bar; for a shape that meets all the boundary conditions it equals \langle Af, f\rangle/\langle f, f\rangle. Its smallest value, over all nonzero shapes f that obey the conditions at clamped or fixed ends, is the lowest eigenvalue, reached at the lowest mode; in this energy form, conditions at free ends need not be imposed. It explains why removing metal near the base of a reed or tine lowers its pitch and removing it near the tip raises it. Wikipedia

Shape and spectrum

Weyl’s law
For a drum of area A, the number N(\lambda) of eigenvalues below a large number \lambda is close to A\lambda/4\pi, so the spectrum “hears” the area. A second term, -P\sqrt\lambda/4\pi with P the perimeter, is proved only under extra conditions, but for drums with smooth or polygonal rims the perimeter can be heard anyway, by a smoother count (page 9). Wikipedia
Isospectral
Having the same spectrum, counted with multiplicity. Gordon, Webb and Wolpert found two differently shaped drums that are isospectral. Wikipedia: Hearing the shape of a drum
