Glossary

The Shape of a Sound

Modified

November 7, 2026

1 · What you hear → · Glossary

Each entry gives a plain definition and a link to a fuller explanation.

Sound

Sound
The air pressure p(t) at a listener, measured from the average air pressure; it travels as a wave, at about 343 m/s at 20 °C. Wikipedia
Hertz (Hz)
One cycle per second, the unit of frequency. The A above middle C is tuned to 440 Hz. Wikipedia
Pure tone
The simplest sound, a single sinusoidal pressure swing c\sin(2\pi f t + \varphi) with frequency f, size c and phase \varphi, which says where in its cycle it starts. A tuning fork’s sound is close to one. Wikipedia
Partial, overtone
A partial is one sinusoidal component c_n\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n) of a sound, with its own frequency f_n, size c_n and fading. An overtone is any partial above the lowest. Wikipedia
Harmonic
A partial at a whole-number multiple nf of a base frequency f: the n-th harmonic. A harmonic sound is one whose partials are all harmonics of one base, so its first overtone is the second harmonic. Wikipedia
Harmonic series
The whole-number multiples f, 2f, 3f, \dots of one frequency f. Wikipedia
Periodic
A signal p(t) is periodic with period P > 0 if p(t + P) = p(t) for all t. A sum of steady partials is periodic exactly when all their frequencies are whole-number multiples of one frequency (post 1). Wikipedia
Repetition rate
This series’ term: for steady partials at exact whole-number multiples of a common frequency, 1 over the shortest period, which is the largest such frequency, so partials at 440, 660 and 880 Hz repeat at 220 Hz. For partials that fade or sit only near multiples, it is the largest frequency in the range we hear as pitch that they fit within a stated tolerance (post 1). Wikipedia: Fundamental frequency
Fundamental
For a harmonic sound, the partial at its repetition rate: usually the lowest and strongest, but it can be weak or missing and the pitch is still heard there. For a sound whose partials are not harmonics of one base, such as a drum’s, it just means the lowest partial. Wikipedia
Pitch
A perception: how high or low a sound is, measured as the frequency of the pure tone listeners match it to. When the partials f_n are whole-number multiples of one frequency f_1, the pitch is f_1, even if that partial is weak or missing; post 1 gives the exceptions. Wikipedia
Missing fundamental
A harmonic sound with no partial at its repetition rate, such as partials at 440, 660, 880, … Hz with nothing at 220 Hz; the ear still hears its pitch at 220 Hz. It is why a deep voice keeps its pitch over a telephone line that carries little below about 300 Hz. Wikipedia, and post 1
Sound pressure level, decibel
The decibel (dB) measures a ratio on a logarithmic scale: two powers in the ratio r differ by 10\log_{10} r dB, so 20 dB is a factor of 10 in pressure. The sound pressure level is 20\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{rms}}/20\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}) dB, with p_{\mathrm{rms}} the root-mean-square pressure and 20 μPa about the quietest 1 kHz tone a young listener can hear. Wikipedia: Sound pressure, Decibel
Loudness
A perception: how strong a sound seems, set mainly by the overall size of its partials, its sound pressure level, and by their frequencies (equal-loudness contours). 10 dB more sounds about twice as loud. Wikipedia, and post 1
Equal-loudness contour
A curve giving, for each frequency, the sound pressure level at which a pure tone sounds as loud as a 1 kHz tone at a chosen level. By the standard curves a 100 Hz tone needs about 79 dB to match a 1 kHz tone at 60 dB. Wikipedia, and ISO 226:2023
Attack and decay
The attack is how a sound starts, sudden for a pluck or strike and gradual for a bowed or blown note; the decay is how it fades, each partial shrinking by the factor e in its own fading time. This series counts both as part of timbre. Wikipedia: Envelope (music)
Formant
A band of frequencies that a source’s fixed resonances favor whatever note is played, such as the throat and mouth resonances that set a vowel, or a violin body’s resonances. This series also counts a clarinet’s tone-hole cutoff, roughly 1 to 1.5 kHz, as one. Wikipedia
Timbre
What tells apart two sounds of the same pitch, loudness and length. Writing the sound as p(t) = \sum_n c_n e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n), timbre comes from three things: the ratios c_n/c_1 (bright versus mellow; missing partials), how far the f_n sit from whole-number multiples (smooth, then shimmer, then clang), and how the c_n change over time, especially at the start. Wikipedia, and post 1
Duration
How long a note sounds, set by its fading times \tau_n and by the player, who may damp it (releasing a piano key, a hand on a drumhead) or, on a bowed or blown note, keep it going. Wikipedia: Duration (music)
Fading time
For a partial or mode that fades as e^{-t/\tau}, the time \tau in which it shrinks by the factor e \approx 2.7, a drop of about 8.7 dB. A mode e^{st}W with \operatorname{Re} s < 0 has \tau = -1/\operatorname{Re} s. Wikipedia: Exponential decay
Inharmonic
Having partials that are not whole-number multiples of one frequency. A bell, a drum and a kalimba tine are strongly inharmonic; a piano string is slightly inharmonic. Wikipedia
Note names: C4, A4, middle C
Notes are named by a letter from A to G and an octave number. C4 is middle C, about 262 Hz; C5 is the C an octave above it; A4 is the A at 440 Hz. Wikipedia
Semitone
The smallest step on a piano, from one key to the next, white or black. Twelve semitones make an octave. Wikipedia
Equal temperament
The usual piano tuning, in which every semitone is the ratio 2^{1/12} \approx 1.0595, so twelve make an exact octave. The other intervals then miss the simple ratios slightly: the fifth is 2^{7/12} \approx 1.4983, not 3/2. Wikipedia
Cent
One hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone, so an octave is 1200 cents and two frequencies in the ratio r are 1200\log_2 r cents apart. Trained listeners notice a few cents. Wikipedia
Octave, fifth, twelfth
Intervals named by frequency ratio: the octave is 2, the fifth 3/2 (about 1.498 in equal temperament), and the twelfth, an octave plus a fifth, about 3. The smaller ones by their simple ratios: the minor third 6/5, the major third 5/4, the fourth 4/3 and the major sixth 5/3. Wikipedia
Beats
When two pure tones of nearly equal frequency sound together, their sum swells and fades in loudness at the difference of their frequencies. Tuners listen for beats; fast beats are heard as roughness. Wikipedia
Heterodyning
Making the difference frequency f_1 - f_2 of two signals in a nonlinear circuit, for instance by multiplying them, since \sin(2\pi f_1 t)\sin(2\pi f_2 t) = \tfrac12\cos(2\pi(f_1 - f_2)t) - \tfrac12\cos(2\pi(f_1 + f_2)t); a theremin makes its tone this way from two radio oscillators. Beats differ: two tones simply added contain no tone at the difference frequency. Wikipedia: Heterodyne, Theremin, and the series index
Consonance, roughness
Consonance is how smooth or stable two notes sound together. Roughness is one ingredient of it: the harsh quality of fast beats between partials that are close but not equal. Wikipedia, and Did instruments shape our scales?

Instrument parts and playing

Nut, bridge
A string runs between two supports: one on the heavy frame, which barely moves (the nut on a guitar, the capo or agraffe on a piano), and the bridge: on a piano or guitar a hardwood strip glued to the soundboard, on a violin a carved maple piece standing on the top plate, held by the strings’ pressure. After a single pluck or strike the string, with height u(x, t) at position x and time t, pushes the bridge up and down hundreds of times a second, for seconds, with a force set only by its slope at that end, F(t) = -T\,u_x(L, t) for tension T and bridge end x = L, which keeps every frequency and weights each mode by its slope there. On a kalimba, the bridge is the bar the tines are pressed against. Wikipedia
Soundboard
The large thin wooden panel of a piano or guitar to which the bridge is glued. A 1 mm string is too thin to move the air by itself, since air simply flows around it, so the string pushes the bridge, the bridge moves the soundboard, and the soundboard pushes the air. Wikipedia
Impulse response h
On a silent instrument, tap the bridge once, sharply, and record the pressure at your ear per unit of the tap’s strength (force times duration): that recording is h(t). During a note the string’s push F on the bridge is a continuous run of such taps, each heard as a delayed, scaled copy of h, so the pressure is p(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} h(t - s)\,F(s)\,ds; this filter is fixed and linear because small motions obey linear equations, nothing changes in time, and nothing happens before its cause. So p has the same frequencies f_n as the string, once the board’s own ring at the start has died away, each only rescaled, by |H(f_n)| with H the Fourier transform of h, and delayed. Post 2, and Wikipedia
Resonator
An object with resonances: frequencies, near those of its modes, at which it responds strongly when driven. In a bowed or blown instrument it is the string or air column that the bow or breath drives, whose modes decide which partials are strong; a violin’s body is a resonator too. Wikipedia, and post 3
Open string
A string played with no finger pressing it down, so its whole length between the nut and the bridge vibrates. Wikipedia
Harmonics, on a string
Notes a string player gets by touching the string lightly at a fraction of its length, such as the middle, instead of pressing it down. Only the modes with a still point under the finger keep sounding. Wikipedia, and the light touch in post 2
Overblowing, register key
Making a wind instrument’s air column jump to a higher mode. A flutist overblows by blowing faster; a clarinetist presses the register key, which opens a small hole that spoils the lowest mode. Wikipedia, and the clarinet in post 3
Blow, draw
On a harmonica, a blow note sounds when you breathe out through a hole, and a draw note when you breathe in; each hole has one reed for each. Wikipedia
Free reed
A thin metal tongue fixed at one end that swings through a closely fitting slot, opening and shutting it like a valve. Harmonicas and accordions use free reeds. Wikipedia
Damper
A felt pad that stops a piano string when its key is released; the right pedal lifts them all, and roughly the top 20 keys have none. Wikipedia: Sustain pedal
Soft pedal, una corda
The left pedal of a piano. On a grand it shifts the hammers sideways so they hit fewer strings (una corda, “one string”); on an upright it moves them closer to the strings, so they strike more gently. Wikipedia
Practice pedal, mute
The middle pedal on many uprights. It lowers a strip of felt between the hammers and the strings, making the sound much quieter and duller. Wikipedia: Piano pedals

Grouping instruments

Hornbostel–Sachs classification
The usual scheme for sorting instruments (Erich von Hornbostel and Curt Sachs, 1914), by what makes the sound: idiophones, the body itself (glockenspiel, kalimba); membranophones, a skin (drums); chordophones, strings (violin, piano); aerophones, air (flute, clarinet, harmonica); and, added later, electrophones (theremin). Each class is divided further by decimal codes: the piano is 314.122, a board zither with a resonator box. Wikipedia, and the series index
Operator family
This series’ name for instruments whose operators share one rule up to constants and ends: the violin string and the air in a flute or clarinet share -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, and the kalimba tine, harmonica reed and glockenspiel bar share \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4. Members can still sound different ratios, because the ends are part of each operator, and a family is not a Hornbostel–Sachs class. See the series index.

Vibration

Partial derivative, u_t, u_{xx}
For a function of several variables, such as a string’s height u(x, t) at position x and time t, the rate of change in one variable with the others held fixed, named by subscripts: u_t is the velocity, u_{tt} the acceleration, u_x the slope and u_{xx}, for small slopes, the curvature. Mixed subscripts mean one derivative after another, in either order for a smooth function: u_{xt} = u_{tx}. Wikipedia
Proportional, \propto
a \propto b means that a is a fixed number times b. So f \propto 1/L^2 means that halving L multiplies f by four. Wikipedia
Angular frequency
\omega = 2\pi f: the frequency counted in radians per second instead of cycles per second. Formulas such as \omega^2 = k/m are shortest in \omega. Wikipedia
Wavenumber
The counterpart of angular frequency in space: a wave \sin kx has wavenumber k = 2\pi/\text{wavelength}. For the wave equation, \omega = ck. Wikipedia
Linear
An equation is linear when it is built from u and its derivatives only by multiplying each by a number or a given function and adding (no u^2, u\,u_x or \sin u), so that sums and multiples of solutions without an outside force are again solutions. The operators of this series are linear; what keeps a bowed string or a reed going is not. Wikipedia
First-order approximation
Keeping only the terms proportional to a small change and dropping its square and higher powers: for small \varepsilon, (1 + \varepsilon)^3 \approx 1 + 3\varepsilon. Wikipedia
Linearization
Replacing a force by its first-order part, accurate for small motions: a pendulum’s restoring force mg\sin\theta becomes mg\,\theta. The result is a linear equation, whose frequencies do not depend on the size of the motion. Wikipedia, and post 2
Superposition
Adding solutions of a linear equation to get another solution. The free motion of a modal instrument is a superposition, a sum, of its modes. Wikipedia
Mode, normal mode
A motion an object makes on its own, with nothing driving it, in which every part follows one time course, one frequency (or none) and one rate of fading; precisely, for a linear system U' = BU, a nonzero solution e^{st}W, which exists exactly when BW = sW. A normal mode is the frictionless kind, a fixed shape X swinging as \cos(\omega t + \varphi)X, which for u'' = -Au exists exactly when AX = \omega^2X (the appendix). Wikipedia
Complex mode
A mode whose parts swing at one frequency and fade at one rate but peak at different moments: the entries of W in e^{st}W have different phases. They appear with friction that the frictionless modes do not split, such as two slightly mistuned piano strings on one bridge (the appendix). NASA NESC: Complex modes in lightly damped structural systems
Standing wave
A wave whose shape stays in place, such as a mode of a string. It can be seen as two equal waves traveling in opposite directions. Wikipedia
Node, antinode, nodal line
A node is a point of a standing wave that does not move, and an antinode one that moves the most. On a drumhead or a plate a mode stays still along whole curves, its nodal lines, where Chladni’s sand collects. Wikipedia
Wave equation
The equation u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx} for the height u(x, t) of a stretched string, and its relatives for air in a pipe and for a drumhead. The constant c is the speed at which a bump travels. Wikipedia, and post 2
Membrane
A sheet with no stiffness of its own, stretched with the same tension T per unit length in every direction, like an ideal drumhead. Its small motions obey u_{tt} = c^2\Delta u, with \Delta the Laplacian, c^2 = T/\sigma and \sigma its mass per unit area. Wikipedia: Vibration of a circular membrane, and post 3
Boundary condition
A rule for what happens at the edge of a vibrating object, set by how it is held: a tied string end has u = 0, a clamped bar end u = u_x = 0, and at a pipe’s open end the pressure equals room pressure. Each is either imposed or natural. Wikipedia, and post 2
Dirichlet and Neumann conditions
A Dirichlet condition fixes the value at the edge, such as u = 0 at a string’s tied end or p = 0 at a pipe’s open end. A Neumann condition fixes the slope out of the edge, such as p_x = 0 at a clarinet’s nearly closed reed end, where the air cannot move. Wikipedia: Dirichlet, Neumann
Imposed and natural boundary conditions
An imposed condition restricts the allowed shapes, and the holder enforces it with whatever force it takes, like a clamp’s u = u_x = 0. A natural condition restricts no shape; the motion obeys it because no force acts where nothing holds the object, like a free bar end’s u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0, and in the energy form, the Rayleigh quotient, only imposed conditions are required. P. J. Olver, Boundary conditions and null Lagrangians in the calculus of variations and elasticity, and post 3
End correction
The amount by which an open pipe acts longer than it is, about 0.6 of its radius beyond a bare open end, because the air just outside the end moves with the air inside. Frequencies computed with p = 0 exactly at the end come out slightly too high without it. Wikipedia, J. Wolfe, UNSW: Pipes and harmonics, and post 3
Damping
Friction that drains energy from a vibration, so that it dies away. A term b\,u_t in the equation, proportional to velocity, is the simplest model. Wikipedia
Critical damping
The friction at which a mass on a spring, released, returns to rest fastest without swinging past it: \gamma^2 = 4\lambda in u'' + \gamma u' + \lambda u = 0. Its solutions (a + bt)\,e^{-\gamma t/2} are not combinations of modes, the sign of a Jordan block. Wikipedia: Damping, and the appendix
Internal friction
Energy lost inside a material as it bends or stretches, turned into heat on every swing. With losses to the supports and to radiation, it is one of the three ways a vibrating object loses energy. Wikipedia: Anelasticity, and post 2
Radiation, as a loss
The energy a vibrating object loses to the sound it makes, which is how we hear it and also damps it; shapes that push the air one way over a wide area, like a drumhead’s round modes, radiate strongly and fade fast. Because the sound carries energy into unbounded air, radiation makes the problem no longer self-adjoint (the appendix). D. A. Russell, Sound fields radiated by simple sources; N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapters 7 and 18; the drums in post 3
Self-sustained oscillation, limit cycle
An oscillation kept at a steady size by a steady supply of energy, such as a bow drawn at constant speed or a steady breath, through a mechanism that is not linear. It settles into one repeating cycle, a limit cycle, from any start near it, and in simple models such as the van der Pol oscillator from any start except complete rest. Wikipedia
Softening and stiffening
In a softening system the restoring force grows less than in proportion to the displacement, so wider swings are slower, as for a pendulum; in a stiffening one it grows more, so wider swings are faster, as for a string or drumhead swung wide, whose average tension rises. Either way the frequency moves with the size of the motion, which in a linear system it cannot. Wikipedia: Duffing equation, and the appendix
Coupling
Two vibrating parts are coupled when the motion of one pushes on the other, as when piano strings share a bridge. Coupled parts trade energy, and their modes mix. Wikipedia
Parametric resonance
Growth of a vibration when a property such as its stiffness is changed back and forth periodically, most strongly at about twice its natural frequency, as when a child pumps a swing. The simplest model, x'' = -(1 + \varepsilon\cos 2t)\,x for a small number \varepsilon, has no fixed modes. Wikipedia: Parametric oscillator
Floquet theory
The theory of linear equations whose coefficients repeat in time with some period P, such as Mathieu’s x'' = -(1 + \varepsilon\cos 2t)\,x with P = \pi. Floquet’s theorem (1883): when the map from the state at time 0 to the state at time P has distinct eigenvalues, every solution is a combination of e^{\mu t}p(t) with p of period P, and an exponent \mu with positive real part means growth. Wikipedia, and the appendix
Added mass
The extra inertia of a vibrating surface that must push the surrounding air or water along with it, which lowers its frequencies. On a timpani head it lowers the coarsest shapes the most (the drums in post 3). Wikipedia
Helmholtz motion
The way a bowed string moves. The string sticks to the bow, slips back, and sticks again; seen from the side, a single sharp corner races back and forth along the string, one round trip per period. J. Wolfe, UNSW: The bowed string
Clamped, free and pinned ends
Three ways of holding the end of a bar: a clamped end can neither move nor tilt, u = u_x = 0 (a kalimba tine’s base); a free end carries no bending moment or shear force, u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 (its tip); a pinned end is held in place but free to pivot, u = u_{xx} = 0 (close to a piano string’s ends). The same bar sounds 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 clamped at one end and free at the other, and 1 : 2.76 : 5.40 free at both. Wikipedia: Euler–Bernoulli beam theory, and post 3
Cantilever, clamped bar
A bar fixed rigidly at one end and free at the other, like a diving board. A kalimba tine, pressed against a bridge near one end, is close to one. Wikipedia
Neutral axis
The line along a bent bar that keeps its length, between the stretched outside and the compressed inside of the curve. For a bar of one material it runs through the middle of each cross-section. Wikipedia, and post 3
Euler–Bernoulli beam
The standard model of a bending bar under no tension, \mu u_{tt} = -EI\,u_{xxxx}, with \mu the mass per unit length, E the Young’s modulus and I the second moment of area. Bending is curvature, two derivatives, and balancing forces along the bar adds two more. Wikipedia
Bending moment, shear force
Cut a bent bar at some point: the bending moment is the turning effect one side exerts on the other, and the shear force is the sideways force across the cut. A free end carries neither. Wikipedia: Bending moment, Shear force
Young’s modulus
How stiff a material is when stretched: the force per unit area needed, divided by the fraction by which it lengthens. Steel is about 200 GPa; nylon, used for classical guitar strings, is a few GPa. Wikipedia
Second moment of area
How the shape of a bar’s cross-section resists bending, written I. For a rectangle of width w and thickness h, I = wh^3/12: doubling the thickness makes the bar eight times harder to bend. Wikipedia
Hyperbolic functions, cosh and sinh
\cosh x = (e^x + e^{-x})/2 and \sinh x = (e^x - e^{-x})/2, with \cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1, (\cosh x)' = \sinh x and (\sinh x)' = \cosh x. In a bar they describe bending that grows or fades along the bar instead of wiggling. Wikipedia
Bulk modulus, adiabatic
How hard a material is to compress: the pressure needed, divided by the fraction by which the volume shrinks. A sound wave’s squeezes are too quick for heat to flow (adiabatic), which for air gives about 1.4 \times atmospheric pressure. Wikipedia
Capacitance
How much charge a capacitor holds per volt across it, C = q/V, in farads. In a circuit of a coil and a capacitor, 1/C plays the part of a spring’s stiffness. Wikipedia, and post 3
Inductance
How strongly a coil opposes a change in the current through it: V = L\,dI/dt, measured in henries. In a circuit of a coil and a capacitor, L plays the part of a mass. Wikipedia, and post 3

Matrices and operators

Matrix times vector
For a square matrix K and a list of numbers v, the product Kv is the list whose j-th entry is \sum_k K_{jk}v_k: row j of K, multiplied entry by entry with v and added up. Wikipedia
Eigenvector, eigenvalue
A nonzero vector v that a matrix K only rescales, Kv = \lambda v; the number \lambda is its eigenvalue. For two beads on a tied string the eigenvectors are (1, 1) and (1, -1), and for this series’ operators an eigenvalue is a squared angular frequency, \lambda = \omega^2 (the appendix). Wikipedia
Eigenvalue
See eigenvector.
Multiplicity
The number of independent eigenvectors, none a combination of the others, that share one eigenvalue. On a ring, the cosine and sine shapes of one wave give their frequency multiplicity 2. Wikipedia
Perpendicular, orthogonal
Two vectors are perpendicular, or orthogonal, when their dot product, the sum of the products of matching entries, is zero. For functions the dot product is replaced by the inner product. Wikipedia
Basis
A set of directions from which every vector can be built in exactly one way by adding multiples of them, such as (1, 1)/\sqrt2 and (1, -1)/\sqrt2 in the plane. The modes of a vibrating object play the same role for its shapes. Wikipedia
Orthonormal
A set of vectors each perpendicular to the others and of length 1, such as (1, 1)/\sqrt2 and (1, -1)/\sqrt2. In an orthonormal basis X_1, X_2, \dots, the amount of X_i in a vector x is the dot product X_i \cdot x. Wikipedia
Symmetric matrix
A square matrix of real numbers whose entry in row i, column j equals the entry in row j, column i. By the spectral theorem its eigenvalues are real and it has a full set of perpendicular eigenvectors. Wikipedia
Diagonalizable
A square matrix B is diagonalizable when it has a basis of eigenvectors W_1, \dots, W_n, complex numbers allowed, so that B = W\operatorname{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n)\,W^{-1} with W the matrix of the W_k and s_k their eigenvalues. For u'' = -Au, the generator is diagonalizable exactly when A is diagonalizable and invertible (the appendix). Wikipedia
Jordan block
A square matrix with one number s down its diagonal, 1 just above it and 0 elsewhere, such as \left(\begin{smallmatrix} s & 1 \\ 0 & s \end{smallmatrix}\right): the smallest way for a matrix to lack a basis of eigenvectors. For U' = BU a block of size 2 gives the solutions (a + bt)\,e^{st}, which are not combinations of modes: a rigid drift (s = 0) or critical damping. Wikipedia: Jordan normal form
Commuting matrices
Two square matrices commute when AC = CA; a multiple of the identity commutes with every matrix, and C = \alpha + \beta A commutes with A. Two symmetric matrices that commute share an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, which is why friction that commutes with A keeps the frictionless mode shapes (the appendix). Wikipedia
Determinant
A number computed from a square matrix that is zero exactly when the matrix sends some nonzero vector to zero; for rows (p, q) and (r, s) it is ps - qr. So linear equations with zero right-hand sides have a nonzero solution exactly when the determinant of their coefficients is zero. Wikipedia
Circulant matrix
A square matrix in which each row is the row above shifted one place to the right, wrapping around. The complex waves e^{2\pi ijk/N} are eigenvectors of every circulant, and of a symmetric one, such as beads closed into a ring, so are the real cosine and sine waves. Wikipedia
Discrete Fourier transform (DFT)
Writing a list of N numbers, indexed by j = 0, \dots, N-1, as a sum of the waves \cos(2\pi jk/N) and \sin(2\pi jk/N) for k = 0, \dots, N-1, or with Euler’s formula of e^{2\pi ijk/N}. Wikipedia
Euler’s formula
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta, where i^2 = -1. It packs a cosine and a sine into one expression that is easier to multiply and differentiate. Wikipedia
Operator
For an object held in a given way, the linear map, defined on the shapes the holding allows, that sends a shape to minus the acceleration it causes, so the free motion obeys u_{tt} = -Au; for a string of tension T and mass \mu per length, A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 with c^2 = T/\mu, on shapes that are zero at both ends. Each eigenvalue \lambda of A gives a frequency \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi. Wikipedia, and post 2
Generator
For a linear equation U' = BU that does not change in time, the matrix or operator B, which turns the state U into its rate of change; for u'' = -Au the state is U = (u, u') and B = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & I \\ -A & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right). The solutions are U(t) = e^{tB}U(0), which is why B is said to generate them. Wikipedia: Matrix exponential, and the appendix
Modal system
This series’ term for a linear system U' = BU whose modes form a basis of its states (in infinite dimensions, a Riesz basis of the energy space), so that every motion is a sum of modes. Two beads on a tied string are modal; closed into a ring, which can drift, they are not (the appendix).
Eigenfunction
A nonzero function f that an operator only rescales: Af = \lambda f. Example: -\frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}\cos 3\theta = 9\cos 3\theta. Wikipedia
Spectrum
For this series’ instruments, the list of the operator’s eigenvalues \lambda, each giving a frequency \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi that passes unchanged into the sound; in general, the set of numbers z for which A - z has no bounded inverse. An infinite string has the continuous spectrum [0, \infty) and no eigenvalues, and for a self-adjoint A \ge 0 the spectrum is a list exactly when the resolvent is compact. Wikipedia, and the appendix
Inner product
A way to multiply two vectors or two functions to get a number, generalizing the dot product: for functions on the circle, \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f\,\bar g\,d\theta, with \bar g the complex conjugate of g. Two functions are perpendicular if it is zero. Wikipedia
L^2(S^1)
The space of functions on the circle S^1 with \int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta finite, with the inner product above (functions that differ only on a set of measure zero count as the same). It is where the shapes of a vibrating loop live. Wikipedia
Symmetric and self-adjoint operators
An operator A is symmetric if \langle Af, g\rangle = \langle f, Ag\rangle for all allowed f and g, which makes its eigenvalues real and its eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues perpendicular. Self-adjoint, A = A^* with domains included, is a stronger condition on the allowed shapes; with a compact resolvent it gives enough eigenfunctions to build every shape (the appendix). Wikipedia
Fourier series
Writing a periodic function as a sum of sines and cosines, or of e^{in\theta}, of whole-number frequencies. In the appendix it is the expansion in the modes of the loop of string. Wikipedia
Laplacian
The operator \Delta u = u_{xx} + u_{yy} in the plane; at each point it is proportional to how far u sits below the average of its values on a small circle around the point. A drumhead’s operator is -c^2\Delta. Wikipedia
Separation of variables
Looking for solutions that are a fixed shape times a function of time, u(x, t) = X(x)\,q(t). Putting this into u_{tt} = -Au gives q''X = -q\,AX, so such a motion exists exactly when AX = \lambda X for a number \lambda, and then q'' = -\lambda q, a spring. Wikipedia
Bessel function
The functions J_m(r) that describe how a round drum’s modes vary from the center to the rim, as sines do along a string. Their zeros, 2.405, 5.520, 8.654, \dots for J_0, are not whole-number multiples of the first one, and set the drum’s frequencies. Wikipedia, and the drums in post 3
Divergence theorem
The total flow out through the boundary of a region equals the sum, over the region, of how much flow is created at each point. It turns forces along the edge of a patch of drumhead into an integral over the patch. Wikipedia
Rayleigh quotient
A shape’s stored energy divided by its mass-weighted size, such as R(X) = \int T\,(X')^2\,dx \big/ \int \mu\,X^2\,dx for a string; at a mode it equals the eigenvalue \lambda, and its smallest value over the shapes that obey the imposed end conditions is the lowest eigenvalue. It explains why removing metal near the base of a reed or tine lowers its pitch and removing it near the tip raises it. Wikipedia
Min–max principle
List the eigenvalues in order, \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots; then \lambda_n is the least, over all n-dimensional families of allowed shapes, of the largest Rayleigh quotient in the family (the Courant–Fischer theorem). So added stiffness never lowers an eigenvalue, added mass never raises one, and holding the object more firmly never lowers one. Wikipedia, and the appendix

The deeper theory

Resolvent
For an operator A and a number z, the inverse (A - z)^{-1}, when it exists and is bounded: the operator that solves Au - zu = f for u. The numbers z at which it fails to exist are the spectrum. Wikipedia: Resolvent formalism
Compact operator, compact resolvent
An operator on a Hilbert space is compact when it is a limit, in the operator norm, of operators whose outputs fill only finitely many dimensions; every matrix is compact. An operator A has compact resolvent when (A - z)^{-1} is compact, and for self-adjoint A \ge 0 this holds exactly when the spectrum is a list \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \to \infty of finite multiplicity with an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, as for strings, bars and drums of finite size. Wikipedia: Compact operator on Hilbert space, and the appendix
Green’s function, Green’s operator
For a linear problem Au = f with boundary conditions, the Green’s function G(x, y) is the response at x to a unit point load at y, and the Green’s operator f \mapsto \int G(x, y)\,f(y)\,dy is A^{-1} written as an integral. For the bar its kernel is continuous on the square [0, L]^2, which makes it compact (the appendix). Wikipedia
Functional calculus
A way to apply a function \varphi of numbers to an operator: for a symmetric matrix with orthonormal eigenvectors X_i and eigenvalues \lambda_i, \varphi(A)f = \sum_i \varphi(\lambda_i)\,(X_i\cdot f)\,X_i, and von Neumann’s spectral theorem does the same for a self-adjoint operator. It lets the appendix write every motion of u'' = -Au as \cos(t\sqrt A)\,f + \big(\sin(t\sqrt A)/\sqrt A\big)\,g. Wikipedia: Borel functional calculus
Semigroup
For U' = BU, the operators e^{tB}, t \ge 0, each sending a starting state to the state at time t, with e^{0B} = I and e^{(t + t')B} = e^{tB}e^{t'B}. For an unbounded generator such as a damped string’s, semigroup theory (the Hille–Yosida and Lumer–Phillips theorems) builds e^{tB} directly, proving that every start has exactly one motion whether or not there are enough modes. Wikipedia: C0-semigroup
Riesz basis
A basis of a Hilbert space that behaves like an orthonormal one up to fixed factors: every vector is uniquely U = \sum_k c_kW_k with m\sum_k|c_k|^2 \le \|U\|^2 \le M\sum_k|c_k|^2 for fixed 0 < m \le M. A set can be complete, with no nonzero vector perpendicular to all of it, and still fail to be a Riesz basis. Encyclopedia of Mathematics, and the appendix
Normal and non-normal
A matrix B is normal when it commutes with its adjoint, BB^* = B^*B, and exactly then it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. A non-normal matrix may be diagonalizable with nearly parallel eigenvectors, so that decaying modes nearly cancel at the start and the motion grows for a while before it decays. Wikipedia: Normal matrix, and the appendix
Pseudospectrum
For a matrix B and a small \varepsilon > 0, the set of numbers z with \|(B - z)^{-1}\| > 1/\varepsilon, equivalently the eigenvalues of all B + E with \|E\| < \varepsilon. For a non-normal B it can reach far beyond the spectrum, measuring behaviour such as growth before decay (Trefethen and Embree). Wikipedia

Shape and spectrum

Weyl’s law
For a drum of area |\Omega|, the number N(\Lambda) of eigenvalues of -\Delta below a large number \Lambda is close to |\Omega|\Lambda/4\pi, so the area can be heard. A second term, -P\sqrt\Lambda/4\pi with P the perimeter, is proved only under extra conditions, but for smooth or polygonal rims the perimeter can be heard anyway (Can you hear the shape of a drum?). Wikipedia
Isospectral
Having the same spectrum, counted with multiplicity. Gordon, Webb and Wolpert found two differently shaped drums that are isospectral. Wikipedia: Hearing the shape of a drum
