Builds on page 2. If you skipped it: on a string of tension T with beads spaced h apart, bead j at height u_j feels the force \frac{T}{h}(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}), which pulls it toward the average of its two neighbors, and the loop’s motion splits into waves called modes, each with its own frequency. About 10 minutes, plus 12 for the folded proofs.

The puzzle. Keep a loop of beads the same size and the same total weight, but share that weight among more and more beads. The frequencies creep toward 1, 2, 3, … times the lowest, and in the limit, a continuous loop of string, they land on the whole numbers exactly. Why exactly? And a harder question, which the best mathematicians of the 18th century argued about for decades: can every shape of a string be built from these simple waves?

Slide the number of beads from a few up to the last position, the continuous loop, and watch the stems settle onto the whole-number grid.

Stems (the vertical lines): frequencies of the first ten distinct modes as multiples of the lowest, against the whole-number grid. The last slider position is the continuous loop. Each mode is played with loudness 1/k, so that the high ones are not shrill. With few beads the frequencies don't line up and the sound is less clear; on the continuous loop they are whole-number multiples, so they repeat together and fuse into one note (page 0).

The second question has a history. From the 1740s on, Jean d’Alembert, Leonhard Euler and Daniel Bernoulli all worked on the vibrating string. D’Alembert showed in 1747 that a string can move in essentially any smooth shape. Bernoulli held that every vibration is a sum of simple modes, which for a string are sine-shaped. Euler objected that a sum of sines could not represent an arbitrary shape, such as a string plucked into a sharp corner, and d’Alembert doubted that such a sum was physical at all. The argument went on for decades. This page answers the first question, and then the second.

Why exactly whole numbers

From beads to a loop. Measure position around the loop by the angle \theta, and choose units so that the loop has circumference 2\pi. Bead j sits at \theta_j = jh with h = 2\pi/N. Let \mu be the mass per unit length, so each bead has mass m = \mu h, and write u(\theta, t) for the height of the loop at angle \theta and time t, so that u_j(t) = u(\theta_j, t). Dividing the bead equation from the previous page by m gives \ddot u_j = \frac{T}{\mu}\cdot\frac{u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}}{h^2}. The fraction on the right has a simple meaning. (u_{j+1} - u_j)/h is the slope of the string just to the right of bead j, and (u_j - u_{j-1})/h the slope just to the left. Their difference, divided by h once more, measures how fast the slope changes: the second derivative. So as the beads crowd together, h \to 0, the bead equation becomes \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial \theta^2}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}. Here u depends on two things, the angle and the time. The symbol \partial u/\partial t, a partial derivative, is the derivative in time with the angle held fixed, and \partial u/\partial\theta the derivative in angle with the time held fixed; the second derivatives are also written u_{tt} and u_{\theta\theta}. This is the wave equation. The constant c is the speed at which a bump travels around the loop; the second demo shows it.

Step-by-step derivationWhy the second difference becomes the second derivative uses Taylor’s theorem

Expand the neighbors with Taylor’s theorem, assuming the shape has four continuous derivatives: u(\theta \pm h) = u \pm h\,u' + \tfrac{h^2}{2}u'' \pm \tfrac{h^3}{6}u''' + O(h^4), where O(h^4) stands for terms no bigger than a constant times h^4. Add the two expansions and subtract 2u. The odd terms cancel in pairs: u(\theta + h) - 2u(\theta) + u(\theta - h) = h^2 u''(\theta) + O(h^4). Divide by h^2: the fraction equals u'' + O(h^2), which tends to u''. What was held fixed: the circumference, the tension T, and the mass per length \mu. So as the beads multiply, each one gets lighter in proportion.

The middle bead sits above the average of its neighbors by about -\tfrac{h^2}{2}u'', which is positive where the curve bends down (u'' < 0). That is why the force on a bead, and so its acceleration, is proportional to the curvature u''.

The operator. Write the wave equation as u_{tt} = -c^2 A u, \qquad A = -\frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}. The operator A takes a shape f(\theta) and returns a new shape, -f''. Strictly, the operator in the sense of the series page, minus the restoring force per unit mass, is c^2A; the constant c^2 only scales the eigenvalues, so this page works with A and puts c back in the frequencies. The minus sign is a convention that makes A of a bump positive at the bump; the restoring force per unit mass is -c^2 Af, which pulls the bump back down. If you read page 2, A is the continuous version of the beads’ matrix K/h^2.

Which shapes. A shape on the loop is a function f(\theta) that returns to itself after one turn: f(\theta + 2\pi) = f(\theta).

Eigenfunctions and eigenvalues. An eigenfunction of A is a shape that A only rescales: Af = \lambda f for some number \lambda, called the eigenvalue. Try \cos n\theta, a wave with n crests around the loop. Its second derivative is -n^2\cos n\theta, so A\cos n\theta = n^2\cos n\theta, \qquad A\sin n\theta = n^2\sin n\theta . These shapes close up after one turn exactly when n is a whole number. So the spectrum of A, the list of its eigenvalues, is 0, 1, 4, 9, 16, \dots Each n^2 with n \ge 1 belongs to two eigenfunctions, \cos n\theta and \sin n\theta, which are the same wave shifted by a quarter of a wavelength; n = 0 belongs to the constant shape, the whole loop lifted at once. The first fold below proves that there are no other eigenvalues.

Frequencies. The shape \cos(n\theta)\cos(\omega t) solves the wave equation when \omega^2 = c^2 n^2: differentiate it twice in t and twice in \theta and compare. Here \omega is the angular frequency, in radians per second, and the frequency in hertz is f = \omega/2\pi. So mode n sounds at f_n = cn/2\pi, exactly n times the lowest. (In ordinary units, for a loop of circumference \ell, this is f_n = nc/\ell: the time for a bump to travel once around, \ell/c, is the period of the lowest mode.) The eigenvalues are perfect squares; the frequencies, proportional to their square roots, are whole-number multiples of one frequency. The ring of beads approaches this from below:

ProofThe eigenvalues are exactly 0, 1, 4, 9, … uses integration by parts

Step 1: no eigenvalue is negative. For periodic shapes f and g, integrate by parts twice. The boundary terms are values at \theta = 2\pi minus values at \theta = 0, which cancel because the shapes are periodic: \int_0^{2\pi} (Af)\,g\,d\theta = -\int_0^{2\pi} f''g\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} f'g'\,d\theta = -\int_0^{2\pi} f g''\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} f\,(Ag)\,d\theta . An operator with this property is called symmetric, by analogy with a symmetric matrix. (With the right choice of allowed shapes it is also self-adjoint, the stronger property that the spectral theorem in the fold below needs.) If Af = \lambda f with f not identically zero, take g = f in the middle expression: \lambda\int_0^{2\pi} f^2\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}(f')^2\,d\theta \ge 0, so \lambda \ge 0. (For a complex-valued f, use \int f\bar f\,d\theta = \int |f|^2\,d\theta instead, as in the complex-form fold below; the same computation shows that \lambda is real and at least 0.)

Step 2: solve the equation. Af = \lambda f means f'' = -\lambda f.

  • If \lambda = 0, then f = a + b\theta, which returns to itself after one turn only when b = 0. Eigenvalue 0, eigenfunctions the constants.
  • If \lambda > 0, write \lambda = s^2 with s > 0; then f = a\cos s\theta + b\sin s\theta, a wave that repeats every 2\pi/s radians. It returns to itself after one full turn exactly when 2\pi is a whole number of these periods, that is, when s is a whole number n. Eigenvalue n^2, eigenfunctions \cos n\theta and \sin n\theta.

Step 3: eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues are perpendicular, in the sense used below: \int_0^{2\pi} \cos n\theta\cos m\theta\,d\theta = 0 for n \ne m, and similarly for sines and for a sine against a cosine. This can be checked directly with the product formulas for cosines, and it also follows from Step 1, just as for a symmetric matrix.

For readers who know functional analysis: A is not defined on every square-integrable function, only on those with two derivatives in L^2, the Sobolev space H^2(S^1). On that domain it is self-adjoint, not merely symmetric: in Fourier coefficients it is multiplication by n^2 on the sequences with \sum n^4|c_n|^2 < \infty. That there is no spectrum besides these eigenvalues follows from the completeness of the eigenfunctions, the Theorem fold below.

Who was right

Bernoulli, in a sense that took mathematicians more than a century to make precise. Every shape of the loop, corners included, is a sum of the operator’s eigenfunctions. Euler’s worry was reasonable, since every sine is smooth and a corner is not, but an infinite sum of smooth waves need not be smooth.

The Fourier series is the eigenfunction expansion. With the beads we wrote the starting shape as a sum of eigenvectors and let each one swing on its own. Here the same move reads f(\theta) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos n\theta + b_n\sin n\theta\big), the Fourier series of f. To find the numbers a_n and b_n, use the fact that the eigenfunctions are perpendicular. For whole numbers n \ne m, \int_0^{2\pi}\cos n\theta\,\cos m\theta\,d\theta = 0, \qquad \int_0^{2\pi}\sin n\theta\,\sin m\theta\,d\theta = 0, while \int_0^{2\pi}\cos n\theta\,\sin m\theta\,d\theta = 0 for all n and m, and \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 n\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2 n\theta\,d\theta = \pi for n \ge 1. To check them, turn each product into a sum with \cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)] and its relatives for sines; every cosine or sine of a nonzero whole-number multiple of \theta integrates to zero over a full turn. These integrals are the continuous version of the dot product: for two columns of bead heights, \sum_j u_j v_j\,h is a dot product times h, and as the beads crowd together it becomes \int f g\,d\theta. Now multiply both sides of the series by \cos m\theta and integrate from 0 to 2\pi. Every term drops out except the one with n = m, which leaves \pi a_m. So a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)\cos n\theta\,d\theta, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)\sin n\theta\,d\theta, just as a vector’s component along an axis is its dot product with that axis. The constant term is written a_0/2 so that the same formula also gives a_0: for n = 0, \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 0\,d\theta = 2\pi, not \pi. Released from rest in the shape f, each eigenfunction swings at its own frequency: u(\theta, t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos n\theta + b_n\sin n\theta\big)\cos(c\,n\,t). Every term returns to its starting value after the time 2\pi/c, so the whole motion repeats, and any sound it makes has a pitch. Complex formThe same series with e^{in\theta}, and the space of all shapes uses complex numbers

With Euler’s formula e^{in\theta} = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta, the pair \cos n\theta, \sin n\theta can be traded for the pair e^{in\theta}, e^{-in\theta}, and A e^{in\theta} = n^2 e^{in\theta}. The Fourier series becomes f(\theta) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\,e^{in\theta}, \qquad c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)\,e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta , with c_{\pm n} = (a_n \mp i b_n)/2 for n \ge 1 and c_0 = a_0/2. The perpendicularity above becomes \langle e^{in\theta}, e^{im\theta}\rangle = 0 for n \ne m, for the inner product \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f\,\bar g\,d\theta; the bar, complex conjugation, is what makes \langle f, f\rangle a positive number for complex-valued f. The natural space of shapes is L^2(S^1), all functions on the circle S^1 with \int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta finite: the shapes of finite “total squared height”.

TheoremEvery shape is a sum of eigenfunctions advanced: uses functional analysis

Statement. The functions e^{in\theta}/\sqrt{2\pi}, n \in \mathbb{Z}, form an orthonormal basis of L^2(S^1). That is, for every f in L^2(S^1), \Big\| f - \sum_{|n| \le M} c_n e^{in\theta} \Big\| \to 0 \quad (M \to \infty), \qquad \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta = \sum_n |c_n|^2 , the second identity being Parseval’s.

Why it needs a proof. For a symmetric N\times N matrix, N perpendicular eigenvectors automatically span everything, because the space has dimension N. Here there are infinitely many eigenfunctions and the space is infinite-dimensional, so one has to show that no shape is perpendicular to all of them. One standard proof shows that trigonometric sums can approximate any continuous periodic function uniformly (Fejér’s theorem), and that continuous functions are dense in L^2. See Stein and Shakarchi, chapters 2 and 3.

Pointwise. Convergence in L^2 is convergence of the total squared error. At individual points more care is needed: there are continuous functions whose Fourier series diverge at a point. For the shapes on this page, continuous and made of straight pieces, the series converges at every point. The plucked triangle’s coefficients shrink like 1/n^2, which is why a few terms already look right in the demo.

What it took. The old argument could not be settled until “sum” had a precise meaning for infinitely many terms. Fourier claimed such expansions for arbitrary functions in his 1807 work on heat. Dirichlet proved in 1829 that the series converges at every point for a broad class of functions, including shapes with corners; the L^2 statement above belongs to the early 20th century; and that the Fourier series of every L^2 function converges at almost every point was proved only in 1966, by Lennart Carleson (Wikipedia). In the L^2 sense, Bernoulli was right and Euler’s corner is no obstacle.

The pattern for every later page. Each instrument’s operator A will be self-adjoint and nonnegative, and (A + 1)^{-1} will be a compact operator. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators then gives the same three facts as here: a list of real eigenvalues growing to infinity, perpendicular eigenfunctions, and an expansion of every shape in them. Only the list and the shapes change.

Reader questionu'' + k^2u = 0 has a single k. Where does an infinite list of frequencies come from?

In that equation k was chosen in advance, and for each k it has the two solutions \cos k\theta and \sin k\theta. The eigenvalue question runs the other way: for which k does a solution go once around the loop and come back to itself? Every whole number k does, and nothing limits how many times a wave may wind around, so the list never ends. The count matches the number of ways the system can move: N beads have N eigenvalues, counted with repetitions, and a continuous loop has infinitely many.

For readers who know functional analysis: on an infinite straight string, with no loop to close up and no ends, every k \ge 0 gives a bounded wave e^{ikx} and the spectrum is the whole half-line [0, \infty). Strictly, these waves are not eigenfunctions, because \int|e^{ikx}|^2\,dx is infinite. This is one reason the general definition of spectrum is more than a list of eigenvalues. A bounded instrument, closed up or with ends that are not too irregular, has a discrete list.

Reader questionHow is the shape \cos n\theta related to the factor \cos(\omega_n t)?

Both come from separating variables. Look for a motion in which the shape stays fixed and only its size changes: u = X(\theta)\,q(t). Substituting into u_{tt} = c^2u_{\theta\theta} and dividing by Xq gives q''/q = c^2X''/X. The left side depends only on t and the right only on \theta. Changing t alone cannot change the right side, so the left side cannot change either; the same holds the other way round, so both sides equal one constant. That constant cannot be positive, because A has no negative eigenvalues (Step 1 of the proof above), so we call it -\omega^2. That splits the equation in two. In time, q'' = -\omega^2 q, so q = \cos(\omega t + \phi), times any constant: the mass on a spring. (For \omega = 0 the solution is q = a + bt instead: the loop drifts as a whole.) In space, -X'' = (\omega/c)^2 X with X periodic: the eigenvalue problem for A, which allows only \omega/c = n. The space part chooses the frequency, and the time part swings at it. The time factor is a cosine, for every nonzero frequency, for every instrument in this series, because every operator here has no negative eigenvalues and every equation has two time derivatives and no friction term; damping, added on page 7, multiplies the cosine by a decaying factor. Everything instrument-specific lives in the space part.

What we found

A continuous loop of string has an operator, -\tfrac{d^2}{d\theta^2}, acting on its shapes; it is what the beads’ matrix becomes when they crowd together. Its eigenvalues are exactly 0, 1, 4, 9, \dots, so the frequencies are exactly 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots times a base frequency. Writing a shape as a sum of this operator’s eigenfunctions is the Fourier series. This is the smallest example of an operator’s spectrum: one dimension, no ends, the same string everywhere. Every later page follows the same pattern, with a different operator: a list of eigenvalues that grows without end, eigenfunctions that are perpendicular to one another, and every shape a sum of eigenfunctions. Only the list and the shapes change.
