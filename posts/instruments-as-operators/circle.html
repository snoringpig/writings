Why does a loop of string land exactly on the lines?

3 · A loop of string

Modified

October 27, 2026

← 2 Beads · The Shape of a Sound · Spectrum of an operator · Map · 4 String →

Builds on page 2, beads on a ring. Math: up to Taylor’s theorem (quoted) and integrals. About 3 minutes for the summary; about 25 with the mathematics.

Keep a loop of beads the same size and the same weight, but share that weight among more and more beads. The frequencies creep toward 1, 2, 3, … times the lowest, and on a continuous loop of string they land on the whole numbers exactly. Why exactly? And a harder question, argued over for decades in the 18th century: can every shape of a string be built from these simple waves?

Listen first: slide the number of beads up to the last position, the continuous loop, and watch the stems settle onto the lines.

Stems: the first ten frequencies, as multiples of the lowest; the slider's last position is the continuous loop. Higher modes are drawn and played quieter, so they are not shrill.

The recipe on this instrument, in brief. This is the recipe in its simplest form.

  1. Newton’s law for beads crowded together into a continuous loop gives the wave equation u_{tt} = c^2 u_{\theta\theta} of a continuous string.
  2. The operator is minus c^2 times the second derivative, A = -c^2\,d^2/d\theta^2, acting on shapes that close up after one turn.
  3. Its spectrum: the waves \cos n\theta and \sin n\theta have eigenvalues \lambda_n = c^2n^2, so frequencies f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi = n f_1, whole-number multiples of the lowest.
  4. On the diagram: every stem sits exactly on a whole-number line.
  5. What we hear: every shape is a sum of these waves (its Fourier series), so a plucked loop repeats exactly: a note.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

1 · Newton’s law: the equation of motion

Mathematics used: derivatives, partial derivatives (explained), Taylor’s theorem (quoted) and integrals of sines and cosines.

Page 2 applied Newton’s law, mass times acceleration equals force, to each bead on a ring. Here we let the beads crowd together, keeping the loop’s size, its tension and its total mass fixed, and watch the bead equation turn into the equation of a continuous string.

1. Set up the loop. Measure position around the loop by the angle \theta, and choose units so that the loop has circumference 2\pi. Put N beads on it, bead j at \theta_j = jh, with spacing h = 2\pi/N. Let T be the string’s tension and \mu its mass per unit length, so each bead has mass m = \mu h. Write u(\theta, t) for the height of the loop at angle \theta and time t, so that bead j is at height u_j(t) = u(\theta_j, t).

2. Each bead is pulled toward its neighbors. The string pulls each bead along itself, on both sides. If the string makes a small angle \phi with the horizontal, the upward part of its pull is T\sin\phi; for a small angle \sin\phi \approx \tan\phi (both are about \phi in radians), and \tan\phi is the slope (page 2 draws it). So the pull from the right contributes the tension times the slope to the right, and the pull from the left minus the tension times the slope to the left: \begin{aligned} m\,\ddot u_j &= T\,\frac{u_{j+1} - u_j}{h} - T\,\frac{u_j - u_{j-1}}{h} \\ &= \frac{T}{h}\,\big(u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}\big). \end{aligned} Divide by the mass m = \mu h: \ddot u_j = \frac{T}{\mu}\cdot\frac{u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}}{h^2}.

3. The fraction measures how the string bends. The fraction compares each bead with its two neighbors. (u_{j+1} - u_j)/h is the slope just to the right of bead j, and (u_j - u_{j-1})/h the slope just to the left; their difference, divided by h once more, measures how fast the slope changes. Where the string bends down, the middle bead sits above the average of its neighbors, and the string pulls it down:

Taylor’s theorem makes this precise. It says that near \theta a smooth shape is its value, plus slope × h, plus half the second derivative × h^2, and so on; if you haven’t met it, the slope argument above is the whole idea. For a shape with four continuous derivatives, \begin{aligned} u(\theta \pm h) = u &\pm h\,u' + \tfrac{h^2}{2}u'' \\ &\pm \tfrac{h^3}{6}u''' + O(h^4), \end{aligned} where O(h^4) stands for terms no bigger than a constant times h^4. Add the two expansions and subtract 2u; the odd terms cancel in pairs: \begin{aligned} &u(\theta + h) - 2u(\theta) + u(\theta - h) \\ &\quad = h^2 u''(\theta) + O(h^4). \end{aligned} So the fraction equals u'' + O(h^2): the second derivative, the string’s curvature, up to an error that vanishes as the beads crowd together. The middle bead sits above the average of its neighbors by about -\tfrac{h^2}{2}u'', which is positive where the curve bends down (u'' < 0).

4. Let the beads crowd together. The height u now depends on two things, the angle and the time. Its derivative in time with the angle held fixed is written \partial u/\partial t, a partial derivative, and its derivative in angle with the time held fixed \partial u/\partial\theta; the second derivatives are also written u_{tt} and u_{\theta\theta}. As h \to 0, the bead equation becomes \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial \theta^2}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}. This is the wave equation. The constant c is the speed at which a bump travels around the loop; the second demo below shows it.

What we assumed. Small slopes, so that the tension stays T and its upward part is T times the slope; the same tension and mass per length everywhere; no stiffness, no air and no friction; and shapes smooth enough for Taylor’s theorem. As the beads multiplied, the circumference, T and \mu were held fixed, so each bead got lighter in proportion.

2 · The operator

The right side of the wave equation is a fixed rule applied to the shape: take its second derivative in angle and multiply by c^2. That rule, with its sign reversed, is the loop’s operator. It acts on one shape f(\theta) at a time, so it is written with d rather than \partial. The minus sign makes Af positive at a bump, where f'' < 0, so the force -Af pulls the bump back down. The loop has no ends; what it imposes instead is that every shape closes up after one turn, and that condition is part of the operator. If you read page 2: there the operator was \frac{T}{mh}K = \frac{c^2}{h^2}K, and -K/h^2 is the second difference divided by h^2, which becomes the second derivative as the beads crowd together; so A is the continuous version of page 2’s matrix.

The loop’s operator. The equation of motion is \begin{gathered} u_{tt} = -A\,u, \\ A = -c^2\,\frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}, \end{gathered} acting on shapes that close up after one turn, f(\theta + 2\pi) = f(\theta). In words: A takes a shape and returns minus its curvature, times c^2: the restoring force per unit mass, reversed.

3 · Its spectrum

A mode is a shape that the operator only rescales: AX = \lambda X for some number \lambda. The shape X is an eigenfunction of A, and \lambda its eigenvalue. Released in that shape, the loop keeps it and swings at the angular frequency \omega = \sqrt\lambda, as on page 1. The list of all eigenvalues is the spectrum of A. Here is the whole computation.

1. Try a wave with n crests. A wave with n crests around the loop is \cos n\theta. Its second derivative is -n^2\cos n\theta, so \begin{gathered} A\cos n\theta = c^2n^2\cos n\theta, \\ A\sin n\theta = c^2n^2\sin n\theta . \end{gathered} A wave n times shorter is pulled back n^2 times harder. In plain words: squeeze a wave into half the length, at the same height, and it bends 4 times as sharply (the slope doubles, and it changes over half the distance: 2 × 2 = 4), so the pull is 4 times as strong.

2. Ask which waves close up, to find every eigenfunction. The equation AX = \lambda X says X'' = -(\lambda/c^2)\,X.

  • If \lambda = 0, then X = a + b\theta, which closes up after one turn only when b = 0. Eigenvalue 0 belongs to the constant shape: the whole loop lifted at once, which makes no sound.
  • If \lambda > 0, write \lambda = c^2s^2 with s > 0. Then X = a\cos s\theta + b\sin s\theta, a wave that repeats every 2\pi/s radians. It closes up after one full turn exactly when 2\pi is a whole number of these periods, that is, when s is a whole number n. Eigenvalue c^2n^2, eigenfunctions \cos n\theta and \sin n\theta.
  • A negative \lambda is impossible: the proof fold below shows that A has no negative eigenvalues.
Reader questionThe equation of one wave has one frequency in it. Where does an infinite list come from?

In the equation u'' + k^2u = 0, the number k is chosen in advance, and for each k it has the two solutions \cos k\theta and \sin k\theta. The eigenvalue question runs the other way: for which k does a solution go once around the loop and come back to itself? Every whole number k does, and nothing limits how many times a wave may wind around, so the list never ends. The count matches the number of ways the system can move: N beads have N eigenvalues, counted with repetitions, and a continuous loop has infinitely many.

For readers who know functional analysis: on an infinite straight string, with no loop to close up and no ends, every k \ge 0 gives a bounded wave e^{ikx} and the spectrum is the whole half-line [0, \infty). Strictly, these waves are not eigenfunctions, because \int|e^{ikx}|^2\,dx is infinite. This is one reason the general definition of spectrum is more than a list of eigenvalues. A bounded instrument, closed up or with ends that are not too irregular, has a discrete list.

3. Each eigenvalue gives a frequency. The motion \cos(n\theta)\cos(\omega t) solves u_{tt} = -Au when \omega^2 = \lambda_n = c^2n^2: differentiate it twice in t, apply A, and compare. Here \omega is the angular frequency, in radians per second, and the frequency in hertz is \omega/2\pi. So mode n sounds at f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{c\,n}{2\pi} = n\,f_1 . (In ordinary units, for a loop of circumference \ell, this is f_n = nc/\ell: the time for a bump to travel once around, \ell/c, is the period of the lowest mode.)

Reader questionHow is a mode’s shape related to its swinging in time?

Both come from separating variables. Look for a motion in which the shape stays fixed and only its size changes: u = X(\theta)\,q(t). Substituting into u_{tt} = c^2u_{\theta\theta} and dividing by Xq gives q''/q = c^2X''/X. The left side depends only on t and the right only on \theta. Changing t alone cannot change the right side, so the left side cannot change either; the same holds the other way round, so both sides equal one constant. That constant cannot be positive, because A has no negative eigenvalues (proof fold below), so we call it -\omega^2. That splits the equation in two. In time, q'' = -\omega^2 q, so q = \cos(\omega t + \phi), times any constant: the mass on a spring. (For \omega = 0 the solution is q = a + bt instead: the loop drifts as a whole.) In space, AX = -c^2X'' = \omega^2 X with X periodic: the eigenvalue problem for A, with \lambda = \omega^2, which allows only \omega = cn. The space part chooses the frequency, and the time part swings at it. The time factor is a cosine, for every nonzero frequency, for every instrument in this series, because every operator here has no negative eigenvalues and every equation has two time derivatives and no friction term; damping, added on page 7, multiplies the cosine by a decaying factor. Everything instrument-specific lives in the space part.

The loop’s spectrum. Eigenfunctions: the constant, and \cos n\theta and \sin n\theta for n = 1, 2, 3, \dots Eigenvalues: \lambda_n = c^2n^2, that is 0, c^2, 4c^2, 9c^2, \dots; each with n \ge 1 belongs to two eigenfunctions, the same wave shifted by a quarter of a wavelength. Frequencies: f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi = n f_1 with f_1 = c/2\pi. In words: the eigenvalues are c^2 times perfect squares, so the frequencies, their square roots divided by 2\pi, are exactly whole-number multiples of one frequency.

ProofNo negative eigenvalues, and different waves are perpendicular uses integration by parts

No eigenvalue is negative. For periodic shapes f and g, integrate by parts twice. The boundary terms are values at \theta = 2\pi minus values at \theta = 0, which cancel because the shapes are periodic: \int_0^{2\pi} (Af)\,g\,d\theta = -c^2\!\int_0^{2\pi} f''g\,d\theta = c^2\!\int_0^{2\pi} f'g'\,d\theta = -c^2\!\int_0^{2\pi} f g''\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} f\,(Ag)\,d\theta . An operator with this property is called symmetric, by analogy with a symmetric matrix. (With the right choice of allowed shapes it is also self-adjoint, the stronger property that the spectral theorem in the complex-numbers fold further down needs.) If Af = \lambda f with f not identically zero, take g = f in the middle expression: \lambda\int_0^{2\pi} f^2\,d\theta = c^2\int_0^{2\pi}(f')^2\,d\theta \ge 0, so \lambda \ge 0. (For a complex-valued f, use \int f\bar f\,d\theta = \int |f|^2\,d\theta instead, as in the complex-numbers fold further down; the same computation shows that \lambda is real and at least 0.)

Eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues are perpendicular, in the sense used below: \int_0^{2\pi} \cos n\theta\cos m\theta\,d\theta = 0 for n \ne m, and similarly for sines and for a sine against a cosine. This can be checked directly with the product formulas for cosines, and it also follows from the symmetry above, just as for a symmetric matrix.

For readers who know functional analysis: A is not defined on every square-integrable function, only on those with two derivatives in L^2, the Sobolev space H^2(S^1). On that domain it is self-adjoint, not merely symmetric: in Fourier coefficients it is multiplication by c^2n^2 on the sequences with \sum n^4|c_n|^2 < \infty. That there is no spectrum besides these eigenvalues follows from the completeness of the eigenfunctions, proved in the complex-numbers fold further down.

4. Every shape is a sum of these waves. From the 1740s on, Jean d’Alembert, Leonhard Euler and Daniel Bernoulli argued about the vibrating string. D’Alembert showed in 1747 that a string can move in essentially any smooth shape. Bernoulli held that every vibration is a sum of simple modes, which for a string are sine-shaped. Euler, who allowed a plucked string’s sharp corner as a starting shape, objected that a sum of sines could not represent such arbitrary shapes, and d’Alembert doubted that such a sum was physical at all.

Bernoulli was right, in a sense that took mathematicians more than a century to make precise. Every shape of the loop, corners included, is a sum of its eigenfunctions: its Fourier series, \begin{aligned} f(\theta) = \frac{a_0}{2} &+ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos n\theta \\ &\qquad + b_n\sin n\theta\big). \end{aligned} The doubt is natural, since every wave is smooth and a corner is not, but an infinite sum of smooth waves need not be smooth.

How much of each wave a shape contains. The waves are perpendicular, in the sense of integrals: for whole numbers n \ne m, \begin{gathered} \int_0^{2\pi}\cos n\theta\,\cos m\theta\,d\theta = 0, \\ \int_0^{2\pi}\sin n\theta\,\sin m\theta\,d\theta = 0, \end{gathered} while \int_0^{2\pi}\cos n\theta\,\sin m\theta\,d\theta = 0 for all n and m, and \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 n\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2 n\theta\,d\theta = \pi for n \ge 1. (To check them, turn each product into a sum with \cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)] and its relatives for sines; every cosine or sine of a nonzero whole-number multiple of \theta integrates to zero over a full turn.) These integrals are the continuous version of the dot product: for two columns of bead heights, \sum_j u_j v_j\,h is a dot product times h, and as the beads crowd together it becomes \int f g\,d\theta. Multiply both sides of the series by \cos m\theta and integrate from 0 to 2\pi: every term drops out except the one with n = m, which leaves \pi a_m. So \begin{gathered} a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)\cos n\theta\,d\theta, \\ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)\sin n\theta\,d\theta, \end{gathered} just as a vector’s component along an axis is its dot product with that axis. (The constant term is written a_0/2 so that the same formula also gives a_0: for n = 0, \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 0\,d\theta = 2\pi, not \pi.)

PreciselyThe same series with complex numbers, and why every shape is a sum of waves uses complex numbers; the second half, functional analysis

With complex numbers. With Euler’s formula e^{in\theta} = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta, the pair \cos n\theta, \sin n\theta can be traded for the pair e^{in\theta}, e^{-in\theta}, and A e^{in\theta} = c^2n^2 e^{in\theta}. The Fourier series becomes f(\theta) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\,e^{in\theta}, \qquad c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)\,e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta , with c_{\pm n} = (a_n \mp i b_n)/2 for n \ge 1 and c_0 = a_0/2. The perpendicularity above becomes \langle e^{in\theta}, e^{im\theta}\rangle = 0 for n \ne m, for the inner product \langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f\,\bar g\,d\theta; the bar, complex conjugation, is what makes \langle f, f\rangle a positive number for complex-valued f. The natural space of shapes is L^2(S^1), all functions on the circle S^1 with \int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta finite: the shapes of finite “total squared height”.

Every shape is a sum of eigenfunctions. The functions e^{in\theta}/\sqrt{2\pi}, n \in \mathbb{Z}, form an orthonormal basis of L^2(S^1). That is, for every f in L^2(S^1), \Big\| f - \sum_{|n| \le M} c_n e^{in\theta} \Big\| \to 0 \quad (M \to \infty), \qquad \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f|^2\,d\theta = \sum_n |c_n|^2 , the second identity being Parseval’s.

Why it needs a proof. For a symmetric N\times N matrix, N perpendicular eigenvectors automatically span everything, because the space has dimension N. Here there are infinitely many eigenfunctions and the space is infinite-dimensional, so one has to show that no shape is perpendicular to all of them. One standard proof shows that trigonometric sums can approximate any continuous periodic function uniformly (Fejér’s theorem), and that continuous functions are dense in L^2. See Stein and Shakarchi, chapters 2 and 3.

Pointwise. Convergence in L^2 is convergence of the total squared error. At individual points more care is needed: there are continuous functions whose Fourier series diverge at a point. For the shapes on this page, continuous and made of straight pieces, the series converges at every point. The plucked triangle’s coefficients shrink like 1/n^2, which is why a few terms already look right in the demo.

What it took. The old argument could not be settled until “sum” had a precise meaning for infinitely many terms. Fourier claimed such expansions for arbitrary functions in his 1807 work on heat. Dirichlet proved in 1829 that the series converges at every point for a broad class of functions, including shapes with corners; the L^2 statement above belongs to the early 20th century; and that the Fourier series of every L^2 function converges at almost every point was proved only in 1966, by Lennart Carleson (Wikipedia). In the L^2 sense, Bernoulli was right, and a corner is no obstacle.

The pattern for every later page. Each instrument’s operator A will be self-adjoint and nonnegative, and (A + 1)^{-1} will be a compact operator. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators then gives the same three facts as here: a list of real eigenvalues growing to infinity, perpendicular eigenfunctions, and an expansion of every shape in them. Only the list and the shapes change.

Why beads fall short. A bead feels only its two neighbors. When a crest is many beads wide, that is enough to sense the bend almost exactly. When a crest is only a few beads wide, the neighbors sit partway down the wave, and the bead underestimates how sharply the string bends: the second difference is smaller than h^2 times the curvature. So the pull is weaker than n² times, and the frequency falls short of n×, the more so the shorter the wave. Crowd in more beads and any given wave becomes many beads wide: its shortfall vanishes.

5 · What we hear

Why exactly on the lines. Mode n swings at f_n = n f_1, so every mode returns to its starting position after the time 1/f_1 = 2\pi/c; the whole motion repeats, and the sound it makes is a note at f_1, when f_1 is in the range we hear as pitch. The tension, the mass and the size of the loop cannot spoil this; they only set f_1 = c/2\pi. The whole-number lines of the pitch ruler come from one fact: a wave on a loop must close up, so it has a whole number of crests. Press the first demo’s button at a few bead counts: with few beads the chord sounds less clear; on the loop the modes fuse into one note (page 0).

Released, each wave swings at its own frequency. From rest in the shape f, with the coefficients a_n, b_n of its Fourier series, \begin{aligned} u(\theta, t) &= \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} q_n(\theta)\,\cos(2\pi f_n t), \\ q_n(\theta) &= a_n\cos n\theta + b_n\sin n\theta. \end{aligned} The sound is this sum: each mode contributes a pure tone at its own frequency f_n, as strong as the shape contains it. The demos synthesize exactly that: the button in the first demo adds the first modes’ frequencies, mode 1 set to 196 Hz, quieter for higher modes; the “Hear the loop” button in the demo below adds the plucked shape’s modes with the strengths from its Fourier series.

What we found

Newton’s law, applied to beads and then to a continuous loop, gave the wave equation u_{tt} = c^2u_{\theta\theta}. Read as u_{tt} = -Au, its operator is A = -c^2\,d^2/d\theta^2, acting on shapes that close up. Its spectrum is \lambda_n = c^2n^2: a wave must close up, so it has a whole number n of crests, and the string pulls it back n^2 times harder. The frequencies, \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi, are exactly 0, 1, 2, 3, … times one base frequency: on the spectrum diagram, every stem on a line. Writing a shape as a sum of the eigenfunctions is its Fourier series, and every shape, corners included, can be written that way; released, each term sounds at its own frequency, and together they repeat exactly: a note. Every later page has the same pattern with a different operator: a list of eigenvalues that grows without end, and a set of simple shapes from which every shape is built. Only the list and the shapes change.
