Why does a drum thud?

8 · The drum

Builds on page 4, the violin string and the loop of page 3. Math: up to Bessel functions (described). About 3 minutes for the summary; about 25 with the mathematics.

A drumhead is a sheet pulled tight: a string with a second dimension. Pluck a string and you get a note you could hum. Strike a drum and you get a thud. What changes when the string becomes a sheet?

Listen first: tap the head anywhere, then tap dead center and compare.

1.00
Tap the head anywhere. The ring marks where we listen.

The motion is the sum of the 40 lowest modes, slowed about 300 times. Dead center sounds only the round modes; a strike on a mode's still line leaves that mode silent.

The recipe on this instrument, in brief.

  1. Newton’s law on a small patch of the head: tension pulls each point toward the average height of the points around it.
  2. The operator is the two-dimensional version of the string’s, -c^2\Delta, with the rim held still.
  3. Its spectrum, for a round drum, comes from the zeros of Bessel functions: frequencies at 1×, 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×, … the lowest.
  4. On the diagram: those stems fall between the whole-number lines; no base frequency in the range we hear as pitch puts them all on or near lines.
  5. What we hear: a sound that doesn’t repeat at any rate we hear as pitch, and fades fast: a thud. Where you strike changes how loud each mode is, never where it sits.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

1 · Newton’s law: the equation of motion

Mathematics used: partial derivatives, integrals over an area, the divergence theorem and polar coordinates (quoted), and Bessel functions (described, computed numerically).

Here is where the drum’s frequencies come from. Apply Newton’s law, mass times acceleration equals force, to one small patch of the drumhead. The steps are the string’s, with one more dimension.

1. One small patch. Let u(x, y, t) be the height of the head above its rest position at the point (x, y) at time t; subscripts are partial derivatives. The head has mass \sigma per unit area, and its tension T is a force per unit length of any cut through it. Take any small patch D of the head. Its mass is the sum of \sigma\,dA over its tiny area pieces dA; the double integral \iint_D below means exactly that, adding up over all the small pieces dA of D. So Newton’s law for its up-and-down motion reads \iint_D \sigma\,u_{tt}\,dA = \text{net force on the patch}.

2. The pull across its edge. Along each short piece ds of the patch’s edge, the sheet outside pulls with force T\,ds, along the surface and outward. If the head makes a small angle \theta with the horizontal there, the upward part of that pull is T\sin\theta\,ds; for a small angle \sin\theta \approx \tan\theta (both are about \theta in radians), and \tan\theta is the slope. So the upward part is T\,\partial_n u\,ds, where \partial_n u is the slope of the head in the outward direction across the edge. This is the string’s step, with the slope taken across the edge.

Tension pulls outward all around the edge. Where the sheet bulges, the pulls tilt and their vertical parts add up to a net force, which step 3 below works out.

3. Add the pulls around the edge. The symbol \oint_{\partial D} means adding up all the way around the edge \partial D of the patch. Adding up the pulls around the edge turns into adding up one quantity over the inside of the patch. That is the divergence theorem, quoted here, applied to the slope vector (u_x, u_y): its outward part across the edge is \partial_n u, and its divergence is u_{xx} + u_{yy}. So T\oint_{\partial D} \partial_n u\,ds = T\iint_D \Delta u\,dA . The quantity \Delta u = u_{xx} + u_{yy}, the Laplacian, adds the second derivatives in the two directions across the sheet. In one dimension a patch is a segment, its edge is its two endpoints, and this step is the fundamental theorem of calculus, u_x(b) - u_x(a) = \int_a^b u_{xx}\,dx: the string’s force again.

4. Newton’s law, at every point. Steps 1 to 3 give \iint_D \sigma\,u_{tt}\,dA = T\iint_D \Delta u\,dA. This holds for every patch, however small, so the two integrands are equal at every point: \sigma\,u_{tt} = T\,\Delta u . In words: on a string, tension pulls each point toward the average height of its two neighbors; on a sheet it pulls each point toward the average height of all the points around it, and \Delta u measures how far a point sits below that average.

What we assumed. Small slopes; a tension that is the same everywhere and does not change as the head moves; no bending stiffness; no air; and no damping and no sound radiation, so the model’s modes never fade, while a real head loses its energy to the air quickly. The air matters for real drums, as the timpani paragraph in stage 5 says.

2 · The operator

Dividing by the mass per unit area \sigma puts the equation in the series’ form u_{tt} = -A\,u: the force per unit mass is a fixed rule applied to the head’s shape, and that rule is the drum’s operator. The rim completes it: the rim is clamped, so u = 0 there. This is the drum’s boundary condition.

The drum’s operator. u_{tt} = -A\,u, \qquad A = -c^2\,\Delta, with c^2 = T/\sigma, acting on shapes of the head that are zero on the rim. In words: tension pulls each point toward the average of its surroundings, and the rim holds still.

3 · Its spectrum

A mode is a shape X that the operator only rescales: A X = \lambda X, with the number \lambda called its eigenvalue (an eigenvalue of A, constant included). Released in that shape, the head keeps it and swings as X\cos\omega t, with \omega = \sqrt{\lambda}, at the frequency f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi. The list of all the eigenvalues is the operator’s spectrum. Writing \lambda = c^2k^2, the mode condition is -\Delta X = k^2 X inside the drum, with X = 0 on the rim and \omega = ck. For a round drum of radius a, it can be solved by splitting it into a problem around the center and a problem outward from it, a separation of variables.

1. A round drum calls for distance and angle. Measure position by the distance r from the center and the angle \theta. In these coordinates the Laplacian reads \Delta X = X_{rr} + \frac1r X_r + \frac{1}{r^2}X_{\theta\theta}. This is a standard change of variables, quoted here; it follows from the chain rule (Laplace operator in polar coordinates). The X_r/r term is there because circles around the center grow longer as r grows.

2. Split the shape into an around part and an outward part. Try a shape that is a function of r times a function of \theta, X = R(r)\,\Theta(\theta). Substituting into -\Delta X = k^2X and multiplying by r^2/(R\Theta) gives \frac{r^2R'' + rR' + k^2r^2R}{R} = -\frac{\Theta''}{\Theta}. The left side depends only on r, the right only on \theta. Change \theta alone: the left side can’t change, so neither can the right. So both equal one constant.

3. Around the center: the loop of page 3. Going once around the drum must bring \Theta back to itself, so -\Theta'' = m^2\Theta with \Theta repeating every turn. This is exactly the loop’s eigenvalue problem from the circle page: m is a whole number and \Theta = \cos m\theta or \sin m\theta. A mode with m \ge 1 has m straight still lines through the center, like spokes.

4. Outward from the center: Bessel’s equation. What remains for R is r^2R'' + rR' + (k^2r^2 - m^2)R = 0, Bessel’s equation. It depends on r only through kr, so its solutions are functions of kr. Of its two essentially different solutions, one, the Bessel function J_m(kr), is finite at the center; the other becomes infinite there, which would take infinite energy, so it is not a free mode. J_m plays the part the sine played on a string: it wiggles like a cosine or sine whose swings slowly shrink.

5. The rim picks out the frequencies. The rim condition R(a) = 0 says J_m(ka) = 0: ka must be a zero of J_m. Writing j_{m,n} for the n-th positive zero, k = j_{m,n}/a. The zeros have no closed form and are computed numerically; those of J_0 are 2.405, 5.520, 8.654, …, in the ratios 1 : 2.30 : 3.60.

The round drum’s spectrum. Modes J_m(j_{m,n}\,r/a)\cos m\theta and J_m(j_{m,n}\,r/a)\sin m\theta, for m = 0, 1, 2, \dots and n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, with frequencies f_{m,n} = \frac{c\,j_{m,n}}{2\pi a}. In order, relative to the lowest: 1, 1.593, 2.136, 2.295, 2.653, 2.917, … Each frequency with m \ge 1 belongs to two modes, the cosine and the sine, the same pattern turned. The eigenvalues of A are \lambda_{m,n} = c^2 j_{m,n}^2/a^2.

These are all the modes: expanding any mode in a Fourier series in \theta (page 3) reduces -\Delta X = k^2X, term by term, to Bessel’s equation for each m.

DetailsWhy the pairs are exact, and how the zeros are spaced

Each frequency with m \ge 1 belongs to exactly two modes because Bessel functions of different whole-number orders never share a positive zero; this was conjectured by Bourget and proved by Carl Ludwig Siegel in 1929. This page computes the zeros numerically. For large n they approach j_{m,n} \approx (n + \tfrac m2 - \tfrac14)\pi: evenly spaced for one fixed m, but shifted by an amount that depends on m. The round head’s symmetry shows up as a multiplicity of 2.

4 · On the spectrum diagram

These are the frequencies in your last strike, as multiples of the lowest: each stem is one j_{m,n}/j_{0,1} from the result box of stage 3. After 1×, the next few, 1.59×, 2.14× and 2.30×, sit between the lines. Higher up the stems crowd together, so some land near a line by chance, such as 2.92× and 4.06×; but no base frequency in the range we hear as pitch puts them all on or near lines. Each stem’s height is how strongly that mode sounds at the listening ring for the strike you made: strike somewhere else and the heights change, never the positions. Tap a stem to see that mode on the drumhead and hear it alone.

5 · What we hear

Off the lines and fading fast: a thud. The frequencies of stage 3 don’t sit at whole-number multiples of any base frequency we hear as pitch, so the sound doesn’t repeat at any such rate; and a real head gives its energy to the air quickly, so it fades fast. Together, a thud.

A struck head moves as the sum of its modes, u = \sum a_{m,n} X_{m,n} e^{-t/\tau_{m,n}} \cos(2\pi f_{m,n} t + \phi_{m,n}), each with its own fading time \tau_{m,n} (damping, added to the model), so its sound is a sum of tones at the frequencies of stage 3; the drum demo at the top synthesizes it exactly this way.

Why the frequencies miss the lines. The frequencies are the zeros of the Bessel functions, scaled. For the modes with no spokes, the zeros of J_0 come at nearly even steps, about \pi apart, but the first one is short, about 0.77\pi rather than \pi, so the later zeros are not multiples of the first: the frequencies go 1 : 2.30 : 3.60. Each number of spokes gives its own sequence, shifted by a different amount, and the sequences interleave at unrelated places. Merged, they give the drum’s list 1×, 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×, 2.65×, 2.92×, …, scattered between the lines. One more pattern: every mode with spokes comes in a pair, the same pattern turned, with exactly the same frequency. That is the round drum’s symmetry, heard in its spectrum.

The sand draws the still lines. In 1808 Ernst Chladni showed the French Institute in Paris, the body that then held the Academy of Sciences, a trick: sprinkle sand on a metal plate, draw a violin bow along its edge, and the sand leaps into a pattern, a different one for each note. The sand is jostled wherever the plate moves and comes to rest where it doesn’t, so each pattern is one mode’s still lines. Here is the same trick on a drumhead: pick a frequency and watch where the sand goes.

Each button drives the drumhead at one of its own frequencies, given as a multiple of the lowest. Every grain is jostled in proportion to how much the head moves under it, so grains drift until they reach a place where the head stays still. Chladni used stiff metal plates, whose operator involves bending, not tension; the sand behaves the same way on both.

Napoleon offered a prize for explaining the patterns. Sophie Germain won it in 1816, the first woman to receive the Paris Academy’s grand prize in mathematics, with a theory that still had errors; Gustav Kirchhoff gave the full theory of vibrating plates, with the correct edge conditions, in 1850. Finding the still lines and finding the frequencies are one problem: on a drumhead, the one on this page; on a plate, the same problem with the plate’s own operator.

PreciselyWhere the sand collects uses polar coordinates

The mode J_m(kr)\cos m\theta with k = j_{m,n}/a is zero where \cos m\theta = 0, at 2m angles, which pair up into m lines through the center, and along the circles where J_m(kr) = 0 inside the rim, n - 1 of them for the n-th zero. Those are exactly the lines where the sand collects.

Where you strike. A sharp strike starts each mode in proportion to how far that mode moves at the point struck. Every mode with spokes stays still at the center, so a strike at dead center sounds only the round modes, those with no spokes; a strike on one of a mode’s still lines leaves that mode silent. Timpanists strike between halfway and three-quarters of the way out from the center, near the still lines of several modes, which keeps those modes quiet (HyperPhysics).

DetailsHow strongly a strike excites each mode, in this model uses integrals over an area

Number the modes X_1, X_2, \dots in order of frequency. A sharp strike, a brief push at a point p, gives mode i a starting speed in proportion to X_i(p), the mode’s height at the point struck, the same rule as the piano’s hammer on page 7. We model what you hear as the speed of the head at a listening point q, which picks up mode i in proportion to X_i(q). Dividing by \iint X_i^2\,dA, the integral of the mode’s square over the head, removes the arbitrary size of each shape. So mode i sounds with weight X_i(p)\,X_i(q)\big/\iint X_i^2\,dA. (A real drum radiates sound from its whole head, and some modes radiate better than others; this model ignores that.) For the timpani strike point: for the first four modes with one still circle inside, that circle lies between 0.44 and 0.65 of the radius. The lowest mode, which dies away fast and is not musically useful, is excited less there than by a strike at the center.

The exception: timpani. A timpani does have a pitch. Its kettle and the air it encloses shift the head’s modes enough to matter: measured, the modes with one, two, three and four lines through the center, and no still circle inside, sit close to 2, 3, 4 and 5 times a common base frequency (HyperPhysics; Fletcher and Rossing). The note is normally heard at the first of these, the “2”, with the next two a fifth and an octave above it (Vienna’s Institute of Musical Acoustics); the missing base, an octave lower, may be heard instead (HyperPhysics). Pulled near the lines, the drum gives a note.

What we found

Newton’s law on a small patch of the head (stage 1) gave the drum’s operator, A = -c^2\,\Delta, with the rim held still (stage 2). For a round head its spectrum is set by the zeros of Bessel functions (stage 3), f_{m,n} = c\,j_{m,n}/2\pi a. Within each family, one for each number of spokes, the zeros are nearly evenly spaced but the first step is short, and the families interleave, so the frequencies land at 1×, 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×, …: off the lines of the spectrum diagram (stage 4). The sound doesn’t repeat at any rate we hear as pitch, and it fades fast: a thud (stage 5). The round symmetry shows up as pairs of modes with exactly the same frequency. Change what surrounds the head, as a timpani’s kettle does, and the frequencies can be pulled back near the lines.

Same operator, different constants, as a first model. Toms, snares and timpani share this operator on a disk. They differ in tension, size and the mass of the head, and in what surrounds it, and the air in and around a drum changes the operator a little.
