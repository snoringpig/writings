A kalimba tine is a steel bar clamped at one end. Its first overtone sits at about 6.3 times its fundamental, off the whole numbers, and overtones off the whole numbers are what make a bell clang. So why does a kalimba sound like a clean note?

Listen first: tap a tine. Then tick the box that keeps the overtones ringing, and tap it again.

Tap a tine, or drag across several.

The tines are drawn to scale: a tine half as long sounds two octaves higher, as this page explains.

In one sentence. A tine’s overtones sit off the whole-number multiples of its fundamental (6.27×, 17.55×), yet a kalimba sounds clean because those overtones die within a fraction of a second (observed, not derived) and the fundamental rings on alone.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

The operator, in one line

A string springs back because it is stretched; a tine is under no tension at all, and springs back because steel resists being bent. Let u(x, t) be the tine’s height at distance x from the clamp at time t. Newton’s law on a thin slice of the tine, with the forces that bending sets up inside it, gives the beam equation u_{tt} = -\kappa^2 u_{xxxx}, with four derivatives along the tine where a string had two; post 2 derives it, as the Euler–Bernoulli beam. The constant is \kappa^2 = EI/\rho S: E is steel’s Young’s modulus, how stiff the material is; I the second moment of area of the cross-section, how strongly its shape resists bending; \rho the density and S the cross-section’s area.

The ends complete the operator. At the base the tine is held down, so it can neither move nor tilt: u = 0 and u_x = 0. A bar held this way at one end and free at the other is called a cantilever. At the free tip nothing bends or pushes the bar: the bending moment, the turning effect that bends it, and the shear force, the sideways force across a cut, both vanish, which reads u_{xx} = 0 and u_{xxx} = 0.

The tine’s operator. u_{tt} = -A\,u, \qquad A = \kappa^2\,\frac{d^4}{dx^4}, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\rho S}, with u = u_x = 0 at the clamp and u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 at the tip. In words: the force per unit mass pulling each point back is the fourth derivative of the shape, times a constant set by the steel and the cross-section. The string’s operator was -c^2\,d^2/dx^2: two derivatives instead of four.

What four derivatives do. Squeeze a shape into half the length, and every rate of change along it doubles. A string’s pull depends on its curvature, a rate of change of a rate of change: 2 × 2 = 4 times harder. A tine’s push depends on how that curvature itself changes along the tine, twice over: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 times harder. Both of the tine’s surprises below come from this. The model assumes small slopes, a uniform bar clamped at exactly one point, and no damping or sound radiation: in the model every mode rings forever, and how fast each one dies is taken from measurement.

Its spectrum

A mode is a shape X(x) that the operator only rescales, A X = \lambda X. Such a shape swings at the frequency f = \sqrt\lambda/2\pi, and the list of all the eigenvalues \lambda is the operator’s spectrum. For the tine, the mode equation is \kappa^2 X'''' = \lambda X.

1. Which shapes does the operator only rescale? Write \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, so that X'''' = \beta^4 X: four derivatives give back the shape, times \beta^4. Four functions do this: \cos\beta x and \sin\beta x, whose sign flips cancel after four derivatives, and \cosh\beta x and \sinh\beta x, where \cosh y = (e^y + e^{-y})/2 and \sinh y = (e^y - e^{-y})/2 are the hyperbolic functions, whose derivatives are each other, (\cosh y)' = \sinh y and (\sinh y)' = \cosh y, with no sign changes at all. So X is a combination of the four, with four unknown constants.

2. The ends pick the shapes. The four end conditions are four equations for those four constants. They have a solution other than all zero exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1 , the mode condition, worked out in the fold below.

3. Find the roots. Since \cosh\beta L grows fast, \cos\beta L must be close to 0, so \beta L sits near (n - \tfrac12)\pi, closer and closer as n grows. Solved numerically, the roots are \beta L = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548,\ 10.9955, \dots; the first is the furthest from its estimate, \pi/2 \approx 1.571.

DetailsThe mode condition from the four end conditions uses a 2×2 determinant

The clamped end. X(0) = 0 and X'(0) = 0 leave two constants: X = a\,(\cos\beta x - \cosh\beta x) + b\,(\sin\beta x - \sinh\beta x) .

The free tip. Differentiating, and dropping the common factors \beta^2 and \beta^3, the conditions X''(L) = 0 and X'''(L) = 0 read, with the shorthand c_1 = \cos\beta L, s_1 = \sin\beta L, c_2 = \cosh\beta L, s_2 = \sinh\beta L: \begin{gathered} -(c_1 + c_2)\,a - (s_1 + s_2)\,b = 0, \\ (s_1 - s_2)\,a - (c_1 + c_2)\,b = 0 . \end{gathered}

When is there a mode? Two equations pa + qb = 0 and ra + sb = 0 have a solution other than a = b = 0 exactly when ps - qr = 0, the determinant (solve the first for a and substitute into the second). Here (c_1 + c_2)^2 + (s_1 + s_2)(s_1 - s_2) = c_1^2 + s_1^2 + 2c_1c_2 + c_2^2 - s_2^2 = 2 + 2c_1c_2 = 0, using c_1^2 + s_1^2 = 1 and c_2^2 - s_2^2 = 1 (multiply out the definitions). So the tine has a mode exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1. The modes are X_n = \cosh\beta_n x - \cos\beta_n x - \sigma_n(\sinh\beta_n x - \sin\beta_n x), with \sigma_n = (\sinh\beta_nL - \sin\beta_nL)/(\cosh\beta_nL + \cos\beta_nL) from the second equation.

The tine’s spectrum. Eigenvalues \lambda_n = \kappa^2\beta_n^4, with \beta_nL = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548, \dots the roots of \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1, and frequencies f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = \frac{\kappa\,(\beta_nL)^2}{2\pi L^2} . Since f_n \propto (\beta_nL)^2, the frequency ratios are 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39 : \cdots, growing like (2n-1)^2: off the whole numbers.

DetailsCompare pinned ends: why the clamp and the free tip matter

Compare pinned ends. A bar held in place at both ends but free to pivot has u = u_{xx} = 0 at each end. Then the shapes \sin(n\pi x/L) satisfy all four conditions, four derivatives multiply them by (n\pi/L)^4, and the ratios are 1 : 4 : 9 : 16: whole numbers. If every root of the clamped–free tine sat exactly at (n - \tfrac12)\pi, its ratios would be the whole numbers 1 : 9 : 25 too; the shift of the first root, set by the clamp and the free tip, is what puts them off the lines.

On the spectrum diagram, the pitch ruler of post 1 with one stem per mode, one tine’s frequencies are 1×, then 6.27×, off the gray whole-number lines, and further up 17.55× and 34.39×, off the right edge. Each stem’s height is how hard that mode pushes sideways on the bridge just after a pluck (next section). Tap a stem to see the shape of that mode.

Plucked: a ringing tine

A plucked tine rings: it moves as the sum of its modes, u = \sum_n a_n\,e^{-t/\tau_n}X_n(x)\cos(2\pi f_n t + \phi_n), each fading at its own rate, and the sound reaching you keeps those frequencies and fading times, with amounts changed by the bridge and the body (post 3).

Frequencies: off the lines. They are the spectrum above: 1, 6.27 and 17.55 times the fundamental. Higher modes bend in shorter humps, and for a tine shorter humps push back much harder, so the frequencies grow like the square of the mode number instead of the number itself.

Amounts: the click. You might guess the overtone is too quiet to hear. It isn’t. In how far the tine swings, the overtone is tiny, 2.5% of the fundamental; but the body hears the push on the bridge, not the swing, and in that push the overtone starts strong, about 55% of the fundamental in the sideways force (fold below). That strong start is the click at the beginning of each note.

Step-by-step derivationHow strongly a pluck at the tip excites each mode uses integration by parts

Chapman measured the tines of one kalimba, a Hugh Tracey pentatonic, and found one dominant overtone at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, near the ideal 6.27, dying away quickly after the attack. A real tine is clamped a little behind the bridge and rests on the bridge partway along, rather than being perfectly clamped at one point, and he found that the measured ratios depend on how the bridge divides the tine’s length, which is one reason they spread. The steps below compute, for the ideal tine, how much of each mode a pluck starts. In short: in displacement, the second mode starts at only 2.5% of the first; but at the clamp, where in this ideal model the tine presses on the bridge, it starts at about 16% of the first in bending moment and 55% in shear force. Those are two different measures of the push on the bridge, not a range of uncertainty.

  1. The pluck shape. Before release, a push F at the tip holds the tine still in a bent shape u_0, so the bar’s restoring force balances the push.
  2. Split it into modes. Write u_0 = \sum_n a_n X_n, with each mode scaled so that \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L. Inside the tine nothing pushes, so EI\,u_0'''' = 0 there; the push enters only at the tip, as the shear force there. Now compute \int_0^L EI\,u_0\,X_n''''\,dx in two ways. First, EI\,X_n'''' = \rho S\,\omega_n^2X_n (the mode equation, with \omega_n = 2\pi f_n), and the modes are perpendicular, \int_0^L X_mX_n\,dx = 0 for m \ne n, so only the a_n term survives: the integral is \rho S L\,\omega_n^2\,a_n. Second, integrate by parts four times to move the derivatives onto u_0. Inside, EI\,u_0'''' = 0; at the clamp both u_0 and X_n vanish with their slopes; at the tip X_n'' = X_n''' = 0 and u_0'' = 0. The one term left is the tip’s shear force times X_n(L), which is F\,X_n(L). So \rho S L\,\omega_n^2\,a_n = F\,X_n(L): mode n behaves like a spring of stiffness proportional to \omega_n^2, pushed with a force proportional to its value at the tip, and it gives way by force over stiffness.
  3. Tip values. Use the modes in the form of the fold above, with \sigma_n about 0.734 and 1.018 for the first two modes. Written this way, each mode already has \int_0^L X_n^2\,dx = L, and its tip value is \pm2 for every n (a known property of clamped–free modes, easily confirmed numerically). So a_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2, and a_2/a_1 = 1/6.27^2 \approx 2.5\%.
  4. Forces. In this model the tine is clamped right at the bridge, so the forces at the clamp are the forces on the bridge. The bending moment at the clamp is EI\,u_{xx} there, and the shear force EI\,u_{xxx} there (up to sign). From the form above, X_n''(0) = 2\beta_n^2 and X_n'''(0) = -2\sigma_n\beta_n^3. Multiplying by a_n \propto 1/\omega_n^2 \propto 1/\beta_n^4, the second mode’s moment at the clamp is (\beta_1/\beta_2)^2 = (1.875/4.694)^2 \approx 16\% of the first’s, and its shear force (\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(\beta_1/\beta_2) \approx 1.39 \times 0.40 \approx 55\%.

Fading: the overtones go first. What keeps the note clean is that the overtone dies within a fraction of a second, while the fundamental rings on. That fast decay is observed, not derived: the model has no damping, and this page takes the decay from measurement. The acoustician D. M. F. Chapman found exactly this pattern on a real kalimba: one dominant overtone, at 5.9 to 6.8 times the fundamental, which dies away so fast that it is heard only at the instant the note starts. A kalimba has no dampers; a note rings until it fades, unless a finger stops the tine.

The demo’s sound is made exactly this way: it adds up the first five modes at the frequencies of the spectrum, each starting with its shear-force strength from the fold above (55% for the second mode). The fundamental fades over a couple of seconds and the overtones within a small fraction of that; those fading times are an illustrative choice consistent with Chapman’s observation, not computed. Tick the box and every mode fades as slowly as the fundamental.

What we hear

A click, then a clean note. The off-line overtones start strong, which is the click, and die within a fraction of a second, while the fundamental rings on. The fundamental left on its own is a single partial, and a sound with one partial has a clear pitch, at its frequency. If every overtone rang as long as the fundamental, the same spectrum would clang; the fading decides. With the box ticked, the off-line overtones keep ringing, and the same tine clangs like a bell.

Half the length, two octaves up. The spectrum says f_n = \kappa(\beta_nL)^2/(2\pi L^2), and the numbers \beta_nL do not depend on the length: so a tine’s frequency goes as one over its length squared. Half the length gives 4 times the frequency, two octaves, where a string, with f \propto 1/L, rises only an octave. That is why the low tines in the demo are not twice as long as the high ones an octave up. Kalimba makers tune a tine by sliding it over the bridge to change its free length (Kalimba Magic), and the 1/L^2 makes a small slide go a long way.

The four aspects

The kalimba’s four aspects.
Aspect Kalimba
Pitch the fundamental, f_1 \propto 1/L^2, set by the tine’s free length beyond the bridge
Loudness how far the tine is pushed before it is let go, since every amount grows with it
Timbre the click: off-line overtones that start strong and die at once; then the fundamental alone
Duration how long the fundamental takes to fade, or until a finger stops the tine

What we found

A tine is a bar clamped at one end, A = \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, and its spectrum puts the overtones at 6.27 and 17.55 times the lowest, off the whole-number multiples. The pluck starts the first overtone strongly, the click; the fading, observed on real kalimbas rather than derived, removes it within a fraction of a second and leaves the fundamental alone: a clean note.
