The question. For a particular instrument, what decides how it sounds: where its partials can sit, how it is played, or how its sound fades?

The series in one paragraph

In one sentence. An instrument’s sound is set by its spectrum (where partials can sit), by the playing (which modes start, and how strongly) and by the fading; for bowed and blown instruments, by a periodic motion shaped by the resonator’s spectrum. Each page shows which of these decides that instrument.

Three things that decide a sound

Each page asks which of three things decides how an instrument sounds: its spectrum, the frequencies its operator allows, which says where partials can sit; the playing, which says which modes start and how strongly; and the fading, which includes the player’s damping; a bowed or blown instrument instead runs one repeating cycle, whose partials sit on whole-number multiples and are shaped by its resonator’s modes (the series in one paragraph).

The orchestra

Musicologists sort instruments into five classes by what makes the sound, the Hornbostel–Sachs classes: strings, air, solid bodies, stretched skins and electric circuits. Here is one of each, playing Grieg.

There are no recordings: every note is built from pure tones. The struck notes fade, at frequencies computed from each instrument's operator (the bass drum's from the ideal drumhead of the drum page), except the kettledrum's, which are measured, because the drum page's simple model gets only halfway to them; the fading times are illustrative. The clarinet holds a steady tone with the tube's odd multiples strong and the even ones faint, and the theremin a single steady pure tone. Listen for the three deciders. The theremin’s circuit has a single mode, so it sounds one pure tone. The piano’s partials sit a little sharp of the lines, set by its spectrum. The clarinet holds one repeating cycle, its strong partials picked by the tube’s modes. The glockenspiel’s overtones sit off the lines but, as played here, fade fast, so the fading leaves a clean note. And the two drums share one skin: on the bass drum its modes fall off the lines and it booms; on the kettledrum the air around the head, the strike point and the fading leave one set of modes near the lines, and it plays a note (the drum page).

The instruments, one page each

Each page opens with its puzzle and a demo, then answers in one sentence:

  1. 4aWhy does a light touch make a string jump an octave?A light touch at the middle of a string moves none of its frequencies: it damps every mode that moves under the finger, and the modes left ringing, the even ones, which stand still there, are the partials of a note an octave up.
  2. 4bWhy is a clarinet almost an octave deeper than a flute?A tube closed at one end has its modes only at odd multiples of its lowest frequency, which is half that of an open tube of the same length, so a clarinet sounds almost an octave below a flute of the same length and overblows an octave and a fifth higher; blown, its tone is one repeating cycle with strong odd partials and weak even ones.
  3. 4cWhy does a kalimba sound clean?A tine's overtones sit off the whole-number multiples of its fundamental (6.27×, 17.55×), yet a kalimba sounds clean because those overtones die within a fraction of a second (observed, not derived) and the fundamental rings on alone.
  4. 4dWhere did the harmonica reed's overtones go?Blown, the reed swings in one repeating cycle just below its own lowest frequency and chops the air into identical puffs, so the steady sound has partials only at whole-number multiples, and the reed's off-line overtones are absent from it.
  5. 4eWhy do piano tuners stretch octaves?A piano wire's stiffness puts each overtone slightly above its whole-number multiple, so tuners stretch octaves to reduce the beating, a slow wobble in loudness, most at the ends of the keyboard.
  6. 4fWhy does a drum thud?An ideal drumhead's frequencies (1, 1.59, 2.14, … times the lowest) fit no pitch, so a plain drum thuds; a kettledrum plays a note because the air the head pushes moves one set of modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5, the strike point favours them, and the other modes fade fast.

The sounds in this list are simplified: the octave pair exaggerates the string’s stiffness, and the clarinet-like and flute-like tones are plain sums of whole-number multiples. Each page has the full demo.

The recipe on every instrument

Each card runs one instrument through the chain the series follows: Newton’s law on a small piece gives the equation of motion (post 2); the operator is its rule, together with how the object is held; its spectrum is the list of frequencies that rule allows (post 3); on the diagram draws them as stems on the pitch ruler, one stem per mode, called the spectrum diagram; and what you hear says which of the three deciders matters. The stems show the spectrum as multiples of the lowest frequency; the button plays a sound made from it.

A violin string

plucked a fifth of the way along
DetailsNewton’s law, the operator and its spectrum
  1. Newton’s law on a short piece of string under tension T, where for small slopes the upward part of each end’s pull is tension times slope: u_{tt} = c^2\,u_{xx}, with c^2 = T/\mu.
  2. The operator: A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, with both ends tied, u(0) = u(L) = 0.
  3. Its spectrum: X = \sin kx with \sin kL = 0, so k = n\pi/L, \lambda_n = (n\pi c/L)^2 and f_n = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}.
  1. On the diagram: every stem on a line; plucked a fifth of the way along, the 5th is missing.
  2. What you hear: a clear note at f_1. The pluck point sets the brightness; a light touch at the middle damps the modes that move there and leaves the even ones, a note an octave up: the fading decides.

A flute and a clarinet

the tubes’ modes; blown, the clarinet’s sound also has faint even multiples
DetailsNewton’s law, the operator and its spectrum
  1. Newton’s law on a thin slab of air, pushed by a pressure difference, \rho\,\xi_{tt} = -p_x, with squeezed air pushing back, p = -K\xi_x, gives p_{tt} = c^2\,p_{xx}, with c^2 = K/\rho, for the pressure p.
  2. The operator: A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, on pressure shapes. Flute, open at both ends: p(0) = p(L) = 0. Clarinet, closed at the reed: p_x(0) = 0, p(L) = 0.
  3. Its spectrum: flute, \sin kL = 0, so f_n = nc/2L; clarinet, \cos kL = 0, so f_n = (2n-1)c/4L.
  1. On the diagram: the flute’s stems on every line; the clarinet’s only at odd multiples of its lowest frequency, 1 : 3 : 5 : \cdots.
  2. What you hear: blown, one repeating cycle, so partials on every line; the clarinet’s note is almost an octave lower for the same length, with strong odd partials picked by the tube’s modes.

A kalimba tine

the overtones fade almost at once
DetailsNewton’s law, the operator and its spectrum
  1. Newton’s law on a thin slice of steel, where bending stretches one side and squeezes the other, giving a bending moment M = EI\,u_{xx} and a shear force V = -M_x: \rho S\,u_{tt} = -EI\,u_{xxxx}.
  2. The operator: A = \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, with \kappa^2 = EI/\rho S; clamped at the bridge (u = u_x = 0), free at the tip (u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0).
  3. Its spectrum: \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1, with \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4, so f_n = \kappa\beta_n^2/2\pi with \beta_nL = 1.875, 4.694, 7.855, \dots.
  1. On the diagram: stems at 1, 6.27 and 17.55 times the lowest, off the lines.
  2. What you hear: a brief click, then a clean note: the overtones die within a fraction of a second (observed, not derived), so the fading decides.

A harmonica reed

same reed, two ways of playing
DetailsNewton’s law, the operator and its spectrum
  1. Newton’s law: the kalimba tine’s, for a small brass bar riveted at one end.
  2. The operator: the tine’s, with \kappa^2 = Eh^2/12\rho for thickness h.
  3. Its spectrum: the same ratios, 1 : 6.27 : 17.55, with f_1 = \frac{(1.8751)^2}{2\pi}\,\frac{h}{L^2}\sqrt{\frac{E}{12\rho}}.
  1. On the diagram: plucked, off the lines like the tine; blown, every stem on a line.
  2. What you hear: plucked, this spectrum; blown, the reed chops the airflow into puffs that repeat exactly, and the sound sits at whole-number multiples of the puff rate: the periodic motion decides.

A piano wire

stiffness exaggerated (B = 0.0012)
DetailsNewton’s law, the operator and its spectrum
  1. Newton’s law on a short piece of stiff wire, where tension pulls it straight and the steel resists bending, the two forces adding: u_{tt} = c^2\,u_{xx} - \kappa^2\,u_{xxxx}, with c^2 = T/\mu, \kappa^2 = EI/\mu.
  2. The operator: A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 + \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, with the ends pinned, u = u_{xx} = 0.
  3. Its spectrum: X = \sin kx with \sin kL = 0 and \lambda = c^2k^2 + \kappa^2k^4, so f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, with f_0 = c/2L and B = \pi^2 EI/TL^2.
  1. On the diagram: each stem a little sharp of its line, more so the higher it is.
  2. What you hear: still a clear note, but an octave tuned to exactly 2 : 1 beats, so tuners stretch octaves to reduce the beating: the spectrum decides.

A drum

struck about a third of the way out
DetailsNewton’s law, the operator and its spectrum
  1. Newton’s law on a small patch of drumhead, where tension T pulls all around the patch’s edge and the divergence theorem turns the total pull into T\,\Delta u per unit area: \sigma\,u_{tt} = T\,\Delta u, with \Delta u = u_{xx} + u_{yy}, the Laplacian.
  2. The operator: A = -c^2\,\Delta, with c^2 = T/\sigma, and the rim held fixed, u = 0.
  3. Its spectrum: for a round drum of radius a, J_m(ka) = 0 (Bessel functions), with \lambda = c^2k^2, so f_{m,n} = c\,j_{m,n}/2\pi a.
  1. On the diagram: stems at 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, 2.65, 2.92, … times the lowest, scattered between the lines.
  2. What you hear: a sound that doesn’t repeat at any rate we hear as pitch, and that fades fast: a thud. A kettledrum gets its note from all three deciders at once.

What we found

Played side by side, the instruments split by which of the three deciders answers their puzzle. The spectrum decides the piano’s stretched octaves and the drum’s thud; the fading decides the kalimba’s clean note and the violin’s touched harmonics; on the kettledrum all three work together; and on the bowed string, the clarinet and the blown harmonica a periodic motion, shaped by the resonator’s modes, puts every partial on a line.
