A drumhead is a sheet pulled tight: a string with a second dimension. Pluck a string and you get a note you could hum. Strike a drum and you get a thud. What changes when the string becomes a sheet? And why do some drums, the timpani (kettledrums), play a note after all?

Listen first: tap the head anywhere, then tap dead center and compare.

1.00
Tap the head anywhere. The ring marks where we listen.

The motion is the sum of the 40 lowest modes, slowed about 300 times. Dead center sounds only the round modes; a strike on a mode's still line leaves that mode silent.

In one sentence. An ideal drumhead’s frequencies (1, 1.59, 2.14, … times the lowest) fit no pitch, so a plain drum thuds; a kettledrum plays a note because the air the head pushes moves one set of modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5, the strike point favours them, and the other modes fade fast.

The operator, in one line

Let u(x, y, t) be the height of the head above its rest position at the point (x, y) at time t. The head has mass \sigma per unit area, and its tension T is a force per unit length of any cut through it. Newton’s law on a small patch of the head, with the tension pulling all around the patch’s edge, gives \sigma\,u_{tt} = T\,\Delta u, \qquad \Delta u = u_{xx} + u_{yy}, where \Delta u, the Laplacian, adds the second derivatives in the two directions across the sheet (post 2 derives it). In words: on a string, tension pulls each point toward the average height of its two neighbors; on a sheet it pulls each point toward the average height of all the points around it, and \Delta u measures how far a point sits below that average.

The rim completes the operator: it is clamped, so u = 0 there.

The drum’s operator. u_{tt} = -A\,u, \qquad A = -c^2\,\Delta, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\sigma}, acting on shapes of the head that are zero on the rim. In words: tension pulls each point toward the average of its surroundings, and the rim holds still.

DetailsWhat the model leaves out

The model assumes small slopes, a uniform tension that does not change as the head moves, no bending stiffness, no air, and no damping or radiation, so its modes never fade; a real head loses its energy to the air quickly, and the air matters, as the two drums further down show.

Its spectrum

A mode is a shape X that the operator only rescales: A X = \lambda X, with the number \lambda called its eigenvalue. Released in that shape, the head keeps it and swings at the frequency f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi. The list of all the eigenvalues is the operator’s spectrum. Writing \lambda = c^2k^2, the mode condition is -\Delta X = k^2 X inside the drum, with X = 0 on the rim. For a round drum of radius a, it can be solved by splitting it into a problem around the center and a problem outward from it, a separation of variables.

For a round drum of radius a, write the shape as a function of the distance r from the center times a function of the angle \theta (first fold below). Going around the center, the shape must come back to itself after a turn, which allows only \cos m\theta and \sin m\theta with m a whole number: m straight still lines through the center, like spokes. Outward from the center, the shape is a Bessel function J_m(kr), which plays the part the sine plays on a string: it wiggles like a cosine whose swings slowly shrink (second fold, and the figure).

Step-by-step derivationSeparating the mode equation in polar coordinates uses the chain rule

Distance and angle. In the coordinates r and \theta the Laplacian reads \Delta X = X_{rr} + \frac1r X_r + \frac{1}{r^2}X_{\theta\theta}. This is a standard change of variables, quoted here; it follows from the chain rule (Laplace operator in polar coordinates).

The split. Substituting X = R(r)\,\Theta(\theta) into -\Delta X = k^2X and multiplying by r^2/(R\Theta) gives \frac{r^2R'' + rR' + k^2r^2R}{R} = -\frac{\Theta''}{\Theta}. The left side depends only on r, the right only on \theta. Change \theta alone: the left side can’t change, so neither can the right. So both equal one constant. The around part and the outward part are solved in the next fold.

PreciselyAround the center a loop, outward Bessel’s equation uses derivatives

2. Around the center: a loop. Going once around the drum must bring \Theta back to itself, so -\Theta'' = m^2\Theta with \Theta repeating every turn: the eigenvalue problem of a loop of string (post 3). So m is a whole number and \Theta = \cos m\theta or \sin m\theta. A mode with m \ge 1 has m straight still lines through the center, like spokes.

3. Outward from the center: Bessel’s equation. What remains for R is r^2R'' + rR' + (k^2r^2 - m^2)R = 0, Bessel’s equation. It depends on r only through kr, so its solutions are functions of kr. Of its two essentially different solutions, one, the Bessel function J_m(kr), is finite at the center; the other becomes infinite there, which would take infinite energy, so it is not a free mode. J_m plays the part the sine played on a string: it wiggles like a cosine or sine whose swings slowly shrink.

The rim picks out the frequencies. The rim condition R(a) = 0 says J_m(ka) = 0: ka must be a zero of J_m. Writing j_{m,n} for the n-th positive zero, k = j_{m,n}/a. The zeros have no closed form and are computed numerically; those of J_0 are 2.405, 5.520, 8.654, …, in the ratios 1 : 2.30 : 3.60.

The round drum’s spectrum. Modes J_m(j_{m,n}\,r/a)\cos m\theta and J_m(j_{m,n}\,r/a)\sin m\theta, for m = 0, 1, 2, \dots and n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, with eigenvalues \lambda_{m,n} = c^2 j_{m,n}^2/a^2 and frequencies f_{m,n} = \frac{c\,j_{m,n}}{2\pi a}. In order, relative to the lowest: 1, 1.593, 2.136, 2.295, 2.653, 2.917, … Each frequency with m \ge 1 belongs to two modes, the cosine and the sine, the same pattern turned.

DetailsWhy these are all the modes, why the pairs are exact, and how the zeros are spaced

These are all the modes: expanding any mode in a Fourier series in \theta reduces -\Delta X = k^2X, term by term, to Bessel’s equation for each m.

Each frequency with m \ge 1 belongs to exactly two modes because Bessel functions of different whole-number orders never share a positive zero; this was conjectured by Bourget and proved by Carl Ludwig Siegel in 1929. This page computes the zeros numerically. For large n they approach j_{m,n} \approx (n + \tfrac m2 - \tfrac14)\pi: evenly spaced for one fixed m, but shifted by an amount that depends on m. The round head’s symmetry shows up as a multiplicity of 2.

On the spectrum diagram, the pitch ruler of post 1 with one stem per mode, each stem is one j_{m,n}/j_{0,1} from your last strike. After 1×, the next few sit between the lines. Higher up the stems crowd together, so some land near a line by chance, such as 2.92× and 4.06×; but no base frequency in the range we hear as pitch puts them all on or near lines. Each stem’s height is how strongly that mode sounds at the listening ring for the strike you made: strike somewhere else and the heights change, never the positions. Tap a stem to see that mode on the drumhead and hear it alone.

Struck: a ringing drumhead

A struck head rings: it moves as the sum of its modes, u = \sum a_{m,n} X_{m,n}\,e^{-t/\tau_{m,n}} \cos(2\pi f_{m,n} t + \phi_{m,n}), each with its own fading time \tau_{m,n}, so its sound is a sum of partials at the frequencies of the spectrum. The demo at the top is made this way, with illustrative fading times.

Frequencies: between the lines. They are the zeros of the Bessel functions, scaled: 1×, 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×, 2.65×, 2.92×, …, scattered between the lines (fold below).

DetailsWhy the zeros miss the lines

The frequencies are the zeros of the Bessel functions, scaled. For the modes with no spokes, the zeros of J_0 come at nearly even steps, about \pi apart, but the first one is short, about 0.77\pi rather than \pi, so the later zeros are not multiples of the first: the frequencies go 1 : 2.30 : 3.60. Each number of spokes gives its own sequence, shifted by a different amount, and the sequences interleave at unrelated places. Merged, they give the drum’s list 1×, 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×, 2.65×, 2.92×, …, scattered between the lines.

Amounts: where you strike. A sharp strike starts each mode in proportion to how far that mode moves at the point struck. Every mode with spokes stays still at the center, so a strike at dead center sounds only the round modes, those with no spokes; a strike on one of a mode’s still lines leaves that mode silent.

DetailsHow strongly a strike excites each mode, in this model uses integrals over an area

Number the modes X_1, X_2, \dots in order of frequency. A sharp strike, a brief push at a point p, gives mode i a starting speed in proportion to X_i(p), the mode’s height at the point struck, the same rule as the piano’s hammer. We model what you hear as the speed of the head at a listening point q, which picks up mode i in proportion to X_i(q). Dividing by \iint X_i^2\,dA, the integral of the mode’s square over the head, removes the arbitrary size of each shape. So mode i sounds with weight X_i(p)\,X_i(q)\big/\iint X_i^2\,dA. (A real drum radiates sound from its whole head, and some modes radiate better than others; this model ignores that.)

A mode’s still lines can be seen with sand. Sprinkle it on a vibrating sheet, and it is jostled wherever the sheet moves and comes to rest where it doesn’t, so each pattern is one mode’s still lines, the places where a strike would leave that mode silent. Ernst Chladni showed this on metal plates in the 1780s; here is the same trick on a drumhead: pick a frequency and watch where the sand goes.

Each button drives the drumhead at one of its own frequencies, given as a multiple of the lowest. Every grain is jostled in proportion to how much the head moves under it, so grains drift until they reach a place where the head stays still. Chladni used stiff metal plates, whose operator involves bending, not tension; the sand behaves the same way on both.

PreciselyWhere the sand collects, and the history uses polar coordinates

The mode J_m(kr)\cos m\theta with k = j_{m,n}/a is zero where \cos m\theta = 0, at 2m angles, which pair up into m lines through the center, and along the circles where J_m(kr) = 0 inside the rim, n - 1 of them for the n-th zero. Those are exactly the lines where the sand collects.

In 1808 Chladni showed the French Institute in Paris, the body that then held the Academy of Sciences, his sand patterns on plates bowed along their edges. Napoleon offered a prize for explaining them. Sophie Germain won it in 1816, the first woman to receive the Paris Academy’s grand prize in mathematics, with a theory that was still incomplete; Gustav Kirchhoff gave the full theory of vibrating plates, with the correct edge conditions, in 1850. Finding the still lines and finding the frequencies are one problem: on a drumhead, the one on this page; on a plate, the same problem with the plate’s own operator.

Fading, and muffling. A real head gives its energy to the air quickly, so the sound fades fast; the round modes, which pump air like a piston, go first. How long a bass drum rings is also the player’s choice: players muffle the head with a leg or a towel when the music calls for short notes, and let it ring for long ones (Aleo, Interlochen 2023).

One drumhead, two drums

A bass drum and a kettledrum (one of the timpani) share this operator, yet one booms and the other plays a note. Step through the three things that separate them: where the head is struck, what surrounds it, and which modes fade first. On the kettledrum, the air the head must push pulls the spoke modes, those with one or more lines through the center and no still circle inside the rim, close to 2, 3, 4 and 5 times a common base (HyperPhysics; Fletcher and Rossing), and the strike point and the fast fading of the round modes leave mostly those.

DetailsThe toy model, the kettle, and other drums uses integrals over an area

Where each drum is struck. A concert bass drum is played slightly off center; center playing, punchier and with less sustain, is kept for rhythmic passages (Graham 2017; Aleo, Interlochen 2023). There the spoke modes move little, so among the lowest modes the round ones dominate. Players muffle the head with a leg or a towel when the music calls for short notes, and let it ring for long ones (Aleo, Interlochen 2023); the step-through’s Muffle button does the first. Timpanists strike between halfway and three-quarters of the way out (HyperPhysics), roughly a third of the way in from the rim (Graham 2017). With the weight |X(p)|\big/\big(\iint X^2\,dA\big)^{1/2} for a strike at p, on a head of radius 1, the lowest round mode (0,1) has 1.09 at the center, 0.73 at halfway and 0.37 at three-quarters, while the spoke modes (1,1) to (4,1) have between 0.74 and 1.19 anywhere from halfway to three-quarters: lower than the spoke modes’ across the whole range. Nearer halfway the stick also sits close to the inner still circles of the modes (0,2), (1,2), (2,2) and (3,2), which lie between 0.44 and 0.65 of the radius, which quiets those too. The clear note also relies on the remaining modes being weaker or fading faster, which this page takes from measurements (Fletcher and Rossing, chapter 18) rather than deriving.

The air’s mass. Treat the air on both sides of the head as not squeezable, and each mode, locally, as a ripple of wavenumber k = j_{m,n}/a, whose crests are about \pi/k apart. The air such a ripple sets moving reaches about 1/k from the head on each side, which adds a mass of about 2\rho/k per unit area (\rho \approx 1.2 kg/m³ for air) to the head’s own \sigma: \omega^2 = \frac{T k^2}{\sigma + 2\rho/k}, \qquad f \propto \frac{k}{\sqrt{\sigma + 2\rho/k}} , so coarse shapes, with small k, slow down most. This is added mass: it can only lower frequencies. A typical Mylar kettledrum head has \sigma \approx 0.26 kg/m², on the two drums Packer studied, radii 0.32 and 0.355 m (Packer 1993, appendix B); with a = 0.33 m, 2\rho a/\sigma \approx 3.0. The spoke modes (1,1) to (4,1) move from 1 : 1.34 : 1.67 : 1.98 to 1 : 1.42 : 1.83 : 2.24, halfway to 1.5 : 2 : 2.5. The rest of the shift comes from a full calculation of the air inside and around a finite head (Christian, Davis, Tubis, Anderson, Mills and Rossing 1984), not from the kettle’s air spring, its uniform pressure.

The kettle. The nearly sealed kettle (it has a small vent) acts as a spring on the volume the head sweeps, V = \iint u\,dA, adding \tfrac12\eta V^2 to the stored energy for some constant \eta > 0. The operator gains the term (\eta/\sigma)\,\mathbf 1\langle\mathbf 1, \cdot\rangle, where \mathbf 1 is the constant shape and \sigma the head’s mass per unit area: a term of rank one. Every mode with m \ge 1 has \iint X\,dA = 0, because \int_0^{2\pi}\cos m\theta\,d\theta = 0, so \big(A + (\eta/\sigma)\,\mathbf 1\langle\mathbf 1, \cdot\rangle\big)X = AX, and in this model it keeps its frequency exactly. Only the round modes are raised, and since the new term couples them to each other, their shapes change too; the explorable treats each on its own. In general, added stiffness never lowers a frequency and added mass never raises one, by the min–max principle.

The bass drum’s second head. A bass drum has a head on each end of its shell, coupled through the air inside, which splits its two lowest modes, (0,1) and (1,1); the two heads’ lowest modes are commonly tuned about a fourth apart (HyperPhysics). Not modelled here.

Other drums. Toms are tuned to rough pitches, and a snare drum’s crack comes from the wires, the snares, stretched across its lower head. The tabla and the mridangam reach near-whole-number overtones a different way: a patch of paste loads the center of the head, which makes the head’s density non-uniform, outside this page’s model (Raman and Kumar 1920; Raman 1934).

What we hear

Off the lines and fading fast: a thud. An ideal head’s frequencies don’t sit at whole-number multiples of any base frequency we hear as pitch, so its sound doesn’t repeat at any such rate; and a real head gives its energy to the air quickly, so it fades fast. Together, a thud: the spectrum decides; where you strike changes only how loud each mode is.

A kettledrum’s note. On a kettledrum all three deciders point the same way. The air the head drags along moves the spoke modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5 times a base (the spectrum); the stick, between halfway and three-quarters of the way out, starts the lowest round mode more weakly than the spoke modes (the playing); and the round modes radiate their energy away within a fraction of a second (the fading). What is left nearly repeats: a note. Players and acousticians name it at the lowest of those modes, the (1,1) mode, the drum’s “principal tone” (Vienna’s Institute of Musical Acoustics).

PreciselyWhich octave the ear names

The modes above the (1,1) sit a fifth, an octave and a major tenth (ratio 5 : 2) above it. The pitch rule of post 1 would put the pitch an octave lower, at the base, and HyperPhysics notes that the missing-fundamental effect might give the drum that lower pitch; which octave the ear names is one of the exceptions post 1 lists, and this series doesn’t settle it.

What we found

An ideal drumhead, A = -c^2\Delta with the rim held still, has its frequencies at the zeros of Bessel functions, off the lines, so a plain drum thuds: the spectrum decides. A kettledrum is the same head in different surroundings, where the spectrum, the playing and the fading work together: the air pulls its spoke modes near 2 : 3 : 4 : 5, and the strike point and the fast-fading round modes leave mostly those, a note.
