Piano tuners don’t tune octaves to an exact frequency ratio of 2 : 1. They stretch them slightly, so the top of a piano ends up sharp and the bottom flat, compared with equal temperament, the tuning in which all twelve semitones are the same ratio. An octave tuned to exactly 2 : 1 on a piano wobbles. Why would exact sound wrong?

Listen first: an octave tuned to exactly 2 : 1, then a stretched one. The slow wobble in loudness is called a beat.

6.0

Until you tick "Play every partial", each note plays only its lowest partials (C4: 1 to 3, C5: 1 and 2), so the one beat a tuner listens for stands out and the stretched octave is steady. Stiffness starts at 6 times a real middle C wire's, so the wobble is quick enough to hear; at 1 it is a slow swell every few seconds.

In one sentence. A piano wire’s stiffness puts each overtone slightly above its whole-number multiple, so tuners stretch octaves to reduce the beating, a slow wobble in loudness, most at the ends of the keyboard.

The operator, in one line

Let u(x, t) be the height of a piano wire above its rest line. Two forces act on each short piece of it, and they add: tension pulls it like a violin string, and stiff steel pushes back against bending like a kalimba tine. Newton’s law on a short piece (post 2 derives it) gives u_{tt} = c^2\,u_{xx} - \kappa^2\,u_{xxxx}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{T}{\mu}, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\mu}, with T the tension, \mu the mass per unit length, E steel’s Young’s modulus and I = \pi d^4/64 the second moment of area of a round wire of diameter d; subscripts are partial derivatives. The ends complete the operator. The bridge at one end and a metal bearing point at the other hold the wire in place, so u = 0 there; if they let it pivot freely they exert no bending moment, which reads u_{xx} = 0. These are called pinned ends; real piano strings are held a little more firmly, which shifts the numbers slightly but not the picture.

The piano wire’s operator. The equation of motion is u_{tt} = -A\,u, with A = -c^2\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + \kappa^2\frac{d^4}{dx^4}, acting on shapes with u = u_{xx} = 0 at both ends. The first term is the string’s operator, from tension; the second is the kalimba’s, from bending: operators add. In words: the force per unit mass on each point is the string’s pull toward the inside of the curve plus the bar’s push back against bending.

DetailsWhat the model leaves out

The model assumes a plain steel wire (bass strings are wound with copper, which adds mass but little stiffness), small slopes, a uniform wire and no damping; the fading is added back below.

Its spectrum

A mode is a shape X that the operator only rescales: A X = \lambda X, with the number \lambda called its eigenvalue. Released in that shape, the wire keeps it and swings at the frequency f = \sqrt{\lambda}/2\pi. The list of all the eigenvalues is the operator’s spectrum. Each sine \sin(n\pi x/L) satisfies the pinned ends, and both terms of A only rescale it, by c^2k^2 and \kappa^2k^4 with k = n\pi/L; so the sines are the modes and the two eigenvalues add (fold below).

PreciselyThe computation, step by step uses derivatives and square roots

1. Try a sine, which already satisfies the pinned ends. Take X = \sin(kx) with k = n\pi/L, where L is the wire’s length and n = 1, 2, 3, \dots It is zero at both ends, and so is X'' = -k^2X: both pinned conditions hold.

2. Apply the operator: each term rescales the same sine. Two derivatives give X'' = -k^2X and four give X'''' = k^4X, so A X = \big(c^2k^2 + \kappa^2k^4\big)\,X . The sine is rescaled, not reshaped: it is an eigenfunction, with eigenvalue \lambda_n = c^2k^2 + \kappa^2k^4. The string’s eigenvalue and the bar’s simply add, because with pinned ends the two operators share their eigenfunctions. There are no other modes: the sines already form a complete set (post 3), and since the operator is symmetric, an eigenfunction for any other eigenvalue would be perpendicular to every sine, hence zero.

3. The frequencies: the string’s, nudged up by stiffness. Factoring out the string’s part, \lambda_n = c^2k^2\big(1 + (EI/T)\,k^2\big), and with k = n\pi/L, taking the square root and dividing by 2\pi gives the result.

The piano wire’s spectrum. Modes \sin(n\pi x/L), n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, with frequencies f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, \qquad B = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{T L^2}. Here f_0 = c/2L is the lowest frequency the same wire would have without stiffness; the actual fundamental is f_1 = f_0\sqrt{1 + B}. Each partial sits at f_n/f_1 = n\sqrt{(1 + Bn^2)/(1 + B)} times the fundamental: a little above n, more so the larger n.

The stiffness constant B is small, but it multiplies n^2, so the fundamental barely moves while high partials run noticeably sharp: the string is slightly inharmonic.

For a middle C wire B is roughly 4.4\times10^{-4}, which puts the 10th partial about 37 cents, a third of a semitone, sharp of ten times the fundamental (fold below). Since I grows like the fourth power of the diameter and B falls like 1/L^2, short treble strings, thick for their length, are the stiffest.

DetailsHow big is B? A middle C wire uses logarithms

For a middle C wire, roughly d = 1 mm and L = 0.6 m, with steel’s E = 2\times10^{11} Pa and density 7850 kg/m³; a tension of about 610 N then gives middle C. So I \approx 4.9\times10^{-14} m^4 and B \approx 4.4\times10^{-4}. The 10th partial (the fundamental counts as the 1st), at 10f_0\sqrt{1 + 100B}, then sits 1200\log_2\sqrt{(1 + 100B)/(1 + B)} \approx 37 cents sharp of ten times the fundamental, about a third of a semitone. These numbers are rough; real strings vary.

Struck: a ringing string

A struck wire rings: it moves as the sum of its modes, u = \sum_n a_n\,e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(2\pi f_n t + \phi_n), and the sound passed on by the soundboard keeps those frequencies and fading times (post 3). The demos are made this way.

Frequencies: just sharp of the lines, as the spectrum says; the low partials sit close enough for one clear note.

Amounts: the hammer. A hammer blow starts each mode in proportion to the mode’s own height where the hammer hits, so a mode that stands still there stays silent. In the demos the hammer strikes an eighth of the way along, where the 8th and 16th modes stand still, so those two are nearly silent. How hard the key is struck matters too, and not only for loudness: the hammer’s felt acts as a spring that gets stiffer the harder it is squashed, so a heavy blow acts like a hard spring and starts many more high partials, and loud notes sound brighter than soft ones (Russell; post 1 and post 2).

DetailsFading in two stages: the coupled strings

Fading: two stages. Each mode fades at its own rate, the high ones faster in the demos, whose fading times are illustrative. Most notes have two or three strings, and Weinreich showed that their coupling through the bridge, each string’s motion slightly shaking the others, produces the piano’s two-stage decay: a loud start that fades fast, then a quiet, long aftersound. Post 3 places that coupled pair among the equations with friction; the demos leave the coupling out.

The player’s damping: dampers and pedals. Letting go of a key drops a felt damper on its strings, which drains each mode in proportion to how much it moves under the felt; the pedals act on the dampers or on the blow. Every control a pianist touches changes how strongly the modes start or how fast they die; only the tuner’s wrench, which changes the tension, moves the frequencies.

On a grand piano the middle pedal is usually the sostenuto, which holds up only the dampers of keys already down.

Step-by-step derivationThe full string equation, the hammer weights and the damping rates uses integrals of products of modes and a first-order approximation

The operator is only part of the string’s equation. A real string is also pushed by the hammer and loses energy to damping; here f(x,t) is a force, not a frequency: \mu\,u_{tt} + b(x,t)\,u_t = T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx} + f(x,t). Each term has one job. The operator’s term, T\,u_{xx} - EI\,u_{xxxx} (that is, -\mu A u), fixes the frequencies and the mode shapes. The forcing f is the hammer, and it fixes how much of each mode is excited. The damping b is the felt dampers plus small internal losses, and it fixes how fast each mode dies. In the table above, the dampers and the sustain pedal change b; the soft pedal and the practice mute change f; only the tuning pin changes the operator.

1. Expand in modes. Write u(x,t) = \sum_n q_n(t)\,X_n(x), with X_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L), A X_n = \omega_n^2X_n and the pinned conditions.

2. Project. Substitute into the full equation, multiply by X_m and integrate along the string. The operator is symmetric with these boundary conditions (in fact self-adjoint), by integrating by parts, so the X_n are orthogonal: \int_0^L X_mX_n\,dx = 0 for m \ne n. The acceleration and operator terms collapse to one term each: \mu N_m\,(\ddot q_m + \omega_m^2 q_m) + \sum_n D_{mn}\,\dot q_n = F_m(t), with N_m = \int X_m^2\,dx, D_{mn} = \int b\,X_mX_n\,dx and F_m = \int f\,X_m\,dx.

3. The hammer. The felt touches a short stretch near x_h, so F_m(t) \approx X_m(x_h)\,P(t), where P(t) is the total force. Every mode gets the same push in time, weighted by X_m(x_h): the mode’s own height at the hammer.

4. The damper. If the damping is weak, the cross terms D_{mn} with m \ne n change the answer only at second order. Keeping the diagonal leaves a damped oscillator for each mode, \ddot q_m + 2\gamma_m\,\dot q_m + \omega_m^2 q_m = 0, \qquad \gamma_m = \frac{D_{mm}}{2\mu N_m}, whose solution e^{-\gamma_m t}\cos\big(\sqrt{\omega_m^2 - \gamma_m^2}\,t\big) decays at the rate \gamma_m while its frequency moves only by order \gamma_m^2/\omega_m. For a felt on a short stretch W of the string, \gamma_m = \int_W b\,X_m^2\,dx \big/ 2\mu\int_0^L X_m^2\,dx: a mode is damped in proportion to how much it moves under the felt, and a mode with a node under the felt barely notices the damper.

5. Honest limits. A real damper is not weak: it stops a note in a fraction of a second. The formula gives the right picture, that modes are damped by how much they move under the felt, but not exact decay times.

What we hear

Why exact octaves beat. A tuner sets an octave by ear, listening for beats between the lower note’s second partial and the upper note’s fundamental. Two nearly equal frequencies played together beat at a rate equal to their difference. The spectrum says where the lower note’s second partial sits: \frac{f_2}{f_1} = 2\sqrt{\frac{1 + 4B}{1 + B}} \approx 2\Big(1 + \frac{3B}{2}\Big), using \sqrt{1+x} \approx 1 + x/2 and 1/\sqrt{1+x} \approx 1 - x/2 for small x. So the second partial is more than twice the fundamental, by a factor of about 1 + 3B/2. An upper note tuned to exactly twice the fundamental sits just below it, and the two beat. Raising the upper note slows this beat and makes the octave slightly wider than 2 : 1. Tuners also listen to other pairs, such as the lower note’s fourth partial against the upper note’s second, and the upper note’s own partials run sharp too, so stiffness makes it impossible to silence all of the beats at once: real tuning is a compromise. Tick “Play every partial” in the demo at the top to hear it: the stretched octave then wobbles too. On a stiff string, “exactly 2 : 1” lines up the wrong pair of frequencies.

The stretch is largest at the ends of the keyboard. Repeated octave by octave across the keyboard, this is stretch tuning: compared with equal temperament, the top of a piano ends up sharp and the bottom flat. Two things make the stretch grow toward the ends. The small widenings add up, octave by octave, away from the middle; and the most inharmonic strings are at the two ends: the short treble strings, and the bass strings, which the size of the piano keeps short for their pitch. O. L. Railsback measured this stretch on pianos tuned by ear in the 1930s; the plot of how far each note sits from equal temperament is called the Railsback curve: close to equal temperament in the middle of the keyboard, flat in the bass, sharp in the treble.

Reader questionWhat does a damper do, and what would a piano without dampers sound like?

Without dampers, releasing a key would do nothing: every string would ring until it died on its own, exactly as if the sustain pedal were always down. On a real piano each key lifts a felt damper off its strings; letting go drops the damper back and stops the note within a fraction of a second. The sustain pedal lifts all the dampers at once, so released notes keep ringing. The keyboard above models both. The top 20 keys have no dampers at all, as on a real piano: those short strings die out quickly by themselves.

What we found

A piano wire is a string and a bar at once, A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 + \kappa^2\,d^4/dx^4, and its spectrum, f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + Bn^2}, puts each partial slightly above its whole-number multiple. So an exact 2 : 1 octave beats, and tuners stretch octaves, most at the ends of the keyboard: the spectrum decides. The hammer, the dampers and the pedals change how the modes start and die, not where they sit.
