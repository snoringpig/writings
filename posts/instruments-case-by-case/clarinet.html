A clarinet and a flute are about the same length. Yet the clarinet’s lowest note is almost an octave below the flute’s. And when players overblow, making the tube jump to its next mode, the flute jumps up an octave while the clarinet jumps an octave and a fifth. The same equation governs the air in both tubes. What is different?

Listen first: hold the blow button on the clarinet, then switch the same tube to a flute and blow again. The register key (on the flute it stands in for a faster, narrower airstream) makes either tube jump to its next mode.

Shading: how much the pressure swings, dark where it swings most, pale where it stays at room pressure. The first open hole acts, roughly, as the end of the tube.

In one sentence. A tube closed at one end has its modes only at odd multiples of its lowest frequency, which is half that of an open tube of the same length, so a clarinet sounds almost an octave below a flute of the same length and overblows an octave and a fifth higher; blown, its tone is one repeating cycle with strong odd partials and weak even ones.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

The operator, in one line

Let p(x, t) be the air pressure in the tube minus room pressure, at distance x along the tube, from the reed end, at time t. Newton’s law on a thin slab of air, pushed by the pressure difference across it, together with the way air pushes back when it is squeezed, gives the wave equation, the equation a stretched string also obeys (violin page), now for the pressure: p_{tt} = c^2p_{xx}, where c = \sqrt{K/\rho} is the speed of sound, about 343 m/s at 20 °C, K the air’s bulk modulus (its resistance to being squeezed) and \rho its density. Post 2 derives it step by step; subscripts are partial derivatives.

So the tube’s operator, the rule that turns a pressure shape into minus its rate of change twice over in time, takes minus c^2 times the curvature of the pressure shape; only the ends differ between a flute and a clarinet. At an open end the air meets the room, so the pressure there is room pressure: p = 0. (The air just outside moves with the air inside, so an open end acts as if the tube were slightly longer, by about 0.6 of its radius, an amount called the end correction.) At the clarinet’s reed the opening is nearly shut, so the air there cannot move back and forth, and air that never moves feels no net push: the pressure has no slope there, p_x = 0.

The tube’s operator. p_{tt} = -A\,p, \qquad A = -c^2\,\frac{d^2}{dx^2}, \qquad c^2 = \frac{K}{\rho} \approx (343\ \text{m/s})^2, acting on pressure shapes along a tube of length L. Flute, open at both ends: p(0) = p(L) = 0. Clarinet, closed at the reed (x = 0) and open at the bell: p_x(0) = 0 and p(L) = 0. In words: the same rule for both tubes, with different ends.

DetailsThe two end conditions, and what the model leaves out

The first end condition, a value held at zero, is a Dirichlet condition; the second, a condition on the slope, is called a Neumann condition. The model assumes a cylindrical tube, narrow compared with the sound’s wavelength, small pressure changes and no friction at the walls. A real clarinet’s bore is not exactly cylindrical, and its holes and bell matter; they refine the numbers below, not the picture.

Its spectrum

A mode is a pressure shape X(x) that the operator only rescales, A X = \lambda X; it swings at the frequency f = \sqrt\lambda/2\pi, and the list of all the eigenvalues \lambda is the spectrum. The ends decide which shapes are allowed: solving -c^2X'' = \lambda X with each pair of end conditions (fold below) gives the two spectra.

PreciselyThe computation, step by step uses derivatives

1. Which shapes does the operator only rescale? Writing \lambda = c^2k^2, the mode equation -c^2X'' = \lambda X becomes X'' = -k^2X, solved by the waves X = a\cos kx + b\sin kx, \qquad f = \frac{\sqrt\lambda}{2\pi} = \frac{ck}{2\pi} .

2. The flute: room pressure at both ends. X(0) = 0 forces a = 0, and X(L) = 0 needs \sin kL = 0, so kL = n\pi: one half wave, two half waves, and so on. The eigenvalues are \lambda_n = (n\pi c/L)^2 and the frequencies f_n = n\,c/2L.

3. The clarinet: no pressure slope at the reed, room pressure at the bell. X'(0) = bk = 0 at the reed forces b = 0, leaving X = \cos kx, and X(L) = 0 at the open end needs \cos kL = 0, so kL = (2n - 1)\pi/2: one quarter wave, three quarter waves, and so on. The eigenvalues are \lambda_n = \big((2n-1)\pi c/2L\big)^2 and the frequencies f_n = (2n-1)\,c/4L.

The two spectra.

Flute, open at both ends: modes \sin(n\pi x/L), eigenvalues (n\pi c/L)^2, frequencies f_n = n\,c/2L, that is 1, 2, 3, 4, … times the lowest.

Clarinet, closed at the reed and open at the bell: modes \cos\big((2n-1)\pi x/2L\big), eigenvalues \big((2n-1)\pi c/2L\big)^2, frequencies f_n = (2n-1)\,c/4L, that is 1, 3, 5, 7, … times the lowest.

The lowest frequencies are c/2L and c/4L: a ratio of exactly 2 for the same ideal tube.

Blown: a sustained tube

A blown tube does not ring and fade like a plucked string: the reed or the air jet keeps feeding it, and the air settles into one motion that repeats.

Playing frequency. The clarinet’s reed is a valve: when the pressure in the mouthpiece drops it closes, and when the pressure rises it opens, so the air column drives the reed that feeds it. In normal playing this settles into a periodic oscillation, a self-sustained one (post 3), at a playing frequency close to the tube’s sounding mode from the box above. For the lowest notes that is f_1 = c/4L on the clarinet and c/2L on the flute, with L the tube up to the first open hole: the fingers choose the note by choosing L. On a flute an air jet, blown across the hole at the player’s lips, does the reed’s job.

Waveform: the tube’s modes pick the strong partials. A sound that repeats has partials only at whole-number multiples of its repetition rate (post 1), but it may have all of them. So the tube’s modes are odd multiples only, while the clarinet’s sound has even multiples too: weak in the low register, the notes played without the register key, where no mode of the tube supports them, but not absent. Wolfe reports exactly this: in the low register the clarinet’s sound has strong first and third harmonics and weak second and fourth, and from about E4 up the even harmonics become more important. The stems below, like those under the demo at the top, show the blown sounds on a ruler whose gray lines are whole-number multiples of the note being played; the heights are how strong each partial is. The demo’s sound is made this way, a repeating wave with strong odd and faint even multiples, whose exact strengths are an illustrative choice.

The blown sounds, as multiples of the note being played: the flute (top) has every whole-number multiple; the clarinet (bottom) has strong odd multiples and faint even ones.

The player’s control. The breath sets how loud and how bright the note is. Blowing harder drives the reed further from gentle, nearly linear motion, which adds high harmonics, so the tone gets brighter as well as louder; played softly, the sound is mellower, with few high harmonics (Wolfe). The note lasts as long as the player keeps blowing.

What we hear

Almost an octave deeper. The flute’s lowest mode, \sin(\pi x/L), has room pressure at both ends and swings most in the middle: half a wavelength fits in the tube. The clarinet’s, \cos(\pi x/2L), swings most at the reed and has room pressure at the open end: only a quarter of a wavelength fits. A wave twice as long has half the frequency, so the same ideal tube sounds exactly an octave lower closed at one end. Real flutes and clarinets differ a little in length, the clarinet’s mouthpiece and bell change its effective length, and the open ends act slightly longer than they are, so the real interval comes out a bit less than an octave.

Overblowing: an octave or a twelfth. The flute’s spectrum has every whole-number multiple of its lowest frequency; the clarinet’s has only the odd ones, with nothing in between. So when a clarinetist presses the register key, the tube jumps to its next mode, 3 times its lowest frequency, an octave and a fifth, called a twelfth (a fifth is the ratio 3/2). (The demo keeps the low-register tone color when the register key is on.) A flutist overblows with a faster, narrower airstream, and the flute’s next mode is 2 times its lowest, an octave. That is why clarinet fingerings repeat a twelfth apart and flute fingerings an octave apart.

DetailsHow the register key picks out the 3× mode

The clarinet’s register key opens a small hole near the top of the tube. An open hole lets air in and out, pulling the pressure there toward room pressure, which spoils the fundamental, a mode that needs pressure variation at that point. For one note in the lower part of the low register (the notes played without the key), the hole sits about a third of the way down the sounding tube, right where that note’s 3f_1 mode has no pressure variation anyway, so the open hole does not disturb that mode (Wolfe). For the other notes the hole is off that point, well off for the lowest and highest notes you play with the key (Wolfe), and a second effect helps: the air in a small hole has mass, and the faster it must move back and forth, the harder it is to move. At the fundamental’s lower frequency it moves more easily, so the hole leaks enough to spoil that mode; at the higher frequency of the 3f_1 mode it moves much less, so the hole acts almost as a plug. Either way the tube jumps to the 3f_1 mode.

The four aspects

The clarinet’s four aspects, read off its blown sound.
Aspect Clarinet (and flute)
Pitch the playing frequency, close to the tube’s sounding mode: c/4L for the clarinet’s lowest notes (c/2L for the flute’s), with L chosen by the fingers; the register key jumps to 3\times (the flute overblows to 2\times)
Loudness the breath: blowing harder is louder
Timbre in the low register, strong odd partials and weak even ones, picked by the tube’s modes; more even ones higher up; brighter when played louder (Wolfe)
Duration as long as the player blows

For a sustained instrument the four aspects come from different places than for a ringing one: while the note is held there are no fading times to read; the repeating cycle gives the whole-number multiples, the tube’s modes set which are strong, and the player sets the rest.

What we found

Air in a tube has the operator A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2, the same for both instruments up to the ends. Open at both ends, the flute’s modes sit at every whole-number multiple of c/2L; closed at the reed, the clarinet’s sit at the odd multiples of c/4L, so it sounds almost an octave lower and overblows an octave and a fifth up. Blown, both run one repeating cycle with partials on every line, and the clarinet’s tube makes its odd partials strong: the resonator’s spectrum shapes the periodic tone.
