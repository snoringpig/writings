A harmonica reed is a little brass bar, fixed at one end and free at the other, like a kalimba tine, with the same overtones, in the ideal model, at 6.27 and 17.55 times its fundamental. Pluck it and you hear them. Blow it and they are gone: every frequency in the note lands exactly on the whole-number lines. Where did they go?

Listen first: pluck the reed, then blow it, and compare the stems with the gray whole-number lines.

Same reed; only how you play it differs. (C5 is the C an octave above middle C.)

In one sentence. Blown, the reed swings in one repeating cycle just below its own lowest frequency and chops the air into identical puffs, so the steady sound has partials only at whole-number multiples, and the reed’s off-line overtones are absent from it.

Skip to what we hear if you only want the answer.

The operator, in one line

A reed is a thin brass bar, riveted at one end over a slot and free at the other: the same kind of bar as a kalimba tine, so it has the same equation, derived in post 2. With u(x, t) the reed’s sideways displacement at distance x from the rivet, the beam equation u_{tt} = -\kappa^2u_{xxxx}, in which the brass’s resistance to bending pulls each point back by the fourth derivative of the shape, reads u_{tt} = -Au, and the ends complete the operator: at the rivet the reed can neither move nor tilt, u = u_x = 0; at the free tip nothing bends or pushes it, u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0.

The reed’s operator is the tine’s: u_{tt} = -A\,u, \qquad A = \kappa^2\,\frac{d^4}{dx^4}, \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{EI}{\rho S} = \frac{E h^2}{12\rho}, with u = u_x = 0 at the rivet and u_{xx} = u_{xxx} = 0 at the tip. Here E is brass’s Young’s modulus, \rho its density, S the area of the reed’s cross-section and I its second moment of area; the last form of \kappa^2 is for a rectangular reed of width w and thickness h, where I = wh^3/12 and S = wh. In words: the force per unit mass pulling each point back is the fourth derivative of the shape, times a constant set by the brass and the thickness.

DetailsWhat the model leaves out

The model assumes small motions, a uniform bar clamped at exactly one point, no damping and no air around the reed. Real reeds are not exactly uniform, so their overtones differ somewhat from the ideal values below.

Its spectrum

A mode is a shape the operator only rescales, AX = \lambda X; its frequency is f = \sqrt\lambda/2\pi, and the list of eigenvalues is the spectrum. The operator and the end conditions are the tine’s, so the spectrum is too: the kalimba page writes \lambda = \kappa^2\beta^4 and finds a mode exactly when \cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1, with roots \beta L = 1.8751,\ 4.6941,\ 7.8548, \dots

The reed’s spectrum. Eigenvalues \lambda_n = \kappa^2\beta_n^4, frequencies f_n = \kappa\beta_n^2/2\pi, in the ratios 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : \cdots, off the whole-number lines, with the lowest f_1 = \frac{(1.8751)^2}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{h}{L^2}\sqrt{\frac{E}{12\rho}} . The width w cancels: it decides how much air the reed moves, not its pitch. Low reeds are long, high reeds are short.

On the spectrum diagram, the pitch ruler of post 1, the plucked reed’s stems (top, and in the demo above) are this spectrum: 1\times, then 6.27\times, off the gray lines. The blown reed’s stems (bottom) sit at 1\times, 2\times, 3\times, 4\times, \dots, every one on a line, and none at 6.27\times. Each stem’s height is how strong that frequency is in the sound. Same reed, same operator; only the way it is played differs.

Plucked (top): 1× and 6.27×, off the lines (17.55× is off the right edge). Blown (bottom): every stem on a line, none at 6.27×.

Plucked: a ringing reed

A plucked reed rings like a kalimba tine. It moves as \sum_n a_n e^{-t/\tau_n} X_n \cos(2\pi f_n t + \phi_n), so its sound is a sum of partials at the spectrum’s frequencies f_n, each fading at its own rate. The overtones at 6.27 and 17.55 times the fundamental are off the lines and die fast, as on the kalimba; the demo’s plucked sound is made exactly this way, with illustrative fading times.

Blown: a sustained reed

Playing frequency: just below the reed’s own. Blow, and the reed swings through its slot, opening and closing the air path hundreds of times a second. It becomes a valve, and the sound comes mainly from the chopped airflow: the acoustics writer Colin Pykett notes that very little comes from the reed’s own motion. A steadily blown reed settles into a motion that repeats itself exactly, at a playing frequency f_p slightly below its first mode’s frequency, so the puffs of air repeat at that rate too. That the playing frequency sits below the reed’s own is observed in studies of blown reeds, not derived here: the physicist James Cottingham, reviewing that work, reports that “the sounding frequency of the blown reed is somewhat below the natural vibrating frequency of the reed tongue”, for the kind of reed that sounds in ordinary blowing and drawing on a harmonica. So the reed’s first mode sets the pitch, and changing the reed retunes the note.

Waveform: whole-number multiples. Why the reed locks into one repeating motion is observed, and reproduced by computer models of reeds, but not derived here (fold below). Granting that it does, the rest is mathematics:

  1. The airflow repeats. Let q(t) be the airflow through the slot. In the steady oscillation it repeats every P = 1/f_p seconds: q(t + P) = q(t).
  2. A repeating signal has only whole-number multiples. Any reasonable function of period P is a sum of \cos(2\pi nt/P) and \sin(2\pi nt/P) for n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, its Fourier series, so it contains only the frequencies n f_p (post 1 proves the matching statement for a finite sum of partials).
  3. So the note lands on the lines. The blown sound has frequencies only at whole-number multiples of the playing frequency, with both odd and even multiples strongly represented (Pykett). How strong each one is depends on the shape of a single puff: short, sharp puffs give strong high partials.

The argument uses only that the motion repeats, not which modes make it up. That is why the reed’s own overtone frequencies, 6.27 and 17.55 times f_1, do not appear in a steadily blown note: that is where they went.

DetailsWhy the reed locks into one repeating motion, and how the demo makes its sound

A steady oscillation kept going by a steady push, like this one, is called a self-sustained oscillation, and the repeating cycle it settles into a limit cycle; the bowed string and the clarinet reed are others, and post 3 gives the smallest example. That the blown reed settles into one is observed, and reproduced by nonlinear models of reeds; it is not derived here. A plausible picture, not a derivation: the airflow feeds energy mainly into the first mode, the other modes are not kept going at their own frequencies, and in the repeating cycle they move only as they are pushed, at whole-number multiples of f_p.

The demos’ blown sounds are models. The “Blow the C5 reed” and filing demos play one puff shape, chosen so that the open part of each cycle is short, repeated at the playing frequency: its whole-number multiples weaker overall as they go up. The chromatic harmonica further down passes the same puff train through two broad peaks, a stand-in for resonances of the player’s mouth and the instrument, and adds a little band-passed breath noise. The peaks were set by ear, not measured, so the timbre is approximate. The pitch and the whole-number spacing are not approximations.

Tuning a reed with a file. Since the reed’s first mode sets the pitch, reed makers tune by scraping metal off, and where they scrape decides the direction. The first mode bends most near the rivet and moves most near the tip. Thin the reed near the rivet and you remove stiffness where it counts: the pitch drops. Thin it near the tip and you remove mass where it counts: the pitch rises. It is the mass on a spring of post 2 again, whose frequency grows with stiffness and falls with mass. Try it below.

The Rayleigh quotient explains the direction. For the first mode, \omega_1^2 = \min_{u} \frac{\int_0^L EI\,(u'')^2\,dx}{\int_0^L \rho S\,u^2\,dx}, the minimum taken over shapes, not identically zero, that satisfy the conditions at the rivet; the minimizing shape is the first mode itself. This is \omega^2 = k/m for a mass on a spring again: stiffness on top, weighted by where the reed bends, and mass at the bottom, weighted by where it moves. The steps below make this quantitative.

  1. The quotient. Multiply \rho S\,\omega^2X = (EI\,X'')'' by X and integrate by parts twice; the boundary terms vanish at the rivet and at the free tip. This gives \omega^2 = \int EI(X'')^2 / \int \rho S X^2 for every mode.
  2. Small change. File the reed so that its thickness changes by \delta h(x) < 0. Then I \propto h^3 changes by \delta(EI) = 3EI\,\delta h/h, and S \propto h changes by \delta(\rho S) = \rho S\,\delta h/h, to first order, that is, ignoring terms in (\delta h)^2.
  3. First order. Because the first mode minimizes the quotient, changing the shape slightly changes the quotient only by an amount proportional to the square of the change. So to first order one may keep the old mode shape X and change only the coefficients: \delta(\omega^2) = \frac{\int \frac{\delta h}{h}\,\big(3EI\,(X'')^2 - \omega^2\rho S\,X^2\big)\,dx}{\int \rho S\,X^2\,dx}.
  4. Sign. Near the rivet X \approx 0 and X'' is largest, so the bracket is positive and, with \delta h < 0, \omega drops. Near the tip X'' = 0 and X is largest, so the bracket is negative and \omega rises. In between there is a point where filing changes nothing, to first order.

The demo uses the full quotient with the original mode shape, which is accurate to first order in the depth of filing.

DetailsThe player’s control: breath, speech and bending

The player’s control. The player’s breath sets how hard the air flows: the reed’s frequency hardly varies with the wind pressure, which changes its loudness, and the shape of each puff changes with the pressure too, and so does the tone (Pykett). A free reed is slow to start, because it loses little energy per cycle (Pykett), and the note lasts as long as the breath. Skilled players can bend some notes on a diatonic harmonica downward, by coupling the reeds to the resonances of their own mouth and throat (Cottingham): a case where the player does move the pitch.

The chromatic slide. A chromatic harmonica has two sets of reed chambers, the second tuned a semitone higher. Each hole has a blow reed, sounded by breathing out, and a draw reed, sounded by breathing in, in each set. Pushing the slide moves a perforated bar that closes one set and opens the other, so every note rises a semitone. In solo tuning, the usual layout for a chromatic harmonica, each group of four holes holds C, E, G, C on the blow and D, F, A, B on the draw. Here is a whole one to play.

What we hear

Blown, the reed’s own overtones are absent from the steady sound. Blowing turns the reed into a valve that chops the air into puffs, and the puffs repeat exactly; a sound that repeats has frequencies only at whole-number multiples of its rate, so 6.27× and 17.55× have nowhere to appear. Plucked, the same reed rings with its own spectrum, and you hear the off-line overtones until they die. Same operator, same spectrum: the periodic motion of a steady drive decides where a blown note’s partials sit.

The same mechanism drives the bowed string and the clarinet, whose own frequencies already sit on or near the lines, so there the resonator’s modes shape which partials are strong. A free reed’s own overtones are far off the lines, which lets you hear that the drive, not the reed’s own frequencies, decides where a blown note’s partials sit.

The four aspects

The harmonica reed’s four aspects, for the two ways of playing it.
Aspect Plucked Blown
Pitch the fundamental f_1 the playing frequency, just below f_1; bent down on some notes by the player’s mouth
Loudness how hard the reed is plucked the wind pressure
Timbre off-line overtones at the start, then the fundamental strong odd and even partials, set by the shape of each puff, which changes with the pressure
Duration the fundamental’s fading as long as the breath, after a slow start

What we found

A harmonica reed has the kalimba tine’s operator and spectrum, 1 : 6.27 : 17.55; plucked, that is what you hear. Blown, it swings just below its lowest frequency and chops the air into identical puffs, and a sound that repeats has frequencies only at whole-number multiples, so the steady note has nothing at 6.27× or 17.55×. Periodicity wins: the drive decides where a blown note’s partials sit, and the reed’s lowest mode its pitch.
