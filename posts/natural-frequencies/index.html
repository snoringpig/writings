Question. How do you find an instrument’s natural frequencies, whatever its shape?

Answer. Newton’s law on each small piece of the vibrating object gives an equation u_{tt} = -Au, where A is an operator: a rule that turns one shape into another, built from derivatives and from how the object is held. Its eigenfunctions, the shapes X with AX = \lambda X, are the modes, and mode n swings at f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi}. When A has a complete set of perpendicular eigenfunctions, as it does for every instrument here, every motion is a sum of modes. So the natural frequencies are the spectrum of A, its list of eigenvalues \lambda_n.

The idea with two beads

Start with a matrix instead of an operator. Put two beads on a weightless string, at a third and two thirds of the way along, each of mass m, with the string’s tension T and spacing \ell. Call their heights u_1 and u_2. Each bead is pulled towards its neighbours, the tied ends counting as neighbours at height zero, so \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix}'' = -K \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K = \frac{T}{m\ell}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Each bead’s acceleration depends on the other’s height: the motion is coupled.

Now measure along two other directions: both beads together, q_1 = (u_1 + u_2)/\sqrt2, and the beads opposite, q_2 = (u_1 - u_2)/\sqrt2. Adding and subtracting the two equations gives q_1'' = -\frac{T}{m\ell}\,q_1, \qquad q_2'' = -\frac{3T}{m\ell}\,q_2 : two separate springs. These directions work because K only stretches them, K(1, 1) = \frac{T}{m\ell}(1, 1) and K(1, -1) = \frac{3T}{m\ell}(1, -1): they are the eigenvectors of K, and the stretch factors, its eigenvalues, are the springs’ stiffnesses. A spring of stiffness \lambda swings at \sqrt\lambda/2\pi, so the two natural frequencies are in the ratio 1 : \sqrt3. In its eigenvector coordinates K is diagonal, and that is the whole point: diagonalising turns a coupled motion into independent springs, one per eigenvector.

The string: the same move with infinitely many beads

Let u(x, t) be the string’s height at the point x, 0 \le x \le L, at time t; T its tension and \mu its mass per unit length.

The piece from x to x + dx is pulled along the string at each end with force T, and for a small slope the upward part of each pull is T times the slope u_x. So the net upward force is T u_x(x + dx) - T u_x(x) \approx T u_{xx}\,dx, and it equals mass times acceleration, \mu\,dx\,u_{tt}:1 u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}, \qquad c = \sqrt{T/\mu}. That is u_{tt} = -Au with the operator A = -c^2\,\frac{d^2}{dx^2}, \quad \text{acting on shapes on } [0, L] \text{ that are zero at both ends.} The tied ends are part of the operator, as the number of beads was part of K.

1 N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., Springer, 1998, chapter 2 (the vibrating string). The model assumes a perfectly flexible string, small slopes and no friction.

Its eigenfunctions. AX = \lambda X says X'' = -(\lambda/c^2)X with X(0) = X(L) = 0. The solutions are combinations of \cos and \sin of \sqrt\lambda\,x/c; the end x = 0 removes the cosine, and the end x = L needs \sqrt\lambda\,L/c to be a multiple of \pi. So X_n(x) = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad \lambda_n = c^2\Big(\frac{n\pi}{L}\Big)^2, \qquad f_n = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_n}}{2\pi} = n\,\frac{c}{2L}. These are page 4’s whole numbers, now as eigenvalues: two derivatives bring down (n\pi/L)^2, and the square root returns n.

They decouple the string. Measure the string by how much of each mode it contains, q_n(t) = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L u(x, t)\,X_n(x)\,dx, the continuous version of q_1 and q_2. Integrating by parts twice moves both derivatives from u onto X_n, with no boundary terms because both vanish at the ends: q_n'' = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L c^2 u_{xx}X_n\,dx = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L c^2 u\,X_n''\,dx = -\lambda_n\,q_n . Each q_n is a separate spring, swinging at f_n. Every starting shape is a sum of sines (its Fourier sine series),2 so every motion is a sum of modes: u(x, t) = \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos 2\pi f_n t + b_n\sin 2\pi f_n t\big)\,X_n(x).

PreciselyDecoupling happens exactly in an eigenbasis inner products, orthonormal bases

Write \langle u, w\rangle = \int_0^L u\,w\,dx. (The main text’s sines are orthogonal but have \int X_n^2 = L/2; normalised, \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L), the factor 2/L in q_n disappears.) For shapes u, w vanishing at the ends, two integrations by parts give \langle Au, w\rangle = c^2\!\int u'w' = \langle u, Aw\rangle: A is symmetric, like K. With w = u, \langle Au, u\rangle = c^2\!\int (u')^2 > 0 unless u' is identically zero, which with u(0) = 0 forces u = 0; so A is positive, and AX = \lambda X forces \lambda > 0. So every eigenvalue is positive.

Proposition. Let \psi_1, \psi_2, \dots be an orthonormal basis of square-integrable shapes, each twice differentiable and zero at the ends, and let q_j = \langle u, \psi_j\rangle. Then q_i'' = -\sum_j M_{ij}q_j with M_{ij} = \langle A\psi_j, \psi_i\rangle, and M is diagonal if and only if every \psi_j is an eigenfunction of A.

Proof. For a smooth solution, q_i'' = \langle u_{tt}, \psi_i\rangle = -\langle Au, \psi_i\rangle = -\langle u, A\psi_i\rangle, by symmetry of A; for a pluck, q_i'' = -\langle u, A\psi_i\rangle is the definition of solution in the next fold, with \varphi = \psi_i. Expanding u = \sum_j q_j\psi_j gives q_i'' = -\sum_j q_j\langle\psi_j, A\psi_i\rangle = -\sum_j M_{ij}q_j. If A\psi_j = \kappa_j\psi_j for every j, then M_{ij} = \kappa_j\langle\psi_j, \psi_i\rangle is diagonal. Conversely, A\psi_j = \sum_i M_{ij}\psi_i (expansion in the basis), which is M_{jj}\psi_j when M is diagonal. \square

In a basis of polynomials vanishing at the ends (Gram–Schmidt on x(L - x)x^k), M has nonzero entries far from the diagonal; with L = 1 its top-left entry is 10c^2, just above \lambda_1 = \pi^2c^2 \approx 9.87c^2.

PreciselyWhat “solution” means for a pluck, and why there is exactly one integration by parts, Cauchy–Schwarz

The triangle has a corner, where u_{xx} does not exist, so the equation cannot hold there literally. The calculation of q_n'' above never needed u_{xx} once the derivatives were moved onto X_n, which suggests the definition. A solution is a shape u(t) with \int u^2 < \infty, changing continuously in mean square, such that for every smooth \varphi vanishing at the ends, \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\langle u, \varphi\rangle = -\langle u, A\varphi\rangle, with \langle u(0), \varphi\rangle = \langle u_0, \varphi\rangle and \frac{d}{dt}\langle u, \varphi\rangle(0) = \langle v_0, \varphi\rangle, where v_0 is the starting velocity (zero for a pluck). Smooth solutions satisfy it (integrate by parts); testing against all such \varphi loses nothing, because they are dense in mean square. (L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., AMS, 2010, section 7.2)

Uniqueness. With \varphi = X_n, each q_n solves q_n'' = -\lambda_nq_n with given q_n(0), q_n'(0). That has exactly one solution: the difference r of two has r(0) = r'(0) = 0, and E = (r')^2 + \lambda_nr^2 has E' = 0, so r = 0. Two solutions with the same start therefore have the same coordinates, so they are equal (the sines are a basis).

Existence. The series \sum_n (a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt)X_n, with \omega_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}, a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int u_0X_n and b_n = \frac{2}{L}\int v_0X_n/\omega_n, is a solution when the start has finite energy, \int(u_0')^2 < \infty and \int v_0^2 < \infty. Integration by parts and Bessel’s inequality give \sum n^2a_n^2 < \infty, so Cauchy–Schwarz gives \sum |a_n| + |b_n| < \infty; the series and its term-by-term time derivatives against \varphi then converge uniformly, and the defining identity holds.

Counting. A mass on a spring has one eigenvalue and a two-dimensional space of motions (\cos and \sin); two beads have two eigenvalues and four; the string has two dimensions of motion for each eigenvalue.

The recipe, and when it works

For any instrument:

  1. Newton’s law on a small piece of the vibrating part gives u_{tt} = -Au.
  2. How the object is held gives the boundary conditions, which are part of A.
  3. Solve AX = \lambda X with those conditions: the modes X_n and the eigenvalues \lambda_n.
  4. The natural frequencies are f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi.

This needs A to have a complete set of perpendicular eigenfunctions with positive eigenvalues: the operator version of a diagonalisable matrix. Every instrument here passes, for two reasons.

  • A is symmetric and positive. For shapes u and w that obey the boundary conditions, integration by parts gives \int (Au)\,w = \int u\,(Aw), and \int (Au)\,u > 0 unless u = 0. For the string, \int (Au)\,u = c^2\int (u_x)^2, which is twice the stretching energy divided by \mu. A symmetric matrix always has perpendicular eigenvectors, and positivity makes every \lambda_n > 0, so every frequency is real.
  • The object is finite. On a bounded interval or a bounded skin, A has a compact inverse, and the spectral theorem then gives a complete set of perpendicular eigenfunctions with eigenvalues \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots that grow without bound.3

3 M. Reed and B. Simon, Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics IV: Analysis of Operators, Academic Press, 1978, Theorem XIII.64; L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., AMS, 2010, section 6.5. The fold below states the theorem.

Where it fails. On an infinitely long string, A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 on the whole line has no eigenfunctions at all: \sin kx satisfies AX = c^2k^2X, but it is not a shape of finite energy, because \int \sin^2 kx\,dx is infinite. The spectrum is the whole half-line [0, \infty), with no gaps. Such a string carries waves of every frequency away and has no natural frequencies. Finite size is what makes an instrument’s spectrum a list of separate numbers, and so a set of notes.

PreciselyThe theorem, and the string’s operator checked against it Hilbert spaces

Theorem. Let A be a self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space \mathcal H, bounded below, with compact resolvent: (A - \mu)^{-1} is compact for some \mu not in the spectrum. Then \mathcal H has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of A, the eigenvalues have finite multiplicity, and, listed in increasing order with multiplicity, \lambda_n \to \infty. (M. Reed and B. Simon, Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics IV: Analysis of Operators, Academic Press, 1978, Theorem XIII.64, with the Hilbert–Schmidt theorem for compact self-adjoint operators, vol. I, Functional Analysis, 1980, Theorem VI.16; for second-order operators on bounded domains, L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations, 2nd ed., AMS, 2010, section 6.5.)

The string. Take \mathcal H = L^2(0, L) and A = -c^2\,d^2/dx^2 on D(A) = \{u \in H^2(0, L) : u(0) = u(L) = 0\}. It is symmetric, by two integrations by parts. It is onto: for f \in L^2, the function u = A^{-1}f, u(x) = c^{-2}\int_0^L G(x, y)f(y)\,dy with G(x, y) = x(L - y)/L for x \le y and G(x, y) = y(L - x)/L for x \ge y, lies in D(A) and solves Au = f. A symmetric operator that is onto is self-adjoint, and A^{-1} is compact because its kernel G is square-integrable on the square (a Hilbert–Schmidt operator). A is positive: \langle Au, u\rangle = c^2\int (u')^2 > 0 for u \ne 0 in D(A), since u' = 0 and u(0) = 0 force u = 0. So the theorem applies, with eigenvectors the sines.

The infinite string. On L^2(\mathbb R), the same operator is unitarily equivalent, by the Fourier transform, to multiplication by c^2k^2. A multiplication operator has an eigenvalue only where its multiplier is constant on a set of positive measure, which c^2k^2 never is; so there are no eigenvalues, and the spectrum is the closure of the range, [0, \infty).

The operators of six instruments

What the table shows. The string and the flute have the same kind of operator and the same end conditions, so the same ratios. The clarinet has the same kind of operator with one end condition changed, and that alone turns 1 : 2 : 3 into 1 : 3 : 5. Whole-number ratios need \sqrt{\lambda_n} proportional to whole numbers, and a second derivative on an interval gives exactly that: equally spaced \sqrt{\lambda_n}, page 4’s “one speed for every wavelength”. A fourth-derivative term (the piano wire, the tine) or a two-dimensional domain (the drum) breaks the equal spacing. For a bowed or blown note, the eigenvalues decide which notes can sound and which partials are strong, while the player’s drive holds the partials at exact multiples (page 4).

DerivationThe drum’s eigenvalues: Bessel zeros separation of variables

On a skin of radius r_0, AX = \lambda X reads X_{rr} + X_r/r + X_{\theta\theta}/r^2 = -\kappa^2X with \kappa^2 = \lambda\sigma/T, and X = 0 on the rim. Try X = R(r)\cos m\theta, with m = 0, 1, 2, \dots so that X returns to itself around the drum. Then R'' + R'/r + (\kappa^2 - m^2/r^2)R = 0, Bessel’s equation, whose solution that stays finite at the centre is J_m(\kappa r). The rim needs J_m(\kappa r_0) = 0, so \kappa r_0 = j_{mk}, and f_{mk} = \frac{j_{mk}}{2\pi r_0}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\sigma}} . With j_{01} = 2.405, j_{11} = 3.832, j_{21} = 5.136 and j_{02} = 5.520, the ratios are 1 : 1.593 : 2.136 : 2.295. The zeros of Bessel functions are not equally spaced, so no whole-number pattern appears. Nothing is missed: the products J_m(j_{mk}r/r_0)\cos m\theta and J_m(j_{mk}r/r_0)\sin m\theta already form a complete perpendicular set, because \cos m\theta and \sin m\theta do around the circle, and for each m the functions J_m(j_{mk}r/r_0), k = 1, 2, \dots, do on [0, r_0] with the weight r (Fourier–Bessel series). (N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed., 1998, chapter 3; Wikipedia, Vibrations of a circular membrane.)

DerivationThe tine’s eigenvalues: \cos\beta L\cosh\beta L = -1 derivatives

AX = \lambda X reads X'''' = \beta^4X with \beta^4 = \lambda\rho S/(EI), whose solutions are combinations of \cosh\beta x, \sinh\beta x, \cos\beta x and \sin\beta x. The clamp at x = 0, X = X' = 0, leaves X = \cosh\beta x - \cos\beta x - \gamma(\sinh\beta x - \sin\beta x). The free end at x = L, X'' = X''' = 0, gives two linear conditions on (1, \gamma), which have a solution only when their determinant vanishes; using \cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1 and \cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1, that is \cos\beta L\cosh\beta L = -1. Its roots are \beta L = 1.875, 4.694, 7.855, \dots, and f \propto \sqrt\lambda \propto \beta^2 gives 1 : 6.27 : 17.55. (Fletcher and Rossing, chapter 2.)

Takeaway. An instrument’s natural frequencies are the spectrum of its operator. Newton’s law on a small piece gives u_{tt} = -Au, with how the object is held built into A; the eigenfunctions of A are the modes, and f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi. In eigenfunction coordinates the motion splits into independent springs, one per mode. It works because A is symmetric and positive and the object is finite; an infinitely long string has no natural frequencies at all.

The series in one line

What you hear is p(t) = \sum_n c_n\,e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin(2\pi f_n t + \varphi_n), heard as pitch, loudness, timbre and duration (page 2). Its frequencies are the spectrum of the instrument’s operator, f_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}/2\pi (this page); its sizes are c_n = |H(f_n)| \cdot (\text{route weight of mode } n) \cdot a_n, from the body, the route to the air and the start (page 3); and whether the f_n fall on whole numbers decides whether you can hum it (page 4).
