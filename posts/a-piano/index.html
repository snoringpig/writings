This is an upright piano with its front panels and lid off: 88 keys, three pedals, 229 strings (as modelled here), and the action, the levers that link each key to its hammer and damper. It follows a Yamaha U1, 152 × 122 × 61 cm [1], with standard key widths [14, 15]. Strike a key and the graphs show its string moving and each mode’s strength; the sound is computed live from a stiff-string model. Built with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model.

Drag to turn the piano with two fingers; Follow key, or a double-click (double-tap) on a key, brings you close to its hammer.

Press a key. The hammer pushes the string for a moment, then the string rings in its own modes.

The string's displacement u(x, t) for the last key played, from its top end (left) to the bridge (right), with the hammer and the damper felt below it. Slowed down and enlarged; the sound is real time.

Blue: each partial's amplitude in the sound you hear now (in this model, proportional to n times the mode's amplitude: the push it gives the bridge); grey: just after the strike. The pitch is still the fundamental's even when a higher partial is stronger. Orange ticks: how hard the damper felt pulls on each mode.

To play, hold a key in the 3D view or on the strip below and let go to damp it; on a computer, A to ’ play an octave, Z and X shift it, and Space holds the sustain pedal.

What you are seeing

The hammer starts the modes. A string fixed at both ends moves as a sum of modes, shapes sin(nπx/L) each swinging at its own frequency. The jack, a lever in each key’s action, throws the hammer and slips out from under it, so the hammer hits the string in free flight and falls back at once [19]. That push sets how strongly each mode rings; once the hammer has left the string, it cannot add a frequency the string does not already have (in this linear model). Books commonly give 1/7 to 1/9 of the way along, though on modern pianos many notes are struck nearer the end, at 1/12 to 1/17 [11]; the model uses 1/8, a node (a point that does not move) of modes 8 and 16, which stay silent.

The damper. Releasing a key returns a felt damper to its strings, just below the hammer [7]. To a good approximation, each mode then dies at a rate set by how much it moves under the felt (the orange ticks); the felt covers about 5 cm of string (less on short strings), so every mode is caught. Pianos leave about the top octave and a half undamped, as those short strings die quickly [12, 13]; this model leaves the top 20 keys, F6 to C8.

The three pedals. On an upright the left (soft) pedal moves the hammer rail toward the strings, so the hammers swing a shorter way and land more gently. The middle (practice mute) pedal drops a felt strip between hammers and strings. The right (sustain) pedal lifts every damper [1, 8, 9, 10]. None changes a string’s tension or stiffness, so none changes its frequencies.

The strings. On a typical piano, and in this model, the lowest 10 notes have one string, the next 15 two, the rest three [3]; the bass strings are copper-wound [2] and overstrung, crossing the tenor strings on a bridge of their own [6, 8, 10].

A stiff string. Thick steel wire also resists bending, which puts each partial (each frequency in the note) slightly sharp of a whole-number multiple, f_n = n f_0\sqrt{1 + B n^2} [5], where f₀ is the frequency the string would have without stiffness. B is least mid-keyboard and grows toward both ends [16, 17]. Tuners therefore stretch octaves slightly wider than 2 : 1 [18], which reduces the beating (a slow wobble in loudness) between the lower note’s sharp partials and the note above [17, 20].

Left out: each note is one ideal stiff string with pinned ends, with no coupling between strings and no soundboard resonances. The hammer is a brief push at one point (its felt’s nonlinearity is ignored); the damper felt is a uniform strip. B and the decay times are chosen by hand, not measured, and B here only rises toward the treble. String lengths other than the longest (119 cm) and a parlour grand’s shortest string (5.2 cm) [1, 4] are illustrative.
